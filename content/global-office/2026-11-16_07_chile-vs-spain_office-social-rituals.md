# Chile and Spain Agree That Eating Alone at Your Desk Is a Cry for Help

🇨🇱 Chile · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Eighty percent of employed Chileans say they have friends at work, according to a Randstad survey published in October 2025, which is a respectable figure for a country that also keeps a firm grip on titles and hierarchy. Spain does not bother with surveys on the matter. It simply sends the whole office out for breakfast at eleven and treats a lunch break of two to five o'clock, per The Local, as a feature of the working day rather than a lapse in it. For anyone relocating, the question is not whether you will be social at work. It is whether you will be social on a schedule set by a birthday committee in Santiago or by a tapas bar in Seville.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the team lunch, and stay for the sobremesa, the post-meal conversation that is part of the job | Eat at your desk every day; Expat.com's guide says it can read as antisocial |
| Show up to the occasional after-work drink or company event | Assume attendance is optional forever; Expat.com says occasional appearances help your reputation |
| Remember birthdays and similar occasions, which Randstad found 29% of Chilean workers see celebrated specially | Expect senior managers to turn up casually; Randstad found half see them only at special occasions |
| Keep small talk personal and warm before getting to the point | Rush straight to business with new colleagues; trust (confianza) comes first |
| Treat social events as an extension of the office, as Randstad's regional chief puts it | Behave at the year-end party in a way Ley Karin, the workplace harassment law, would not enjoy |
| Mind hierarchy when befriending seniors | Befriend your manager so visibly that colleagues suspect favouritism |

### In Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the mid-morning coffee and tostada run around 11am | Treat it as a distraction; it is the office's informal meeting room |
| Take a proper lunch, often the menú del día, and linger over it | Bring a sandwich to your desk; The Local says it is generally frowned upon |
| Accept that you may be at your desk until 7 or 8pm after the long lunch | Announce that you are leaving early because the work is finished; presentismo says otherwise |
| Plan August around reduced hours and staff on holiday | Schedule anything important in August and expect a reply |
| Suggest after-work drinks late, because dinner often starts around 9pm | Propose a 6pm happy hour and wait by yourself |
| Be on time yourself, even if colleagues are not | Assume the relaxed schedule applies to the foreigner |

## Chile

Chile's office social life is warmer than its reputation for formality suggests. The Randstad survey, covering more than 4,000 workers in Chile, Argentina and Uruguay and reported by Diario Financiero, found that 80% of employed Chileans have friends at work and 73% believe it is possible to build relationships at their companies. Employers are less sentimental about it: only 43% said their employer actively encourages friendship among colleagues, below Argentina's 56% and Uruguay's 55%. The same survey found that 44% of Chileans say their employer frequently holds social activities such as after-office gatherings, the highest of the three countries, and 29% say birthdays and similar occasions are celebrated in a special way, well above Uruguay and Argentina.

Expat.com's guide to networking in Chile describes sharing lunch with the team as a daily ritual in most workplaces, complete with sobremesa, and notes that drinks and company events matter for team building. It also notes that hierarchy still applies and that friendships across large seniority gaps are handled carefully. Robert Half Chile said much the same in a July 2025 press release: friendship at work is good for engagement, provided leaders set an example and keep boundaries clear enough to prevent favouritism. Randstad's regional chief executive added a bureaucratic wrinkle, suggesting that concern over Ley Karin may be discouraging recreational activities, while arguing that after-office events are an extension of the workplace and should be held to its standards of respect.

The hours behind all this are shrinking. According to Radio Pauta, Chile's statutory working week fell from 44 to 42 hours on 26 April 2026, with 40 hours due in April 2028. The law applies mainly to workers under the labour code and excludes public servants and workers paid on honorarios. Fewer hours should, in theory, leave more time for the social calendar, though the evidence so far suggests that Chileans were managing it anyway. Randstad also found that 51% of Chilean workers say senior managers join company social events mainly on special occasions, such as the year-end party, which is a useful calibration for how much of your boss you should expect to see at the Friday drinks. In practice, the ritual calendar runs on goodwill, not on obligation, and the penalty for skipping it is a slow drift to the outer edge of the team rather than a formal reprimand.

## Spain

Spain organises social life around the clock rather than the calendar. The Local describes groups leaving around 11am for coffee, toast and conversation, sometimes in rotating shifts in public administration, followed by a lunch it calls a long, sacred break, often between 2 and 5pm. Expat.com's guide to work culture in Seville says some companies stagger breaks so that someone always covers the office, and that in August many firms shorten their days, often stopping at 2 or 3pm. Another Local piece on eating habits adds that lunch cannot be hurried, that deals are often sealed over meals, and that the sobremesa can stretch for hours.

None of this means Spaniards work less, a point on which the data is stubborn. The Local, citing European Commission figures, reports that Spanish employees worked 1,658 hours per worker in 2023, against 1,610 in the EU and 1,556 in the eurozone. Citing the OECD, it puts the share working very long hours at 3%, against an OECD average of 10%. The catch is productivity: it cites OECD data putting GDP per hour worked at about $53 in Spain against $61 in the eurozone. Long breaks, in other words, push the day later without making it noticeably shorter. Presentismo, the expectation of staying visible until the boss leaves, is a large part of why.

The social rituals also extend to the evening. Late dinners, late drinks and the habit of sharing dishes keep colleagues together long after the spreadsheet is closed. The Local notes that drinking is social rather than heavy, and that ordering a pint instead of a caña may raise an eyebrow. This is the closest thing the Spanish office has to a dress code. A newcomer who treats these habits as optional extras will find that the real decisions, and the real gossip, tend to happen at the table or the bar rather than in the meeting room, and that the invitation to join quietly stops arriving for those who decline it three times.

## The Reckoning

Compared head to head, Chile organises social life around occasions and Spain organises it around meals. A Chilean team will have its friendships, its birthdays and its year-end party, and managers will appear at the last of those when required. A Spanish team will have its eleven o'clock breakfast and its two o'clock lunch, and managers will appear at both because they are hungry. Hofstede Insights scores Chile at 63 for power distance and Spain at 57, both in the moderately hierarchical range, and both at 86 for uncertainty avoidance, which is to say that each prefers its rituals to arrive on schedule and with the correct paperwork. The practical difference is that Chile's rituals are relationship-driven and event-driven, while Spain's are structural.

For a newcomer, the Spanish arrangement is easier to join and harder to leave. There is little to decide about the eleven o'clock coffee, and no calendar invite required, but there is also the 7pm exit. In Chile, friendships come with the job, but the invitations depend on seniority, the occasion and, if Randstad is right, a lingering anxiety about harassment legislation. Neither country will let you remain a stranger for long, though they differ on whether they will do it with a cake or a croqueta.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (English) — a Brazilian who worked in Chile in roughly 2013 to 2015 found the line between work and personal life thin: colleagues stood close, touched often, and sometimes invited coworkers home. He disliked it enough to advise others against working there. It is one dated, personal account.*

> *Expat.com (Spanish) — a Venezuelan who had lived in Chile for seven years, writing around 2022 to 2023, said integration into the team was slow, and that learning Chilean Spanish and the way requests were phrased took a long time. They also claimed that personal connections matter for getting a good job, which is their opinion rather than a verified fact.*

> *Expat.com — a corporate trainer in Madrid, writing many years ago, found that the 9am start was respected, but that long breakfasts, lunch trips home and frequent smoking breaks made colleagues' days look much less productive than his own. Colleagues were relieved when he proposed a mid-morning pause.*

> *Hacker News — in a thread about Spain's time zone and productivity, one commenter noted that France shares Spain's longitude and its habits of long lunches, coffee breaks and dinner around 8pm, yet has high productivity. The clock, the commenter suggested, is not the whole explanation.*

> *The Local Spain — a roundup of office phrases observes that nobody in a Spanish office suggests leaving early because the work is done, and that colleagues will announce a long lunch and then be unreachable for much of the afternoon.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on the strength of the office social calendar, the answer is a matter of temperament. Chile suits people who want colleagues to become friends and do not mind a year-end party with the boss present. Spain suits people who regard lunch as a legal entitlement and can tolerate leaving the building at eight. Both will punish the desk-bound sandwich.

Practically, accept the invitations for the first six months, learn the local terms for the meal, and keep your own hours visible. In Chile, expect the relationship to be built over lunch and sobremesa. In Spain, expect it to be built over coffee and the realisation that nobody is leaving before the boss. Whichever you choose, the sandwich stays in the bag.
