**Chile Built a Government Program to Make You a Founder. Brazil Built an Org Chart to Make Sure You Never Forget You're Not One.**

**🇨🇱 Chile · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Chile spent the last fifteen years marketing itself as "Chilecon Valley" — a state-funded accelerator, visa fast-tracks for foreign founders, more than 1,700 companies through its doors since 2010. Brazil, meanwhile, has produced more unicorns than the rest of Latin America combined and still runs on an org chart where, as one relocation guide for employers puts it, "the person at the very top will make the important decisions." One country built its brand on sounding like a startup. The other quietly became one while keeping the hierarchy of a bank. Neither description survives first contact with an actual office.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build your *pituto* (personal network) early — most roles are filled through referrals, not job boards | Rely on cold applications and expect call-backs at the same rate as your home country |
| Address senior colleagues by title and surname until invited to use first names | Assume Chile's startup branding means the whole office is on a first-name, hoodie basis |
| Bring complete information into a meeting — the senior person decides, not the room | Expect consensus-building or a vote; present your case and let them rule on it |
| Expect a currently-42-hour legal workweek (on its way to 40 by 2028) to still run long in practice | Panic when your contract says 42 hours and your calendar says 50 |
| Soften disagreement — read between the lines rather than push back directly | Deliver blunt, US-style critical feedback in front of the group |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in small talk and genuine rapport before getting to business | Treat relationship-building as a prelude to skip past — it is the business |
| Use WhatsApp for fast-moving work discussion; it's often faster than email here | Assume a lack of immediate email reply means something is wrong |
| Learn Portuguese greetings and phrases, even basic ones — it's noticed and appreciated | Default to Spanish because "it's close enough" — it isn't, and people notice |
| Share credit loudly and specifically for team contributions | Let someone else's work go unacknowledged, even by omission |
| Keep physical greetings to a handshake at work, even if social settings are warmer | Mistake Brazilian social warmth for an invitation to skip workplace formality entirely |

### Chile: The Startup Nation With a Corporate Hangover

Start-Up Chile, the government's flagship accelerator, is a genuinely unusual policy success: a visa-and-funding pipeline that has pulled in founders from Singapore to San Francisco and kept a meaningful share of them in Santiago afterward. One founder who went through the program in its early cohorts described it as "entrepreneurship with a seatbelt on" — no formal assigned mentors, just a dense peer network of founders teaching each other through meetups and demo nights. It's a genuinely startup-coded culture: self-directed, networked, allergic to hierarchy.

Walk into an established Chilean company, though, and the seatbelt disappears along with most of the organizational chart you'd recognize from a startup. Chilean business culture, per the Cultural Atlas, runs on centralized decision-making, formal titles, exchanged business cards, and conservative dress — a world apart from the accelerator down the street. One American entrepreneur who has hired across both worlds in Santiago put it bluntly: large local firms can be "very unorganized and even chaotic," with managers who "don't know all that much about technology" running teams anyway. Startups targeting the Chilean market pay less (roughly $2,000–3,500 a month) but offer more locally fluent, Spanish-language work; those building for the US market pay closer to $6,000 for top talent and run in English. As of this past April, the legal workweek sits at 42 hours — the second step down from 45 in a phased reduction that reaches 40 by 2028 — but the real constraint on your calendar isn't the law. It's *pituto*: personal connections that open doors, speed up bureaucracy, and determine which jobs you hear about before they're ever posted.

### Brazil: Unicorns on Top of a Very Old Org Chart

Brazil is, by most measures, Latin America's dominant startup ecosystem — São Paulo alone hosts a cluster of fintech and e-commerce unicorns, with Nubank now profitable at a scale few global neobanks have matched. But "startup" in Brazil doesn't mean "flat." A worker describing their experience at a major Brazilian fintech on the professional network Blind called management "very good and ambitious" while noting that most staff work hybrid, visiting an office roughly once a quarter — an arrangement that sounds thoroughly modern, layered onto a national business culture where, per one HR guide for employers, "at the cornerstone of all business transactions in Brazil are individual relationships and trust," and where major strategic calls still flow from the top with limited consultation downward.

That relational culture has real teeth outside the fintech bubble. A Belo Horizonte-based entrepreneur mentoring newer founders noted that Brazil's complex tax and labor code is written for fixed salaries, not equity, so stock-based compensation — the lifeblood of startup hiring everywhere else — sits awkwardly against rules that assume everyone's paid the same way every month. Communication, meanwhile, leans informal and fast: WhatsApp carries more real work than email, Portuguese fluency (not Spanish) earns real goodwill, and jokes that land in North America frequently do not translate, a gap one longtime expat described as a source of ongoing, good-natured embarrassment. Hofstede Insights scores Brazil at roughly 69 on power distance against Chile's 63 — both solidly hierarchical by global standards, which makes the contrast less about *whether* authority concentrates at the top and more about how warmly it's delivered on the way down.

### The Reckoning

The actual surprise isn't that one country is "the startup one" — it's that both countries run a two-track system where the branding and the lived reality point in opposite directions. Chile sells itself internationally as a scrappy, government-subsidized founder's paradise, and then most newcomers' actual employer turns out to be a formally hierarchical, title-conscious firm that would look conservative in Toronto. Brazil, despite a reputation for informality and warmth, has built some of the hemisphere's most valuable startups inside a business culture where decisions still cascade from a single desk at the top, and the law itself resists paying people the way startups elsewhere default to paying them.

What should actually guide your decision is less "which country is more startup" and more "which startup is actually a startup." A Chilean accelerator badge doesn't guarantee a flat structure once you're hired by a company that graduated from it years ago. A Brazilian fintech unicorn doesn't guarantee Silicon Valley equity norms just because it has a foosball table — the tax code alone can quietly rule that out. Ask about decision rights and compensation structure in the interview, not the branding on the website.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Nathanlustig.com (Santiago-based entrepreneur's blog) — A hiring manager who has placed people at both established Chilean firms and startups described the former as sometimes "very unorganized and even chaotic," with technically unqualified managers running engineering teams — the opposite of the stereotype that "corporate" means more professionalized than a scrappy startup.*

> *Blind — A worker at a major Brazilian fintech said leadership was "very good and ambitious," colleagues were talented, and the hybrid setup (an office visit roughly once a quarter) worked well, though they noted work-life balance was only "good enough" and varied noticeably by team.*

> *Expat.com — A Canadian transplant in Brazil admitted she kept unintentionally offending colleagues by chewing gum at her desk — a habit she hadn't realized reads as rude locally — while another contributor confessed to repeatedly flashing the North American "OK" hand gesture out of habit, calling it "always very embarrassed," since the same gesture carries an obscene meaning in Brazil.*

> *Brazilglobal.net — A founder mentoring Belo Horizonte's startup cluster noted that Brazil's tax and labor law, built around fixed monthly salaries, actively fights against equity-based pay, forcing young companies to improvise compensation structures that would be routine paperwork anywhere with startup-friendly legislation.*

Genuinely fetchable Reddit and Quora threads pairing Chile and Brazil's startup-versus-corporate dynamics specifically did not turn up during research for this piece — both platforms' results skewed toward unrelated company-review spam or were not independently accessible — so this section relies on named entrepreneur blogs, Blind, and expat forums instead.

### Conclusion

If you're weighing the two on this axis alone: Chile will let you build something from nothing with real government backing, then quietly hand you a formal, title-based corporate culture the moment you take a job at someone else's company. Brazil will hand you a unicorn-studded tech scene with real global ambition, built on top of a decision-making structure and tax code that are, underneath the WhatsApp messages and the foosball table, pretty traditionally Latin American. Neither country's "startup" label means what it means on a pitch deck in San Francisco. Read the org chart, not the accelerator's press release.
