# Ninety Days Paid, Two Days for Dad: Parenthood at Work in Argentina and Nepal

**🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇳🇵 Nepal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Argentina pays new mothers 100% of their salary for a full 90 days, funded directly by the national social security system, and requires large employers to provide on-site childcare or reimburse it for children under four. Nepal offers new mothers 98 days too, on paper — but a Kathmandu Post investigation found that "despite robust legal provisions, pregnant women still find it difficult to get maternity leave" in practice, and the country's own labor data shows that fewer than 10% of Nepal's workforce is even covered by these formal-sector protections in the first place. Both countries' laws look reasonably similar side by side. What happens when a woman actually asks her employer to honor them is where the two countries stop resembling each other at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your 90-day maternity leave split (45/45 or 30/60 around the birth) with HR well in advance | Assume the split is fixed — Argentine law allows the employee to choose the configuration |
| Check if your employer has 100+ staff — they're legally required to provide on-site childcare or reimbursement for children under four | Assume childcare reimbursement applies universally; it's tied specifically to company size |
| Know that ANSES (social security), not your employer, pays your maternity salary directly | Worry your employer is footing the maternity leave bill directly — it's a state-run benefit |
| Ask about unpaid extension options (3-6 months) if you want more time beyond the paid 90 days | Assume no extension exists beyond the fully paid period — it's available, just unpaid |
| Expect a genuinely warm, family-oriented culture around children in daily public life outside of work | Expect that warmth to automatically translate into fast integration into local parent social circles as a newcomer |

#### 🇳🇵 Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your maternity leave terms in writing before you need them — legal entitlement and practical access are documented to diverge | Assume having a legal right to 98 days of leave guarantees you'll receive it without friction |
| Ask directly whether a lactation room or flexible schedule exists — only 18.9% of workplaces have one, but it meaningfully improves outcomes where present | Assume standard workplace facilities include maternity-specific accommodations |
| Recognize that only the roughly 10% formal-sector workforce has any legislated parental benefits at all | Assume protections you've read about apply if you're working in Nepal's much larger informal sector |
| Expect fathers to receive just 15 days of paid paternity leave, with no broader statutory parental leave concept | Expect a father's leave to meaningfully rebalance childcare responsibility on its own |
| Budget realistically for the fact that working mothers report roughly 54 hours a week of unpaid childcare versus 24 for men | Assume dual-income households split childcare anywhere near evenly by default |

---

Argentina's system is built on a genuine, if imperfect, welfare-state model: maternity pay flows through ANSES rather than the employer, insulating new mothers from the layoff risk that comes when a company itself bears the direct cost of leave, and the mandated childcare provision for larger employers extends support well past the leave period itself, into the years when daycare costs otherwise bite hardest. Hofstede's data marks Argentina as strongly collectivist with tight-knit familial structures valued over individual accomplishment, a cultural backdrop reflected in daily life — porteño culture is frequently described by residents and expats alike as intensely child-friendly, with strangers looking out for children in public and parents commonly waved to the front of lines. The system isn't without gaps: paternity leave is a mere two days, reflecting an assumption, common across much of Latin America, that childcare remains primarily the mother's domain even where the state actively supports her doing it.

Nepal's system reveals a much larger gap between what the law promises and what workers actually receive. The 98 days of maternity leave and 15 days of paternity leave exist in statute, but the Kathmandu Post's reporting on "robust legal provisions" that pregnant women still struggle to access describes a workforce where enforcement, not law, is the binding constraint — and the ILO's country brief on care work in Nepal confirms the structural reason why: with less than a tenth of Nepal's employed population in the formal sector where these laws even apply, the vast majority of working mothers have no legislated protection to invoke in the first place. Hofstede-adjacent research on Nepal describes a high-power-distance, low-individualism culture where hierarchical acceptance runs deep, and where — as one ILO researcher put it plainly — "in a patriarchal society like Nepal, mothers are expected to be solely responsible for raising children," a norm reflected starkly in the data: employed mothers report 54 hours a week of unpaid childcare against fathers' 24.

The reckoning: Argentina's gap is between a genuinely strong legal framework and merely adequate implementation — the state pays, the law is mostly honored, and the main shortfall is a father's leave too short to meaningfully redistribute the caregiving load. Nepal's gap is structural and much larger — a legal framework that reads almost as well as Argentina's on paper collides with a labor market where the vast majority of workers simply aren't covered by it, and where even covered workers report real difficulty accessing what they're formally owed. A parent moving to Argentina for work can reasonably expect the law to function as written; a parent moving to Nepal needs to verify, employer by employer, whether the law functions at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "what is the hardest thing(s) about raising children in Argentina," one respondent pointed to the widening gap between free but strained public education and increasingly expensive private schooling, especially in Buenos Aires, as the defining financial pressure point for working parents rather than the leave policy itself.*

> *Quora (Re: Nepal space) — A thread asking "why is it a cause for concern" that several employers in Nepal deny maternity leave outright drew answers describing informal-sector and even some formal-sector employers treating the legal entitlement as negotiable, with pregnant employees sometimes pressured to resign rather than take the leave they were legally owed.*

> *Buenos Aires Expats Community forum — A thread on "family life" as an expat highlighted the city's affordable and widely available guarderías (daycares) and niñeras (nannies), with several posters noting that childcare costs there ran a fraction of equivalent options back home, even as they cautioned that breaking into local parent social circles took longer than expected due to language and a perceived reserve toward outsiders.*

> *Kathmandu Post — Its 2020 investigation into maternity leave access, still frequently cited in Nepali labor-policy discussions, documented specific cases of pregnant employees being denied leave they were legally entitled to, concluding that the country's legal framework was "robust" on paper but inconsistently enforced across employers of every size.*

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family and weighing these two, the honest filter isn't "what does the law say" — both countries' laws read reasonably well — it's "who actually enforces it." Argentina's state-funded, employer-agnostic maternity pay structure means the law mostly does what it says. Nepal's formal-sector-only coverage, thin enforcement, and documented cases of employers simply denying legally-owed leave mean the law is a starting point for negotiation, not a guarantee. Ask about actual precedent at your specific employer in Nepal before you ask about the statute — the statute alone won't tell you what you need to know.
