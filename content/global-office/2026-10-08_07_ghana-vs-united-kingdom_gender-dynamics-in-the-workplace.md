**26% and 12.8%: Two Very Different Ways of Measuring the Same Gender Gap in Ghana and the UK**

**🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Women hold just 26 percent of decision-making roles across Ghana's government departments, according to the country's own Public Services Commission — a figure its chairman calls plainly inadequate. The UK's gender pay gap, meanwhile, sits at 12.8 percent as of April 2025, according to the Office for National Statistics — down slightly from the year before, and still, after decades of equality legislation, very much open. Ghana is still fighting for seats at the table. The UK has the seats. It's the paycheck at the end of the table that's still short.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect leadership roles to still be heavily male, even in sectors with strong female entry-level representation | Assume visible progress at junior levels means equal representation at the top |
| Set personal boundaries clearly and calmly if a colleague's comment crosses a line — it's generally respected when raised directly | Assume you have to silently tolerate comments you find inappropriate just because "it's cultural" |
| Know that Ghana's 2024 Affirmative Action Act sets real targets — 30% female representation in governance roles by 2026, 50% by 2030 | Assume there's no formal policy push behind the push for better representation |
| Build relationships with the women already in your workplace — they can be a genuine source of practical guidance | Assume older colleagues who tolerate casual sexism also agree with it personally |
| Give credit publicly when a woman's idea is adopted, the way Ghanaian teams often do for strong contributions | Assume having your idea respected means every boundary around you will automatically be respected too |
| Raise concerns with HR or a trusted senior colleague if informal boundary-setting doesn't work | Escalate immediately to a formal complaint before trying a direct, personal conversation first |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly about your organization's published gender pay gap figures — larger UK employers are legally required to report them | Assume pay transparency laws mean the gap has actually closed |
| Name specific instances of being spoken over or having your competence questioned, rather than letting them go unaddressed | Let "it's probably nothing" become your default response to a pattern of small slights |
| Expect formal HR processes and documented pay-gap reporting to be taken seriously on paper | Assume formal process alone guarantees a fair outcome in practice |
| Negotiate pay proactively — research consistently finds women ask for raises less often than men, not that they're less deserving of them | Assume staying quiet about compensation will eventually be noticed and rewarded |
| Push back professionally on being assigned "office housework" (notes, scheduling, tidying) disproportionately | Accept these tasks by default just because no one else volunteers first |
| Use your organization's formal reporting channels for harassment or discrimination — the legal framework genuinely exists | Assume raising a concern will automatically be held against you; document it either way |

Ghana's gender gap is, right now, mostly a representation problem with an increasingly serious policy response attached. The Public Services Commission's own 26 percent figure for women in government decision-making roles comes with real teeth behind the push to change it: the 2024 Affirmative Action Act sets binding targets of 30 percent female representation in governance roles by 2026 and 50 percent by 2030, which Commission Chairman Victor Agyeman called the "most serious attempt to address systemic gender inequality" the public sector has made. What official statistics miss is the texture of daily workplace interaction underneath the numbers. One American intern's account of her first week at a Ghanaian advertising agency, published by the University of Oregon's Ghana program, captures it precisely: colleagues respected her pitch ideas enough to use two of them in a FedEx Ghana campaign, even as the same colleagues made comments about her rings that she read as indirect questions about her relationship status. She initially reasoned that respect for her ideas implied respect for her as a person — and later concluded those two things don't automatically travel together. A separate account from a Black American professional who relocated to Ghana, speaking to theGrio, framed the trade-off from the other direction: "You can be your natural self here without being ridiculed" — a specific, real freedom that coexists, in the same workplaces, with the patriarchal norms both accounts describe.

The UK's gender gap is, by contrast, mostly a compensation and advancement problem dressed in two decades of formal equality law. The ONS's 12.8 percent overall pay gap figure sits alongside a separate finding, reported by HCA Magazine, that almost 90 percent of UK women believe their gender has been a barrier to their career — with 17 percent saying they'd been explicitly passed over for a promotion because of it. The study's list of commonly experienced workplace sexism reads almost like a checklist: 43 percent report being expected to make tea or coffee, 33 percent have had their appearance commented on, and 18 percent report being spoken over or patronized in meetings. Stephanie Liston, chair of Women in Telecoms & Technology, pushed back on a related 2024 pay-gap survey's framing but conceded the underlying mechanism plainly: "I don't think men are as reluctant to ask for a pay rise" as women are — not a legal barrier, but a behavioral one the law can't easily legislate away. Lauren Hine, a UK tech marketing executive, described a more visible version of the same gap from the stage: "I've worked in some places where I would go on stage and all the men would leave the audience."

### The Reckoning

Here's the real contrast: Ghana's problem is largely upstream — getting women into the room at all, which is why its most serious recent intervention is a legal quota with hard deadlines. The UK's problem is largely downstream — women are broadly in the room, in the workforce, often in the meeting, and the gap shows up afterward, in pay, in promotion timing, in who gets interrupted. A quota can meaningfully move Ghana's 26 percent. A quota wouldn't obviously move the UK's 12.8 percent, because the UK's gap isn't primarily about headcount — it's about what happens to people who are already counted. Ghana is scored only at the regional-cluster level in Hofstede's framework — grouped under "West Africa" at a high 77 on power distance — while the UK sits at a much flatter 35, a gap consistent with the UK's more procedural, if still incomplete, approach to workplace hierarchy and inclusion.

The deeper irony is that the UK's problem, being more diffuse, may actually be harder to fix than Ghana's. A missing seat at the table is visible and has a known solution — put more people in seats, by law if necessary. A pay gap sustained by thousands of small, uncoordinated behaviors — who gets asked to take notes, who gets interrupted, who doesn't ask for the raise — doesn't respond to a single legislative deadline nearly as cleanly.

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing these two for a career move, the honest framing isn't "which country treats women better" — it's "which specific failure mode you're better equipped to navigate." Ghana will likely respect your competence quickly and visibly, while asking you to set your own personal boundaries actively rather than relying on an unspoken office norm to protect you. The UK will give you the formal protections and the reporting structures on paper, while asking you to notice — and fight — a thousand smaller, harder-to-document slights that no single law was built to catch.

Ask either country's hiring manager not "do you have a gender pay gap or diversity policy," but "can you name the last woman who was promoted here, and what changed for her afterward." In Ghana, that answer is improving quickly under legal pressure. In the UK, it's been improving slowly for twenty-five years — and 12.8 percent is what "slowly" currently looks like.
