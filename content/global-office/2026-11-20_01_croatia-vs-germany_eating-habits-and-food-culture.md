# Croatia Treats Lunch as a Four-Hour Treaty Negotiation; Germany Gives It Thirty-Three Minutes and a Sandwich

🇭🇷 Croatia · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A German employee takes, on average, 33.4 minutes for lunch, according to a 2024 survey of 1,000 workers by the jobtensor.com platform. A Croatian colleague, in the right season and the right café, may spend longer than that deciding whether the coffee they have not yet had counts as a break. Both countries take food seriously. They merely disagree about whether it belongs inside the working day or is an interruption to it, and anyone relocating between the two should learn which view the office holds before the first awkward Tuesday.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the coffee or food when it is offered; refusing can read as refusing the relationship | Say you are too busy for a coffee and expect the deal to survive |
| Plan for a long, multi-course lunch as the main meal of the day | Treat lunch as a desk-side logistics problem |
| Let a host or senior colleague pay if they insist | Fight over the bill as though it were a customs inspection |
| Raise a glass with "živjeli" when the occasion calls for it | Drink before the toast has been made |
| Treat the café as the real meeting room and arrive without an agenda | Open with item one and expect the small talk to be skipped |
| Invite colleagues for "a coffee" when you want something done | Expect the legal 30-minute break to be the only break |

### In Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Mahlzeit" in the corridor around noon and take a proper break away from your desk | Eat at your desk and expect it to pass without comment |
| Wait until everyone is served and has said "guten Appetit" before eating or toasting | Start on your plate while the others are still waiting |
| Announce the total when tipping ("make it 23") and aim for 5 to 10 percent | Leave coins on the table and walk out |
| Expect the bill to be split by item | Assume an even split, or offer one after you ordered more than everyone else |
| Bring a mug, and cake on your own birthday | Arrive empty-handed on the day the office expects cake |
| Pack a sandwich and plan a hot meal in the evening | Expect a staffed canteen at every employer |

## Croatia

Croatian employment law is more modest than Croatian lunch. According to a Worksuite glossary that cites the Labour Act, workers on shifts of six hours or more are entitled to a paid 30-minute break that counts as working time, and denying it is a misdemeanor. That is the floor. Many workers, the same source says, take 45 to 60 minutes depending on the employer, and Remitly's guide to Croatian meal times places the main meal, ručak, somewhere between 1 and 4 pm, with a mid-morning snack called marenda and a lighter dinner at around 9 pm.

The working day is organized around that structure, and the café is where the organizing is done. A columnist for Total Croatia News, writing about running a real-estate business from a café in Jelsa, describes meeting clients over coffee and strudel, taking them to long wine lunches, and being advised that the surest way to a meeting with the mayor was to buy the mayor's usual morning coffee. The same column cites a research claim that 56 percent of Croatian deals are closed over coffee or something stronger; the figure is unsourced and should be treated as folklore with a decimal point. A piece in Croatia Week on Zagreb café culture adds that an unplanned coffee break reads locally as a sign of good work-life balance, while also noting that the habit can slow urgent work, a conflict summed up by the sentence "I haven't even had my coffee yet."

The newcomer's mistake is to see this as lost time. It is the commute to the decision. According to the Cultural Atlas, declining food or coffee when it is offered can read as declining hospitality, and a host who insists on paying should be allowed to. The sensible approach is to treat every offered espresso as an item on the agenda, and to schedule the real agenda for after the second one.

## Germany

The German lunch break is a short and highly efficient ritual. The jobtensor survey, reported by ingenieur.de, found that 52 percent of respondents take 30 minutes, 10 percent take 45 and 11 percent take a full hour, and that 88 percent consider the time sufficient. Canteens exist at 47 percent of respondents' employers; of those who have one, 36 percent use it always or often and 47 percent occasionally, and 71 percent of users are satisfied with the food. Another 42 percent bring their own food. An older Monster survey reported by The Local in 2012 found that around 30 percent of Germans skipped lunch at work altogether, and half took 15 minutes or worked while eating, which suggests the modern 33 minutes represents a relative extravagance.

Within that economy the rules are exact. The Local reports that tipping runs at 5 to 10 percent, that service is included in menu prices, and that the polite method is to say the total when paying rather than leave change. A reader of that article asked how to do this when the card terminal shows only the exact bill, which is a fair question to which Germany has not yet issued a form. Separately, The Local's readers advise waiting until everyone has been served and wished a good meal before eating or toasting, and note that groups usually split the bill by item.

None of this is unfriendly. It is a system for reaching lunch without improvisation. The German worker uses the break to eat, and the Croatian worker uses the meal to work, which is the difference between a canteen and a terrace.

Small rituals carry much of the load on both sides. Germany's office day has its own noon liturgy: according to deutschland.de, colleagues say "Mahlzeit" to one another in the corridor around midday and expect the canteen to fill at about 12:00, and the Expat.com thread on German meals suggests that workday takeaway costs roughly 8 to 12 euros, which is why many people pack a sandwich and save the restaurant for an occasion. Croatia's rituals are slower and more social. A Zagreb guide for expats, last updated in 2013 and so to be read with caution, says lunch is the most important meal of the day and that central cafés are full of professionals in the middle of the afternoon, a description that remains easy to believe. The shared ground is that in both countries the break is considered part of being a competent colleague, and in neither does the person who ignores it come out ahead.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights, whose scores are recalled here from the published dataset, puts Croatia at roughly 80 for uncertainty avoidance and Germany at 65, which is a surprising pairing for two countries so far apart in their handling of lunch. The difference is not about anxiety. In Germany the rule reduces uncertainty by fixing the time, the length and the bill. In Croatia the relationship does the same job, and the rule is whatever the relationship currently permits.

The practical consequence is that Germany makes food a boundary and Croatia makes it a channel. A German meal ends when the break does, and the conversation about work resumes at the desk. A Croatian meal ends when the matter at hand has been settled, and the conversation about work is the meal. Neither is inefficient by its own standard; each looks irresponsible from the other's chair.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — Posters on a Germany thread say company canteens have largely disappeared and survive mainly at very large employers. Lunch was once the main hot meal; many now pack a sandwich and eat the hot meal in the evening, with workday takeaway running around 8 to 12 euros and restaurants kept for occasions.*

> *The Local Germany, reader comments — Commenters on a piece about foreigners' habits advise waiting until everyone is served and has said "enjoy your meal" before eating or toasting, and warn against assuming an even bill split. One suggests that offering to pay half after you ate more is regarded as kind.*

> *The Local Germany, tipping guide comments — One reader asked how to tip when the card terminal shows only the exact total. A reader in Munich joked sarcastically that one should tip 10 percent even for mediocre, unfriendly service.*

> *Croatia Week — A Zagreb respondent working from home said they miss the networking and the new ideas that came from café breaks with colleagues, while the article notes that the same café habits can make urgent work wait.*

> *Total Croatia News — An expat columnist ran a property business from a café on a main square, met clients over coffee and strudel, and was advised that buying the mayor his usual morning coffee was the way to get an appointment.*

## Conclusion

If your job depends on speed and a clean calendar, Germany will suit you, provided you can eat a sandwich in under 35 minutes and say "Mahlzeit" with conviction. If your job depends on trust, Croatia will reward you, provided your schedule can survive an espresso that turns into a lunch that turns into the afternoon.

In either country, ask at the interview whether there is a canteen, how long people actually take for lunch, and who pays when the team goes out. The answers will tell you how the office really runs, which is more than the careers page ever will. The compromise, if you move between the two, is to bring German punctuality to the table and Croatian patience to the coffee, and to accept that only one of them will be appreciated in either country.
