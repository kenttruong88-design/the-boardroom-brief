# Egypt Has a Boss, Germany Has a Process: A Field Guide to Being Managed

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Germany, 71 percent of employees call their boss "Du," the informal you, according to a 2025 survey of 2,083 workers by HR WORKS. In Egypt, the boss is addressed with a deference that Hofstede Insights quantifies at a power distance score of 80, against Germany's 35. Both countries employ managers. Only one of them appears to enjoy being told that the manager is, technically, just a colleague with a larger calendar.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat your manager's decision as final once it has been communicated, and raise concerns privately beforehand | Contradict a superior in a meeting; public disagreement costs the boss face and you the goodwill |
| Invest in personal rapport: ask about family, share tea, remember the details | Treat colleagues as interchangeable résumés; work and personal life are not tidily separated |
| Learn who actually holds influence, since informal conversations often settle things before the formal approval | Assume the org chart tells you who decides |
| Stay in your manager's good graces; a displeased boss may simply make your life unpleasant instead of firing you | Expect a direct "we have a problem" conversation about missed deadlines |
| Show respect to age and seniority, and defer on seating and introductions | Skip the greeting rounds or jump straight to business; the personal preamble is part of the work |
| Document agreements in writing, because Egyptian labor law is thin on workplace bullying | Count on HR to mediate a personal clash with a senior figure |

### Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Match the form of address you are offered; wait for Du before using it | Assume "Du" means you may criticise your boss's strategy in a corridor |
| Disagree openly and factually in meetings, with data | Cushion every criticism in praise and expect it to be received as kindness |
| Take the initiative on your own tasks; autonomy is the default | Ask permission for decisions that sit within your job description |
| Respect process: approvals, documented decisions and the organigram | Skip steps because they feel slow; even small decisions can travel up the chain |
| Keep work and private life separate, and leave on time | Overshare personal details or expect an after-work beer to be routine |
| Seek feedback in the regular one-to-ones and use them | Wait for praise; silence is frequently the compliment |

## Egypt

The official picture is of a deeply vertical system. According to The Cairo Review of Global Affairs, decision-making in the Egyptian bureaucracy is highly centralised, lower-level employees have little discretion, and matters are referred upward, sometimes all the way to headquarters. The same piece puts the public payroll at roughly seven million people and recalls a Ministry of Planning in which junior staff installed a larger chair for the minister. The OECD, in its public governance review of Egypt, notes that the country is modernising its administration under Egypt Vision 2030 and lists strategic human resources management among its priorities, which is diplomatic language for the fact that the modernising has some distance left to travel.

The private sector is less ceremonial but not more egalitarian. Commisceo Global describes Egyptian management as hierarchical and paternalistic: supervisors are assumed to be more experienced, so consulting juniors is considered unnecessary, and employees do not question decisions in public. The flip side is warmth. Managers advise, listen to personal problems and mediate disputes, and may avoid confronting someone over a missed deadline to protect the relationship. Reporting to such a boss resembles a family arrangement, with both the comfort and the obligations that implies.

Patronage is the shadow side. The Cairo Review describes supervisors who, since 2011, have appointed relatives and friends and treated the privilege as a right, and reports, in the piece's own dated account, that starting government pay of around 1,200 pounds a month would barely cover basic food for a family of five. Yet public jobs stay popular for their security and status, and short hours let many employees take private work on the side. A hierarchy that offers tenure, not money, tends to produce loyalty to the position rather than enthusiasm for the task.

The results are mixed. Gallup figures from 2017, as reported by Egyptian Streets, found that only 12 percent of Egyptian employees were engaged at work, with 65 percent not engaged and 24 percent actively disengaged. Zawya, quoting career coaches and HR directors, links toxic managers to owner-driven decisions at local firms and small businesses, and notes that Egyptian labor law lacks clear provisions on workplace bullying. A boss who cannot be questioned and a rulebook that does not restrain him is a combination best researched before signing. Younger Egyptian developers interviewed by Egyptian Streets complained that extra effort is treated as an expectation, not a reason for reward, while mistakes draw heavy criticism, which suggests the next generation is already renegotiating the family arrangement.

## Germany

The statistics describe a country that has reduced the distance between desk and corner office to roughly the width of a first name. In the HR WORKS Leadership-Monitor, 61 percent of respondents use Du with the first name for their line manager and a further 10 percent use the so-called Munich Du, with the surname. Only 22 percent say Sie with a surname. Authoritarian managers fare worse, with 38 percent of their staff keeping the formal Sie. Informality, in other words, is something the boss earns by not behaving like one. The same survey found that a manager who was previously a colleague is addressed with Du 81 percent of the time, and that managers under 30 get Du 75 percent of the time, against 67 percent for those over 50, so seniority, like promotion, no longer buys a title. Tellingly, Germany's formality is now a style choice rather than a structural fact, which is a luxury of a country in which the structure is fully documented elsewhere.

Autonomy follows. Destatis, reporting the 2019 European Labour Force Survey, found that 21 percent of German employees were completely free to set their start and finish times, and only 50 percent had to follow specified hours, compared with 61 percent across the EU. Researchers at the Technical University of Munich and ISF München found that about two-thirds of roughly 1,000 Germans surveyed wanted companies run more democratically, though they thought it unlikely, and that many firms still manage by figures, which concentrates decisions at the top even in flat structures.

That last finding explains why the flat hierarchy has fine print. Germans are informal in address and rigorous in procedure. Responsibility is allocated precisely, and a decision outside your remit goes to whoever owns it, however friendly the Du. The reward is a boss who leaves you alone to do your job. The cost is a boss who also leaves you alone when you would have liked a mentor.

## The Reckoning

Egypt and Germany divide the labour of authority differently. In Egypt, hierarchy is personal: the boss is a patron, loyalty is currency, and the relationship does the work the process would do elsewhere. In Germany, hierarchy is procedural: the boss is a role, the relationship is polite and slightly distant, and the process does the work the relationship would do elsewhere. The individualism scores in the same Hofstede data, 13 for Egypt against 79 for Germany, tell the story from another angle: one country thinks in families and loyalties, the other in individuals and job descriptions.

For a newcomer, the practical difference is where the risk lies. In Egypt, the risk is political: misread who matters, embarrass the wrong person, and nothing in writing will protect you. In Germany, the risk is procedural: skip a step, take a decision that belonged to someone else, and the consequences arrive in a perfectly courteous email. Neither system is kinder. They are merely unkind in different registers.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Berlin forum) — a non-EU communications professional working beneath her previous level described a 28-year-old German manager who took credit and left deadlines to the team. Replies were less sympathetic than practical: German directness is "different" rather than absent, strong managers are rare everywhere, and employers may prize certificates over track records. Research the team before you accept.*

> *Reddit (r/AskEurope) — a commenter at a Swedish-headquartered multinational reported that German managers micromanage more than their Scandinavian counterparts, who hand over decision-making as long as updates arrive. Italian managers, they added, care more about titles and often eat apart from their teams.*

> *Reddit (r/AskAGerman) — advice for newcomers was blunt: Germans keep work and private life strictly apart, are rarely reachable at weekends, share personal details only with people they know well, and do not treat an after-work beer as a given.*

> *The Local (Germany) — a writer who spent time at Berlin startups found a surprising attachment to hierarchy beneath the hoodies: even a Facebook post meant working up an organigram of supervisors, though feedback was frequent and anonymous complaints were read aloud weekly.*

> *Expat.com (Egypt forum) — a newer call-centre expat found colleagues warm, curious and generous with help and food, but passed on advice to stay in good standing with management, since a disliked employee may be pressured to resign rather than fired. Finger-snapping for attention, they noted, takes adjusting to.*

## Conclusion

Choose Egypt if you thrive on relationships and can read a room; the boss will be present, personal and occasionally impossible to contradict. Choose Germany if you prefer to be left alone with clear rules; the boss will be informal, direct and entirely uninterested in your weekend. In either case, find out how the actual team behaves before you sign, because the national average is only the forecast, and your manager is the weather.

The honest summary is that Egypt asks you to manage your boss and Germany asks you to manage your paperwork, and most people find only one of those tasks restful.
