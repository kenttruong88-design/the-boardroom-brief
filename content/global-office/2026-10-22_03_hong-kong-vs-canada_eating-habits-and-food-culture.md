**Thirty-Minute Lunches, Hundred-Dollar Dinners: What Eating Actually Costs You in Hong Kong and Canada**

**🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Hong Kong, the average basic restaurant meal costs about half what it does in Canada, according to cost-of-living data compiled from Numbeo — and yet the average Hong Kong household earning under roughly HK$13,000 a month still sinks 35 percent of its budget into food, versus 21 percent for the highest earners, per the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department. Canadians, meanwhile, now spend a historic low share of their household budgets on food — 10.2 percent, down from 18.7 percent in 1969, Statistics Canada figures reported by CBC show — while Dalhousie University's Agri-Food Analytics Lab projected Canadian families would pay up to C$801 more for groceries in 2025 alone. Put plainly: one country has made eating out structurally cheap and groceries a quiet luxury; the other has made groceries cheaper as a share of income while prices still climb every year the lab issues a report. Neither fact will show up on the relocation brochure.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat at a cooked-food centre or *dai pai dong* for your first week of lunches — prices are low and the food is often better than mid-range restaurants | Don't assume a "quick dinner out" is cheap; a casual meal for two routinely runs past US$100, and a bad one can hit US$200 |
| Buy perishables in small quantities every day or two | Don't stock up on fresh produce like you would at a North American big-box store — fridges are small and a wasted US$10 avocado is a real complaint among transplants |
| Keep a fallback cheap meal in rotation (many expats default to McDonald's or Subway once or twice a week) | Don't expect a full hour for lunch everywhere — some offices run on 30-minute breaks, and colleagues have genuinely organized around how to eat that fast |
| Use food delivery apps; near-universal coverage means almost anything arrives at your desk or door | Don't be surprised when the grocery bill still outpaces the restaurant bill for a comparable week — groceries can run higher than dining out, item for item |
| Ask local colleagues where they actually eat, not where the guidebook sends you | Don't treat Michelin stars as the baseline experience — most daily eating happens in markets and canteens, not at the 75-plus starred restaurants |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack lunch from home — about 72 percent of Canadians do, per a Dalhousie University survey cited by The Globe and Mail | Don't assume a one-hour, sit-down lunch is standard; roughly 40 percent of Canadians (closer to half in Atlantic Canada) eat at their desks |
| Join or start an office lunch club (soup, salad, or slow-cooker rotations are common and cut weekly cooking to once every few weeks) | Don't skip budgeting for grocery inflation — Dalhousie's Food Price Report has projected multi-hundred-dollar annual increases for an average family in recent years |
| Learn regional specifics before assuming "Canadian food" is one thing — the Prairies, Quebec, the Maritimes, and First Nations traditions differ sharply | Don't call Canadian bacon "Canadian bacon" to an actual Canadian; peameal bacon is the real thing, and the version exported under that name is barely recognized domestically |
| Expect dining out to cost noticeably more than Hong Kong for a comparable casual meal | Don't put ketchup on everything; it's not a default fry condiment the way it is in the US, though it's traditional on Quebec's tourtière at Christmas |
| Bring your own cultural food to the office without hesitation — shared microwaves and diverse lunch rooms are the norm, not the exception | Don't expect a long commercial lunch culture; the "lunch hour" as a leisurely sit-down meal has largely given way to solitary, portable eating |

### Hong Kong: Cheap to Eat Out, Expensive to Eat In

The maths of Hong Kong food is backwards from what most newcomers expect. A basic restaurant meal runs roughly 51 percent cheaper than in Canada, and a mid-range three-course dinner for two is about 12 percent cheaper, according to cost-of-living data aggregated from Numbeo. But groceries don't follow the same pattern: local cheese costs more than double, beef nearly 50 percent more, and bananas 60 percent more than in Canada, even as rice, chicken, and most vegetables run cheaper. One American transplant's account, published by Talesmag's Real Post Report series, put it bluntly: a basket of groceries carried home easily topped US$100, and "nothing worse than having to throw out a US$10 avocado," which is advice about portioning, not poetry. The family in that account ate at McDonald's once or twice a week specifically because it was one of the few affordable options left once rent and groceries were accounted for.

Work culture compresses the eating day further. Hong Kong logs some of the longest average working hours in the world, and Human Resources Online's reporting on a local discussion-forum thread found office workers trading tactics for surviving a 30-minute lunch — cold salads that need no reheating, pre-ordered takeaway, or simply eating fast enough to risk indigestion. Mastercard Economics Institute data from 2023 showed restaurant receipts had recovered to 91 percent of pre-pandemic levels, with lunchtime spending still concentrated around Central, Western, and Wan Chai — the business core — while dinner spend has drifted toward residential districts like Sha Tin and Sai Kung as hybrid work reshuffled when and where people eat.

Socially, food does a lot of the work that small talk does elsewhere. ExpatArrivals quotes a Filipino expat, Elmer, offering the standard and still-correct advice: eat local dishes, go where locals go — meaning markets and cooked-food centres over the 75-plus Michelin-starred restaurants that get the press coverage.

### Canada: Groceries Are Cheaper, Lunch Is Lonelier

Canada has the opposite problem. Food's share of the average household budget has fallen for decades — from 18.7 percent in 1969 to 10.2 percent by 2009, per Statistics Canada figures reported by CBC — even as the lowest-income fifth of households still spend 16.3 percent, and even as Dalhousie University's annual Food Price Report has repeatedly projected several hundred dollars in added grocery costs per family year over year, most recently up to C$801 for 2025. The structural affordability is real; the year-to-year sticker shock is also real, and both show up on the same receipt.

Eating at work in Canada has drifted toward something quieter and more solitary than Hong Kong's communal canteen culture. A Dalhousie University survey reported by The Globe and Mail found 72 percent of Canadians bring lunch from home, two-thirds eat out at least once or twice a week, and nearly 40 percent eat at their desks — rising to roughly half in Atlantic Canada. Cookbook author Allison Day, interviewed in that piece, described the shift from a basic sandwich toward lunches that mimic restaurant flavours, a change she links to rising access to recipes and cooking media. The counter-trend is the office lunch club — informal rotations where one person cooks a soup or salad for a dozen colleagues once every few weeks — which several Canadians interviewed by The Globe and Mail described running in Edmonton and Calgary offices. On Blind, the anonymous professional-network forum, tech workers discussed bringing home-cooked ethnic food to the office specifically because cafeteria options didn't match what they grew up eating, alongside the predictable complaints about what that does to a shared microwave.

### The Reckoning

The paradox: Hong Kong makes it cheap to abdicate cooking and expensive to replace your own groceries, so people eat out constantly and treat home cooking as the occasional indulgence. Canada makes groceries a shrinking share of the average budget and restaurant meals a comparatively costly outing, so people cook more and eat alone at their desks more, treating a shared office lunch as a small act of effort rather than a default. Hofstede Insights scores Hong Kong at 25 on individualism against Canada's 80 — which tracks oddly against the actual eating patterns, since Hong Kong's supposedly collectivist culture produces faster, more solitary desk lunches dictated by punishing hours, while Canada's individualist one has sprouted deliberate, organized lunch clubs as a reaction against desk-eating. Culture doesn't predict lunch behaviour here; office hours and grocery receipts do.

The sharper divergence is timing. Hong Kong's dinner geography is following residential sprawl as hybrid work frees people from eating near the office, per Mastercard's 2023 data, while Canada's lunch hour has been shrinking toward the desk for years, per the Dalhousie/Globe and Mail research, with no comparable working-hours crisis to blame. One country's eating-out culture is being reshaped by where people now live; the other's by what people now expect from a sandwich.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag — Real Post Report — One American transplant to Hong Kong described carrying home a grocery basket that topped US$100, learning the hard way not to overbuy perishables ("nothing worse than having to throw out a US$10 avocado"), and defaulting to McDonald's once or twice a week simply because it was one of the few meals the family could justify on a regular basis.*

> *Hong Kong Discussion Group (via Human Resources Online) — Office workers compared notes on surviving a 30-minute lunch break, with cold salads and pre-ordered takeaway as the common tactics and outright job-quitting floated, half-seriously, as the alternative.*

> *ExpatArrivals — A Filipino expat named Elmer, quoted in the outlet's Hong Kong lifestyle guide, offered the advice that outlasts every listicle: eat local dishes, go where locals go, rather than anchoring your food budget to the Michelin-starred end of the city.*

> *The Globe and Mail — Calgary office worker Sheena Abboud McKenzie described a thirteen-person soup club that meant she only had to cook for the group once every few months, an organized response to the drift toward solitary desk lunches that food writer Allison Day says now defines much of Canadian office life.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — Tech workers on the forum described bringing home-cooked, culturally specific lunches to North American offices precisely because shared cafeterias didn't replicate what they grew up eating, a practical workaround that drew its own share of friction over microwave smells and who was "allowed" to skip the free food.*

### Conclusion

If the deciding factor is simply affordability, Hong Kong wins for anyone planning to eat out often and cook rarely; Canada wins for anyone planning to cook most meals and treat restaurants as an occasional outing. If the deciding factor is time, Hong Kong will cost you lunch hours before it costs you dollars, and Canada will cost you the social ritual of lunch before it costs you much else. Neither country's food culture is better; they're optimized for different failure modes, and you should pick the one whose failure mode you can actually live with five days a week.

Over a drink, the honest version is shorter: in Hong Kong you'll eat better food faster and alone more often than you'd like; in Canada you'll eat your own cooking more often than you expected and have to organize your own company for lunch. Budget your grocery app accordingly.
