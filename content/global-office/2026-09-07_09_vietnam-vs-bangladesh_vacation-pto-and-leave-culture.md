# Twelve Days a Year, If You Can Get Them: Vacation Culture in Vietnam and Bangladesh

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Vietnam, the law grants you twelve paid days off a year and then, with the quiet cruelty of Southeast Asian scheduling, arranges for most of them to be devoured in one nine-day gulp around Tet. In Bangladesh, the law grants you leave calculated by division — one day for every eighteen worked — and then a workplace culture that treats the resulting number as a threat rather than an entitlement. Neither country is short on public holidays. Both are notably short on anyone actually using the leave that isn't a public holiday, which is, if you think about it, the entire point of the exercise.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your leave tier in writing — 12, 14, or 16 days depending on job hazard category — before you sign anything | Assume unused leave rolls over indefinitely; only 3 days carry into next year's first quarter |
| Book Tet travel the day dates are confirmed | Schedule anything important for the week around Tet — the country is on a train home |
| Track your seniority bonus (1 extra day every 5 years) yourself | Trust HR to flag it for you; they often don't |
| Ask whether your employer observes the 9-day informal Tet block or the 5-day statutory minimum | Plan a hard deadline for late January or early February without checking the lunar calendar first |
| Request your two home-country holiday days if you're a foreign hire | Forget they must be requested — they aren't automatic |

#### 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your earned-leave accrual rate (roughly 1 day per 18 worked) explained in writing | Assume unused casual leave carries into next year — by law, it doesn't |
| Ask specifically about Eid festival leave, which is separate from annual leave | Confuse the 11 paid public holidays with your personal leave balance |
| Expect a one-day weekend at many private firms, with Friday as the sole day off | Assume a five-day week is standard outside multinationals |
| Clarify sick-leave documentation rules (a medical certificate is generally required) | Take repeated leave without a paper trail — it invites scrutiny |
| Notice which colleagues actually take their full entitlement, and ask why | Be the first person in the office to test how "discouraged" discouragement really is |

### Vietnam

Vietnam's Labor Code is unambiguous: twelve working days of paid annual leave for standard roles, rising to fourteen or sixteen for hazardous work, plus an extra day for every five years of tenure. Modest by European standards. In practice, the number that matters more is eleven — the count of public holidays, dominated by a Tet break many employers stretch to nine consecutive days by borrowing from weekends. According to the ILO, average weekly working hours in Vietnam still sat near 42 hours as of 2025, among the higher figures in the region. The labor code caps overtime at 200 hours annually and requires employee consent for it — a rule regularly tested and, per multiple HR compliance guides, regularly bent.

What makes Vietnam interesting is the mismatch between formal leave scarcity and reported satisfaction. InterNations' 2024 Expat Insider survey found 85% of expats in Vietnam happy with life there, against a 72% global average. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data shows only 13% of Vietnamese employees reporting significant daily stress, versus a regional average of 25% and a global average of 40%. Twelve statutory days does not, evidently, translate into twelve statutory miseries — Tet appears to be doing structural work a Western-style PTO calendar would need many more line items to replicate.

### Bangladesh

Bangladesh's Labour Act calculates annual leave as a ratio rather than a flat number: one day earned for every eighteen days worked in shops, factories, and commercial establishments (newspaper and tea-plantation workers get different ratios, for reasons rooted in sector-specific labor history). Layered on top are ten days of casual leave, fourteen of sick leave, and eleven paid festival holidays — Eid, principally — that function as the closest thing to a real vacation for most salaried workers. None of this is trivial on paper. The trouble, per reporting by The Business Standard, is that Bangladeshi workplaces frequently treat the exercise of leave as a mark against the employee; one labor leader interviewed described workers being informally "red-marked" for taking their full allotment, with no recourse when requests are simply refused.

The weekly rhythm compounds this. Many private firms still run six-day weeks with Friday as the sole rest day, itself frequently punctuated by work WhatsApp groups that don't observe the calendar. Multinationals — Unilever is the case most often cited — offer markedly more generous packages (25 to 30 annual days, uncapped sick leave, extended parental leave), making "which employer you land" a bigger variable in quality of life than the national leave law itself.

### The Reckoning

Set the two side by side and a counterintuitive pattern emerges: Vietnam offers less statutory annual leave than Bangladesh's earned-leave formula typically yields, yet reports better outcomes on stress and satisfaction. The likely explanation is cultural rather than arithmetic — Hofstede Insights scores Vietnam notably lower on uncertainty avoidance than many neighbors, consistent with a "relaxed attitude" toward formal process that, applied to leave-taking, means fewer social penalties for using what's on the books. Bangladesh's entitlements are written down clearly enough, but a workplace culture — echoing Deloitte's research on "leaveism," taking time off while remaining functionally on call — discourages cashing them in.

The other reversal: Vietnam's headline vacation is one enormous holiday (Tet) wrapped around a thin annual-leave allowance; Bangladesh's is several smaller holidays sitting atop a leave allowance nobody feels safe using. A newcomer optimizing for actual days off should worry less about the number on the offer letter and more about which single week the whole country shuts down — and whether their employer honors it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a poster advising prospective hires in Vietnam to get job scope, tasks, and benefits, leave included, written into a bilingual contract signed by both employee and employer, since verbal assurances from some Vietnamese employers have not held up when disputes arose.*

> *The Business Standard (Dhaka feature, substituting for unavailable InterNations/Blind forum content) — a Bangladeshi media professional who relocated to Germany recalled that at his Dhaka office, leave existed on paper but the company never hired backup staff, so casual and sick days simply expired unused and uncompensated at year's end.*

> *The Business Standard — a labor organizer described employees who take their full leave entitlement being quietly "red-marked" by management, with no practical protection even though the law technically guarantees the days.*

> *The Business Standard — an HR executive at a Bangladeshi manufacturer admitted that even on the single weekly rest day, employees keep answering assignments through WhatsApp groups, meaning the day off exists mostly in a legal rather than a practical sense.*

> *vir.com.vn (Vietnam expat essay) — a long-term foreign resident marking a fifth Tet in the country wrote that what struck them wasn't the days off itself but the ritual built around it — flower markets, family meals, ceremonial poles outside every house — describing it as functioning more like a cultural reset than a Western-style vacation.*

### Conclusion

On raw numbers, Bangladesh's leave formula edges out Vietnam's flat statutory minimum most years. On whether you can actually use what you've earned without professional consequence, Vietnam wins comfortably, helped by a national holiday so large it functions as a built-in permission slip. The honest advice for anyone weighing an offer letter in either country: stop reading the leave-days line item as a number and start reading it as a hypothesis — one you test only by watching, in your first weeks, who actually leaves the office and who merely stops answering, unconvincingly, for a day.
