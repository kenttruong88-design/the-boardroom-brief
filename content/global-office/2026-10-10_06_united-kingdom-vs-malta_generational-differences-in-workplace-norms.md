**The United Kingdom vs. Malta: One Office Argues About Who Should Be in Charge, the Other Just Can't Find Anyone Young Enough to Ask**

**🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 🇲🇹 Malta**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A British office has a generational problem of abundance: too many opinions, across four working generations, about whether loungewear counts as business casual. A Maltese office has a generational problem of scarcity: the under-30 workforce has shrunk by roughly 15,000 people in a decade, so the argument about who should run the place is increasingly academic — there's nobody left to hand it to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that nearly a third of workers report a real generational "culture barrier" at work | Assume generational friction is exaggerated or confined to one office |
| Ask rather than assume what a colleague considers acceptable office attire | Assume loungewear reads the same way to a 24-year-old and a 58-year-old colleague |
| Expect younger staff to ask for pay rises and promotions more assertively and more often | Read a direct ask for more money as entitled rather than simply direct |
| Give specific, regular feedback — nearly a quarter of young workers feel their opinions carry little weight | Assume silence from a junior colleague means they have nothing to say |
| Recognise that over 4 in 10 under-35s sometimes feel like "the odd one out" by age | Dismiss a younger colleague's discomfort as oversensitivity |
| Offer real preparation and onboarding — only 5% of new young workers feel fully ready on day one | Assume confidence equals competence, or the reverse, based on age alone |

#### 🇲🇹 Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to compete hard for any Maltese candidate under 30 — the pool is small and shrinking | Assume young local talent is easy to hire just because the island is small |
| Budget real time for bureaucracy; Malta ranks near the bottom internationally for ease of admin | Assume a small country means small paperwork |
| Keep a lower profile professionally until you understand the room — reputation here travels by word of mouth | Visibly outperform established colleagues before you understand the local dynamics |
| Lean into Malta's genuine strength: it's easy to make friends and build a social life quickly | Assume the social ease extends automatically to career advancement or workplace trust |
| Expect older, experienced staff to hold outsized institutional knowledge, simply because fewer young people are there to absorb it | Assume you can skip paying your dues with someone more senior just because you're capable |
| Factor foreign hires into your realistic headcount plan — several sectors depend on them structurally | Treat hiring from abroad as a stopgap rather than a standing part of the labour market |

The UK's generational divide is a divide of disagreement, not absence. A 2025 survey of 2,000 UK workers found 32% report a generational divide at their own workplace, with younger workers (45% of 16-34s) far more likely to notice it than workers over 55 (22%), according to research reported by Startups Magazine. The disagreements are granular and almost comic in their specificity: 16% of under-35s consider workout gear acceptable office wear versus 10% of over-55s; 56% of under-35s would prefer to work with similarly-aged colleagues, against just 25% of the over-55 group. A separate Kahoot-commissioned survey found 44% of 21-to-25-year-olds believe older colleagues judge them on age rather than ability, with "too passionate" the single most common criticism levelled at them.

Malta's generational divide is a divide of mathematics. The island's 16-to-30 population fell by around 15,000 over the past decade, per Finance Minister Clyde Caruana's own figures cited in Parliament, even as women's workforce participation rose by roughly 13,000 over the same period, cushioning but not reversing the shortfall. The Malta Chamber of Commerce has floated five-year tax exemptions to lure qualified Maltese professionals back from abroad — a brain-drain countermeasure, not a culture initiative — while entire sectors (iGaming, construction, hospitality) now depend structurally on foreign labour simply to function, which is a very different kind of generational tension than arguing about Slack emoji use.

**The Reckoning.** The UK's Hofstede individualism score (around 89) predicts exactly the generational conflict it's getting: a society of people who each feel entitled to their own workplace preferences, loudly, across every age bracket. Malta's own Hofstede profile is less individually documented in isolation, but the practical culture — described by one expat forum poster as rewarding a "serene working life" built on not visibly outshining established colleagues — suggests a more hierarchical, reputation-driven system where generational friction gets absorbed into seniority rather than argued out loud.

The deeper irony: Britain has every generation present and arguing, which is exhausting but is at least evidence of a functioning pipeline. Malta has removed the argument by removing the participants — the youngest cohort simply isn't there in sufficient numbers to contest anything, which solves the noise problem by creating a much larger one. Both countries are, in their own way, trying to buy their way out of the problem: Britain with surveys, consultants, and reverse-mentoring programmes designed to translate between age brackets; Malta with tax exemptions aimed at coaxing its own emigrated graduates home before the gap widens further.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey, via BusinessNow.mt — Malta ranked 43rd of 52 countries overall for expats in the latest Expat Insider results, landing dead last for bureaucracy ("just behind Vietnam," the reporting notes) and struggling on quality of life and climate — but scoring in the top third for ease of making friends, which the survey ties to widespread English and an active social calendar rather than anything about the workplace itself.*

> *Expat.com Malta forum — One poster, going by Fionn, advised that "the key to a serene working life in Malta" is making sure you never visibly appear to outperform established local colleagues — guidance a second poster pushed back on as too broad, saying it "depends so much on the field and job and circumstances" to generalise.*

> *Kahoot!-commissioned UK survey, reported by HR Review — Among 2,000 workers aged 21-25, 44% believed older colleagues judged them on age rather than ability, the most common specific criticism being that they came across as "too passionate" at work; only 5% felt fully prepared on their first day, and 39% had seriously considered quitting within the first year.*

> *Applied survey of 2,000 UK workers, reported by Startups Magazine — The data shows the generational gap is less about values than etiquette: workers over 55 are more likely to think eating lunch at your desk is acceptable (34% vs 27% of under-35s), while under-35s are far more likely to want a same-age line manager (39% vs 16%) — a preference the survey frames as comfort-seeking rather than any judgment about older managers' competence.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a move between these two, the generational question answers itself differently depending on which end of your career you're on. Early-career in Britain means joining a loud, ongoing argument about what work should look like, with real statistical backing for your side whichever side you're on. Early-career in Malta means being a genuinely scarce resource, for better (leverage, demand) and worse (less of a cohort of peers figuring it out alongside you).

Here's what I'd tell a friend: if you want your generational grievances heard and litigated in a survey next year, go to the UK. If you want your generational scarcity to actually set your salary, go to Malta — just don't expect anyone there to admit that's what's happening, and don't expect the bureaucracy to move any faster just because you're the demographic everyone's suddenly desperate to keep.
