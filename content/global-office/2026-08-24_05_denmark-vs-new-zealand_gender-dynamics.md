# Denmark Gave Fathers Eleven Mandatory Weeks Off. New Zealand Still Beat It on the Gender Gap Scoreboard.

## 🇩🇰 Denmark · 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Denmark has legislated eleven non-transferable weeks of parental leave for fathers, subsidized childcare that lets nearly seven in ten women work full time, and a national self-image so thoroughly egalitarian that "gender equality" appears on the tourist board's website. New Zealand has none of that legislative machinery, a masculinity score on Hofstede's index more than three times Denmark's, and yet it ranks fifth in the world on the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report 2025 — five places above where Denmark typically lands. The lesson, before you've even packed, is that generous policy and closed gaps are not the same thing.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your full eleven weeks of earmarked paternity leave without apologizing for it | Assume the leave is penalty-free — informal promotion tracks still quietly favor those who took less |
| Leave the office by 3-4pm for daycare pickup, regardless of your gender | Schedule "urgent" meetings past 4pm expecting full attendance |
| Address the CEO by first name in flat, consensus-driven meetings | Mistake informal seating charts for equal airtime — women hold under 20% of listed-company board seats |
| Quote hard numbers when negotiating a raise | Lead with self-promotion — Jante's Law norms read it as arrogance, and the penalty lands harder on women |
| Expect subsidized childcare to keep both parents working full time | Expect that participation to close the pay gap — Denmark's unexplained gap still runs around 7% |

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names and expect a "mateship" tone even with senior leadership | Read the friendly, egalitarian style as proof of equal representation in the C-suite |
| Take flexible or part-time arrangements openly if you have caregiving duties | Expect flexible work to be a fast lane to promotion — it still clusters in lower pay bands |
| Negotiate pay directly and assertively; it reads as competence, not conceit | Expect quota-driven fixes — NZ relies on voluntary targets, not legislated boardroom quotas |
| Note that public-sector leadership is now majority women | Assume the private sector matches it — women hold under a third of executive roles there |
| Ask which team collapsed the pay gap fastest — public service dropped it from 12.2% to 6.1% since 2018 | Assume that trajectory applies company-wide without checking |

### Denmark

Denmark's welfare architecture is real and generous: since August 2022, each parent has been entitled to eleven weeks of non-transferable parental leave, and according to Statistics Denmark, fathers in 2024 took roughly 37.6 more days of leave than the 2021 cohort while mothers took 38.1 fewer — a genuine structural shift. But the country's own press has started asking harder questions. The Local's "IN NUMBERS: How gender equal is Denmark really?" reporting found women still earn 13.9% less than men, a wider gap than the EU average of 12.7%, with roughly 7 percentage points unexplained by job, sector, or hours. Only around 19% of board members at listed companies are women, and just 26.9% of Danish managers overall are female — well below the 40.7% figure often cited for the United States. The EU-aligned Gender Balance Act, in force since December 2024, is a direct legislative response to that lag, requiring listed companies' executive bodies to move toward parity.

The cultural undertow matters as much as the law. Hofstede Insights scores Denmark at 16 on the masculinity dimension — one of the most "feminine," consensus-oriented cultures measured — which shows up as flat hierarchies, first-name meetings, and near-universal 3pm exits for daycare pickup that apply to fathers as readily as mothers. That informality, though, coexists with a boardroom that the Copenhagen Post has described as "stagnating" on gender progress, and with a private sector where promotion still runs on Jante's Law modesty norms — where self-advocacy, disproportionately penalized in women according to Danish workplace researchers, is treated as poor form rather than professionalism.

### New Zealand

New Zealand arrives with none of Denmark's statutory paternity carve-outs, yet it consistently outperforms Denmark on the WEF's aggregate gender-gap ranking, sitting fifth in the 2025 edition with 82.7% of its gap closed, trailing only Iceland, Finland, Norway, and the UK. Stats NZ and the Ministry for Women report the official gender pay gap fell from 8.2% in June 2024 to 5.2% by June 2025 — the lowest since the series began in 1998 — and the public service has driven its own internal gap from 12.2% in 2018 down to 6.1% in 2024, with women now holding 55.9% of senior management roles (Tiers 1-3) in government.

The private sector tells a different story. Women hold just 28.5% of director positions and 26.4% of executive roles outside government, and The Economist's Glass-Ceiling Index placed New Zealand a comparatively modest 13th among OECD countries in 2024, well behind its WEF standing. Hofstede's masculinity score for New Zealand sits at 58 — more than triple Denmark's — consistent with a workplace culture that rewards direct, assertive negotiation and competitive achievement rather than Danish-style consensus. The mismatch between "friendly, egalitarian" social tone and "underrepresented in the boardroom" outcomes is the country's central contradiction, and it is not resolved by legislation the way Denmark's is attempting to resolve its own.

### The Reckoning

Put the two head to head and the paradox sharpens: Denmark has the policy infrastructure — earmarked paternity leave, subsidized childcare, a new EU-driven boardroom law — and still posts a wider raw pay gap and fewer female executives than New Zealand, which has essentially none of that legislative scaffolding. New Zealand's advantage looks less like policy and more like workplace style: direct negotiation is rewarded rather than read as immodest, and public-sector gains have been unusually fast and deliberate.

Denmark's advantage, meanwhile, is invisible on a spreadsheet but very visible in daily life — a 3pm exit for childcare that draws no raised eyebrows, for men as much as women, and a leave system built to normalize fathers stepping back from work without stigma. If your priority is structural time off and long-run cultural resets to parenting norms, Denmark is playing a longer game. If your priority is who actually gets promoted and paid in the next five years, New Zealand's public sector currently has the better track record, with the private sector still catching up in both countries.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora, "What are the pros and cons of living in Denmark for women?" — paraphrased: A poster who'd relocated from a more assertive corporate culture described struggling with Jante's Law norms around self-promotion; describing your own achievements plainly in a performance review, they said, gets read as arrogance, and colleagues quietly noted it more when a woman did it than when a man did.*

> *Expat.com Denmark forum — paraphrased: A contributor working in Copenhagen said the flat, first-name office culture was real, but in practice meetings still skewed toward male voices getting the last word, echoing local reporting that gender equality in Danish workplaces has stalled rather than progressed in recent years.*

> *Quora, "Denmark's paradox of gender equality" — paraphrased: A commenter said no one warned them that although parental leave is technically shared, the informal expectation among Danish managers is still that mothers take the bulk of it, and that assumption quietly shapes who gets the stretch assignment before the leave even starts.*

> *Quora, "Can you describe what it is like to be a woman in New Zealand?" — paraphrased: A respondent said they expected the country's laid-back, matey reputation to translate into equal opportunity, but found women clustered in lower-paid administrative and caregiving-adjacent roles, with senior leadership in their industry still overwhelmingly male despite the friendly surface culture.*

> *thelocal.dk / howtoliveindenmark.com reader account — paraphrased: An expat praised being able to leave the office at 3pm for daycare pickup without a single comment from managers, noting it was the first employer they'd had — anywhere — where this applied as automatically to men as to women.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on gender dynamics alone, the honest advice is to separate what you are optimizing for. Denmark offers the deeper structural commitment to shared parenting and time off, paired with a quieter, harder-to-name resistance to women's advancement once you are in the building. New Zealand offers a faster-moving, assertiveness-rewarding culture with a public sector that has genuinely closed its internal gap, sitting atop a private sector that has not caught up. Neither country has actually solved the problem; they have just chosen different parts of it to get right first.

The wry truth is that both nations will tell you, sincerely, that they are further along than they are — and the data agrees with each of them, selectively, depending on which chart you're looking at.
