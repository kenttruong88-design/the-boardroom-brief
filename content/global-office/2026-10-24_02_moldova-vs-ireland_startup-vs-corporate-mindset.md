# Pitch Decks in Chișinău, Pension Schemes in Dublin: A Field Guide to Two Very Different Offices

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Moldova's information-technology sector now contributes 6.3% of GDP, up from 1.8% in 2015, and pays its workers roughly three times the national average salary, according to the Moldova Innovation Technology Park's own figures reported by Logos Press. Ireland, meanwhile, hosts more than 1,800 multinational operations employing over 302,000 people, per IDA Ireland's 2024 annual report — a workforce roughly equivalent to the entire population of Chișinău, filing expense reports. One country built a tech economy by letting a few thousand firms skip almost all the usual bureaucracy. The other built one by becoming the bureaucracy of choice for the world's largest companies. Neither is lying to you in the brochure, exactly. Neither is telling you everything, either.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your employer is a Moldova IT Park resident — the flat 7% single tax on turnover only applies inside that regime, and it is the whole reason salaries are competitive | Assume HR exists in any form you'd recognize; many resident firms run thirty or forty people and "HR" is the office manager with a spreadsheet |
| Negotiate your salary in USD or EUR terms, or at least ask how it's indexed — the leu has a history of sliding | Expect a formal pension match, private health insurance, or a written leave policy as standard; ask explicitly, in writing |
| Expect direct, same-day access to the founder or CTO — flat structures are real, not a slogan | Expect that flatness to extend outside the office; government paperwork, utilities, and banking still run on Soviet-administrative time |
| Learn enough Romanian or Russian to run daily errands — the office may run in English, the pharmacy will not | Expect card payments everywhere outside central Chișinău; carry cash |
| Treat the IT Park badge as a credential — ask which resident companies actually export and to whom, not just who has a nice office | Assume "startup" means equity; most resident firms are outsourcing or nearshoring shops, not venture-backed bets |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for Dublin rent before you budget for anything else — it will eat the salary premium you moved for | Expect a quick answer from anywhere; a two-week Slack thread to get sign-off on a minor change is normal, not a red flag |
| Learn your employer's internal "level" system in week one — your title, raise, and even your visa sponsorship priority may hinge on it | Mistake free snacks and a foosball table for a startup culture; most of this is a multinational's EMEA headquarters wearing a hoodie |
| Ask specifically about RSUs, pension matching, and relocation allowance — these are often the real compensation, not the base salary | Count on stock options behaving like Silicon Valley equity; Irish tax treats vesting as income, and private-company shares can be taxed before you can sell them |
| Expect long, formal onboarding — compliance modules, security training, mandatory e-learning — before you do any actual work | Assume a fast promotion path; large multinationals run on annual cycles, not merit sprints |
| Use the extensive expat and professional-network infrastructure — IDA-backed firms are used to processing work permits and relocations at scale | Expect the office to feel small or personal; you are one of tens of thousands on a campus, not an early hire |

Moldova's pitch to the aspiring arrival is unusually specific: come work in the IT Park, pay almost no corporate tax, and get paid nearly three times what everyone around you earns. The Moldova Innovation Technology Park reported more than 2,370 resident companies by the end of 2024, with 533 joining that year alone — the largest annual intake since the park opened — and resident revenue of €785 million, projected to cross €1 billion in 2025, a roughly 30% jump, according to Logos Press's reporting on the park's own figures. IT services exports hit €523 million in 2023, about ten times their 2015 level. The park's administrator has been candid about the mechanism: the tax regime, not patriotism, is what keeps programmers from boarding the next bus to Berlin.

What the pitch undersells is how thin the layer of flat-structure, founder-accessible startup culture actually is. Most resident firms are nearshoring and outsourcing operations serving Romanian, Ukrainian, and Western European clients — forty-one are Romanian-owned, forty-two Ukrainian-owned, a count that has risen sharply since 2021 — rather than venture-funded companies building their own product. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace puts engagement at work in Moldova at 14%, below the global average of 20%, and only 27% of Moldovans say it's currently a good time to find a job locally, against a global figure of 52%. The IT Park is a genuine and well-documented anchor against emigration, per the National Bureau of Statistics' wage data showing average pay near 16,900 lei a month nationally — but it is an anchor for a fairly small, self-selecting slice of the workforce, not evidence that Moldovan offices in general run lean and fast.

Ireland's pitch is the inverse: not "come build something," but "come run something enormous, extremely well, forever." IDA Ireland's 2024 annual report counts 302,566 people employed across its client companies, concentrated in information and communication (111,816 jobs) and manufacturing — essentially the operational, legal, and financial nerve centers of American and European corporations that happen to be headquartered, for tax and talent reasons, on a damp island. Hays Ireland's Tech Talent Explorer, reported by ThinkBusiness, finds Irish tech pay running 17% above the UK and 19% above Germany, with AI demand pushing wages up further still. This is a genuinely excellent place to be a mid-career specialist.

It is a less obviously excellent place to be an entrepreneur. Gallup records engagement among Irish employees at just 9% — below the European average of 12% and well below Moldova's own 14% — even as 49% of Irish workers report "thriving" in life overall, nearly matching Europe's wealthiest cohorts. The gap between those two numbers is the whole Irish corporate bargain: comfortable, well-compensated, and quietly disengaged, because the org chart above you has eleven more rungs and the decision you're waiting on is being made in Austin. Hofstede Insights' data places Ireland near the low end globally on power distance and high on individualism — in theory, a culture of flat, direct, individually accountable work — a profile that describes Irish people well and describes the multinational campuses they mostly work on rather less.

The Reckoning, then, inverts the stereotype both countries sell about themselves. Moldova markets flatness and fast decisions, and inside the IT Park's best firms, genuinely delivers them — but it delivers them within a labor market where, per Gallup, barely a quarter of people feel good about their local job prospects at all, so "flat" exists alongside real scarcity and real fallback risk if a nearshoring contract ends. Ireland markets itself as Europe's tech capital, home to companies whose founders built billion-dollar firms from nothing — and then mostly employs people to administer the forty-thousandth hire at those same companies, with engagement scores that suggest a large number of extremely well-paid people checking their phones in meetings. If you want the startup feeling, you may find more of it, proportionally, in a Chișinău IT Park office than in a Dublin "innovation campus." If you want the startup payout — real equity that turns into real money — you are statistically more likely to get burned chasing it in Ireland, where a Series A options grant is, per one tax-forum exchange among Irish professionals, taxed as income on vesting before anyone knows if the company will ever be worth anything.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a Dublin-based senior developer with 15+ years in the market said most of the city's jobs sit inside large corporations with very few true startups, and laid out the trade-off in hard numbers: contract work around $11,500 a month on rolling six-month terms, against roughly $8,500 a month plus health insurance and a pension for a comparable permanent role — and added that the work itself was often not very challenging either way.*

> *Blind — an Irish-based professional with ten years' experience and a €125k salary described being offered stock options worth 0.1% to 0.5% of a US Series A startup, and asked whether Irish tax would hit them at 52% simply for the options vesting, before any sale was even possible; another commenter's reply was blunt: equity in an unlisted, unsold company "means nothing" and should be valued at zero until an exit actually happens.*

> *Hacker News — a separate, older comment on the same thread described Dublin as having "very little focus on startups," noting that computer-science graduates there are trained toward employment at existing firms rather than toward founding their own.*

> *expat.com (Moldova forum) — a prospective mover planning a business-focused internship found that almost every job listing he could find for an entry-level office role traced back to one employer, the telecom firm Orange, and could not find any published salary ranges for accounting or analyst positions — a reminder that outside the IT Park's self-reporting ecosystem, Moldova's formal job market is thin and poorly documented.*

> *expat.com (Moldova forum) — another poster claimed, without much evidence offered, that well-paid white-collar roles such as bookkeeping or office management are difficult for non-citizens to land, while physical and entry-level work is comparatively easy to find — a claim nobody else in the thread verified, but one worth testing for yourself before assuming your CV alone opens doors.*

### Conclusion

The honest version of this choice is not "startup energy versus corporate stability." It's "a small, genuinely fast-moving tech sector sitting inside a thin and uncertain broader economy" versus "a vast, extremely well-paid administrative machine sitting inside a culture that, by its own workers' admission, is quietly checked out." Moldova will give you more autonomy per square foot of office and a tax regime that makes the math work; Ireland will give you a bigger salary, better documentation, and a much longer queue for anyone's attention, including your own manager's. Choose based on what kind of boredom you can tolerate — Moldova's is the boredom of a shallow local safety net, Ireland's is the boredom of being employee number forty thousand with excellent dental.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: go to Chișinău if you want to feel like you're building something and are prepared to be wrong about how big it gets; go to Dublin if you want to feel like you're part of something enormous and are prepared to never quite meet the people who run it.
