# One Country Will Fire You Only After a Judge Signs Off. The Other Will Just Let Inflation Do It.

🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇦🇷 Argentina

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In the Netherlands, terminating a permanent employee generally requires either the employee's consent, a court ruling, or sign-off from the state employment agency UWV — a process closer to eviction law than to office memos. In Argentina, no court is required to erode your salary; INDEC's own numbers show consumer prices rising 33.2% year-on-year as of May 2026, which will quietly do the job inflation-adjusted employers cannot. One country protects you from your boss. The other protects you, on paper, from your boss, while your payslip gets protected from nothing at all. Welcome to two entirely different definitions of job security.

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with your gemeente and obtain a BSN before your first day — nearly everything else (bank account, payroll, health insurance) is gated behind it | Assume a verbal promise counts; get vakantiegeld, 13th-month bonus, and notice terms written into the contract |
| Negotiate explicitly on the 8% holiday allowance and any 13th-month payment — these are standard, not perks | Treat a "vast contract" (permanent contract) as static; renegotiation is normal and expected |
| Speak up in meetings, including to disagree with your manager — flat hierarchy is the operating norm | Mistake Dutch directness for hostility; blunt feedback is aimed at the work, not you |
| Use your full statutory leave (minimum 20 days, often more) | Book client calls during borrelen (the after-work drinks) without checking — it's genuine networking time |
| Check eligibility for the 30% tax ruling for skilled migrants before signing | Skip reading the fine print on notice periods and severance triggers — dismissal law is generous but procedural |

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask specifically about aguinaldo, the mandatory 13th-month salary paid in two installments | Assume a peso salary quoted today buys the same in September — ask how raises track inflation |
| Have any indemnización por antigüedad (severance) terms explained before signing, even for short contracts | Rely on a single employer as your only income stream without a contingency plan — pluriempleo is common, not shameful |
| Ask when the next paritaria (union wage negotiation round) lands for your sector | Be surprised by short-notice, quarterly wage renegotiations — this is the system working as designed |
| Network aggressively; a large share of roles fill through personal referral before they're posted | Assume "registered" (en blanco) work is a given — informal employment affects a large share of the workforce |
| Weigh monotributo (freelance/self-employed registration) against salaried status early | Expect job tenure to be rewarded the way it is elsewhere — mobility is the norm, not the exception |

### Netherlands

The Dutch have built, almost accidentally, the world's most comfortable cage. According to the OECD Better Life Index, the Netherlands scores 9.3 out of 10 on work-life balance, and only 0.5% of Dutch employees regularly work very long hours, against an OECD average of 13%. Nearly half the workforce works part-time — the highest rate among wealthy economies, with roughly 74% of working women and 27% of working men on reduced hours, per OECD data drawn from Statistics Netherlands (CBS). None of this reads like a labor market optimized for restlessness.

Then there is the dismissal law, which functions less like HR policy and more like tenure. An employer cannot simply let a permanent employee go; barring narrow exceptions, the case has to clear either mutual agreement, a subdistrict court judge, or the UWV, and the employer typically must show there was no reasonable alternative role available, per Dutch employment law summaries from Expatica and legal firms including Adams Multilingual Recruitment. Add a statutory transition payment on the way out, and the incentive structure tilts hard toward staying put — for employer and employee alike, since firing someone is expensive and slow, and switching jobs voluntarily means forfeiting a comfortable status quo. Hofstede Insights scores the Netherlands low on power distance and high on individualism, which sounds contradictory until you see it in practice: flat hierarchies where anyone can challenge a decision, wrapped around a system that otherwise rewards sitting still.

The result, per OECD tenure data, is closer to the roughly decade-long average job tenure typical across Europe — employees who stay not out of sentiment but because the legal and financial architecture makes staying the rational move. It is loyalty as engineering, not virtue.

### Argentina

Argentina inverts the equation entirely: the law is generous, the currency is not, and the currency wins. Argentine severance law is, on paper, one of the most protective in the hemisphere — an employee dismissed without cause is generally entitled to one month's salary for every year of service, calculated on their best recent monthly pay, under the Labor Contract Law (Ley de Contrato de Trabajo), according to employment law summaries from Rivermate and L&E Global. That protection means little when the salary itself is losing a third of its value annually. INDEC recorded 33.2% year-on-year inflation as of May 2026, and Argentine unions negotiate wages accordingly — not in annual raises, but in paritarias, quarterly or even more frequent renegotiations indexed to inflation benchmarks, as the Buenos Aires Herald and Bank for International Settlements analysis both describe.

The practical effect is a labor market where loyalty is a luxury few can afford. INDEC's own figures put informal employment at roughly 44% of the workforce in 2026, and pluriempleo — holding down two or three income streams simultaneously — is common enough that Buenos Aires expat forums casually mention professionals moonlighting in entirely unrelated trades to make the math work. Hofstede Insights scores Argentina at 86 on uncertainty avoidance, among the higher readings globally, which tracks: a culture this exposed to volatility develops elaborate rituals — indexed contracts, dual invoicing, informal side income — precisely because uncertainty is the baseline condition, not the exception.

### The Reckoning

Here is the joke neither country finds funny: the Netherlands gives you a legal fortress and asks you not to leave it, while Argentina gives you a legal fortress and lets inflation quietly dismantle the walls while you're still inside. Dutch job security is real and structural — a judge has to agree you should be fired. Argentine job security is real and structural too — one month's salary per year of service is written into law — but it is security against being fired for cause, not security against your salary becoming worth less each quarter than it was the quarter before. One system protects the relationship. The other protects the exit.

The irony compounds when you look at who actually moves. In the Netherlands, where staying is the path of least resistance, that generous severance and transition-payment regime means employees who do want to leave can negotiate hard, using outside offers as leverage, because Dutch employers know a departure is expensive to replace and a counteroffer is often cheaper than a lawsuit. In Argentina, where switching jobs looks superficially chaotic, workers who do stay in one place are often the ones locked in by informality — unregistered, without the severance protections the law promises, unable to leverage anything because there is nothing on paper to leverage. Mobility in Argentina is frequently not ambition; it is survival dressed as a resume line.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American expat in the Netherlands described being startled early on by how junior her manager seemed and how quickly first-name terms replaced titles, before realizing the flatness was structural, not friendliness.*

> *Blind — A thread on employers in Amsterdam noted that compensation at the top end has crept up as more global tech firms opened local offices, but even now, six-figure-plus packages at the principal level remain the exception rather than the rule.*

> *baexpats.org (Buenos Aires Expats Community) — A poster recounted knowing an emergency-room physician who also drove a cab most days, not as a curiosity but as an ordinary example of how multiple income streams function in the local job market.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter on salary negotiation in the Netherlands warned that leaving money on the table is common among new arrivals who don't know to ask about vakantiegeld and the 13th-month bonus separately from base salary, since Dutch offers are rarely all in one line item.*

> *Quora — A respondent answering a question on Dutch work culture described the first performance review as unexpectedly blunt, with critical feedback delivered flatly and without cushioning, and noted it took months to stop reading it as personal.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two on the promise of "stability," ask which kind you actually want. The Netherlands offers institutional stability: your job is hard to lose, your hours are protected, and the price is a system gently engineered to keep you exactly where you are. Argentina offers legal stability that inflation routinely outruns, which means the real career strategy is mobility — treat every year in one role as a year your peso salary needs renegotiating, because your employer, your union, and INDEC's monthly print all assume you will.

The honest version I'd give a friend over a drink: move to the Netherlands if you want a career that rewards patience, and move to Argentina if you want a career that punishes it — then plan your resume, and your bank account, accordingly.
