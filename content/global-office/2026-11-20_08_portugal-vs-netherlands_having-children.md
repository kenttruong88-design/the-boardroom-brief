# Portugal Gives You Five Months and a Waiting List; the Netherlands Gives You Six Weeks of Paperwork and a Bill

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Statistics Netherlands reports that six years after a first birth, women's work income is about 33 percent lower than before, against about 3 percent for men. That figure is an improvement on the 57 percent drop recorded for births in the late 1990s, which tells you what kind of progress the Dutch have been making: substantial, and still leaving a third of the income on the table. Portugal offers a longer and better-paid leave, and a nursery system that the forums describe in terms of waiting lists. Both countries have worked out how to have babies. Neither has worked out how to do it cheaply.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan leave around the sharing bonus, with 30 days each for 100 percent pay | Take the leave unshared and forget the 80 percent rate |
| Join creche waiting lists early, as public places have waits for ages 3 months to 3 years | Wait until the leave ends to find childcare |
| Use the father's leave, 28 mandatory days at 100 percent plus 7 optional | Assume it is voluntary |
| Compare public creche fees, which are income-based, with private ones | Assume private care is out of reach |
| Check the current rules before planning, since a reform is pending | Count on the 180-day full-pay reform until it passes |
| Confirm the details with Social Security, not a forum post | Trust an unchecked claim that preschool is free from age 4 |

### In the Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for the childcare benefit (kinderopvangtoeslag); it generally requires both parents to work | Assume everyone qualifies |
| Register for daycare early, as waitlists are long | Wait until the birth |
| Use the 9 weeks at 70 percent pay in the child's first year | Assume all 26 weeks are paid |
| Plan for the 17 unpaid weeks, which can be taken until the child is 8 | Plan your income around leave you cannot afford |
| Expect to pay above the hourly cap for childcare if you are among the 63 percent who do | Assume the subsidy covers the full cost |
| Ask how part-time work will affect your career | Assume part-time is free of cost |

## Portugal

Portuguese leave is generous on paper, and the details reward planning. According to the government's Portal Gov.pt, initial parental leave is either 120 or 150 consecutive days: 120 days is paid at 100 percent, while 150 days without sharing is paid at 80 percent. If the parents share, with each taking at least 30 consecutive days, or 15 plus 15, the period extends by 30 days, and 150 days is paid at 100 percent, or 180 days at 83 percent. If the father takes 60 consecutive days, 180 days is paid at 90 percent. Father's leave is 28 mandatory days at 100 percent plus 7 optional.

A reform is on the table but not in force. Euronews reported on 14 October 2026 that a parliamentary working group backed a joint proposal from the PS and PSD parties giving 180 days at 100 percent pay if shared, and 150 days if unshared, which still needs committee review and a final vote and would apply only to leave starting in 2027. The Portuguese outlet DN reported that the Labour Minister called the extension not sustainable, a headline that suggests the final vote is not a formality. Readers should treat the reform as pending and verify before planning around it.

The weak point is childcare. Expat.com posters say public creches have waiting lists for ages 3 months to 3 years, with income-based fees, and estimated private creches at roughly 200 to 300 euros a month with more flexible hours. One poster's claim that preschool is free from age 4 was corrected by a later reply. The figures are anecdotal and no official Portuguese childcare cost was found, so the sensible reading is that private care is cheaper than in northern Europe and that the public place is the prize.

## Netherlands

The Dutch system looks balanced and is less generous than it sounds. According to IamExpat, parental leave is 26 weeks per parent, calculated as 26 times weekly contracted hours; the first nine weeks, taken in the child's first year, are paid at 70 percent of pay, with a 2026 daily cap of 212.97 euros, while the other 17 weeks are unpaid and can be taken until the child is 8. Statistics Netherlands says that in 2025, 92 percent of employed fathers of babies under one took paternity leave and 46 percent took the full six weeks, while 33 percent of men and 35 percent of women took parental leave, and 17 percent of women cut their hours against 11 percent of men.

Childcare is the largest cost. DutchNews, citing CBS, reported that in 2025 the average spending was 10,510 euros per claiming parent, with the benefit covering 68 percent and the average parental contribution at 3,340 euros, and that 63 percent paid above the hourly cap for at least one child. Near-free childcare for working parents is planned from 2029 but has been repeatedly delayed. Hence the CBS Emancipation Monitor's finding of a 33 percent income decline for mothers: a country that makes part-time work normal also makes the career cost of it normal.

The Dutch also have a good deal of what the brochure advertises. An Australian mother writing for DutchReview praised kraamzorg, the home nurse who visits for up to seven days after the birth, and said subsidies make daycare affordable if you want to work. Readers' comments on the article disputed that, with commenters from 2019 and 2023 saying that daycare can take more than half a salary.

One structural detail from the Dutch data is worth emphasizing. The CBS figures on leave show a country in which most fathers now take some time, with 92 percent of employed fathers of babies under one taking paternity leave in 2025, but fewer than half taking the full six weeks, and a minority of either parent taking the longer unpaid leave. That is consistent with the Blind and DutchReview accounts of childcare costs, since a leave that is mostly unpaid is a leave that few can afford. In Portugal the incentives point the other way: the sharing bonus pays parents to divide the leave, and the 28 mandatory days for fathers make participation a default and not a choice. A prospective parent comparing the two should ask less about the headline weeks and more about who is paid for them.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's scores, recalled from the published dataset, put Portugal at about 31 for masculinity and 99 for uncertainty avoidance, and the Netherlands at about 14 and 53. Both are soft on the masculinity scale, meaning both value quality of life, and the difference in uncertainty avoidance shows in the way each system handles risk: Portugal writes rules into the leave schedule, the Netherlands leaves a good deal to the household.

The head-to-head comparison is that Portugal has the longer paid leave and the shorter childcare bill, and the Netherlands has the more established part-time culture and the larger childcare bill. For a mother, the Portuguese system offers more months at home and the Dutch system offers more ways to stay in work, at a cost. For a father, both countries now expect participation, and the Dutch data show most take it.

## Conclusion

Choose Portugal if you want a longer paid leave and a lower childcare bill, and can survive a waiting list. Choose the Netherlands if you want a country that treats part-time work as normal, and can absorb a childcare bill that the state subsidises only in part.

Whichever you choose, apply for childcare before the baby arrives, check the leave rules against your own contract, and ask your employer how it treats staff who return part-time. The legislation will tell you what you are entitled to. The office will tell you what it costs to use it.
