# Shouting Rounds and the Vrijmibo: How Australia and the Netherlands Actually Bond at Work

**🇦🇺 Australia · 🇳🇱 Netherlands**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Australia, buying your colleague a beer is a small act of egalitarianism disguised as a drink order — skip your round in the "shout" and people notice. In the Netherlands, the "vrijmibo" (Friday afternoon drinks) exists specifically to soften a workplace culture built on blunt, unsentimental feedback. Both countries use alcohol and informality as social technology. Both would be mildly offended if you called it that to their faces.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the "shout" — buying a round is a real gesture of equality, not just a drink order | Accept rounds without ever buying one back; it reads as taking without reciprocating |
| Use first names immediately unless told otherwise | Default to titles and surnames — it can read as needlessly distant |
| Take Friday drinks or team lunches seriously as bonding time, even as they decline in popularity | Assume the old "always at the pub" stereotype still applies everywhere — health-conscious alternatives are rising |
| Mirror direct, concise communication mixed with self-deprecating humour | Take blunt banter personally — it's usually a sign of comfort, not hostility |
| Show up to informal team rituals — footy chat, birthday cake, casual catch-ups | Opt out of every social gesture and expect the team to read you as merely private |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend the "vrijmibo" (Friday borrel) when you can — it's where trust actually gets built | Treat it as optional networking fluff you can skip indefinitely |
| Expect blunt, direct feedback during the week, and don't take it personally | Assume directness reflects a personal opinion of you rather than the issue at hand |
| Use the borrel to build the informal trust that makes weekday bluntness tolerable | Try to build all your workplace relationships purely through formal meetings |
| Bring genuine personal conversation to the borrel — it's meant to drop the professional guard | Keep even after-work drinks strictly work-focused |
| Recognise Dutch feedback as a form of respect — they think you can handle it | Interpret criticism as a sign you're not liked or valued |

### Australia: Equality, One Round at a Time

Australian workplace bonding runs on "mateship" — a cultural idiom that blends loyalty, equality, and an insistence on giving everyone, including total strangers, "a fair go." In practice, this shows up as strikingly informal cross-rank socializing: Friday afternoon drinks, team lunches, and birthday celebrations where managers, interns, and staff from different departments mingle without much corporate scaffolding, trading jokes about footy and office politics with equal ease. The "shout" — buying a round for the group — carries specific social weight; according to Turning Point's research on workplace drinking culture, sharing a round functions as an unspoken statement of solidarity, not merely hospitality.

That said, the ritual is visibly fading. Rising alcohol costs, the flexibility of hybrid work, and a more health-conscious younger workforce have pushed some of this bonding toward run clubs and coffee catch-ups instead of the pub. The underlying value — informal, cross-hierarchy connection — persists even as its preferred vehicle changes.

### Netherlands: Bluntness Softened by the Borrel

Dutch workplace bonding solves a different problem: how do you sustain a culture famous for direct, unsentimental feedback without it curdling team relationships? The answer, largely, is the "vrijmibo" — Friday afternoon borrel — a designated social ritual, distinct from the workday itself, where colleagues are expected to set aside professional boundaries and connect personally. According to iamexpat.nl and multiple Dutch workplace guides, the borrel functions almost as maintenance infrastructure: it builds the interpersonal trust that lets Monday-through-Thursday directness land as honest rather than harsh.

Hofstede Insights scores the Netherlands high on both individualism and indulgence — a profile that favours personal autonomy during the working week paired with a genuine cultural permission to enjoy informal social time, rather than treating leisure as something to feel guilty about. Dutch directness itself is explicitly meant to separate the person from the issue; feedback is framed as a sign of respect, an assumption that a colleague can handle honest input, not an attack.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures use a scheduled social ritual to do work that a purely task-focused office culture wouldn't accomplish on its own — but they're solving inverse problems. Australia's mateship rituals exist to reinforce an egalitarianism that's already the cultural default; the shout is a reminder that hierarchy shouldn't matter here. The Dutch borrel exists to counterbalance a directness that could otherwise feel cold; it's a scheduled release valve for warmth in a culture that keeps the workweek itself unsentimental.

The irony is that the Netherlands, often perceived internationally as the more "reserved" of the two, actually institutionalizes emotional repair more explicitly — a named, weekly ritual — while Australia's more visibly warm mateship culture is informally eroding as social habits shift toward health and flexibility, with nothing yet as codified stepping in to replace it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved to Australia for work described being surprised at how quickly first names and blunt feedback appeared in meetings, and said the trick was learning to respond with the same directness rather than reading it as rudeness, since mirroring the tone was what actually built trust with the team.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread about alcohol at work events, a poster who'd worked across a few countries said the expectation to join informal after-work drinks felt heavier in some offices than others, and that opting out consistently — even for good reasons — quietly cost them a bit of social capital over time regardless of the country.*

> *Reddit — An expat who relocated to Amsterdam for a corporate role said the vrijmibo took months to click for them — early on, they treated it as optional and skippable, until a colleague pointed out that most of the informal trust behind the office's blunt Monday-morning feedback culture had been built at exactly those Friday drinks they'd been missing.*

> *Reddit — An American working in Sydney described the "shout" as the single clearest unwritten rule of the office — buying a round felt like a small thing until they noticed a colleague who never reciprocated slowly becoming the person nobody quite trusted, without anyone ever saying why out loud.*

### Conclusion

If communal bonding is what you're weighing, the practical question isn't which culture is friendlier — both are, by most global standards — but which ritual you're more willing to actually show up for. Australia rewards reciprocity in small, informal gestures like the shout, low-effort but easy to get quietly wrong by omission. The Netherlands rewards attendance at a specific, named weekly ritual that does real relational work the rest of the week can't. My honest advice: skip the vrijmibo in Amsterdam or the shout in Sydney more than once or twice, and you won't get a formal warning — you'll just notice, a few months in, that you're slightly on the outside of a trust you never realized was being built without you.
