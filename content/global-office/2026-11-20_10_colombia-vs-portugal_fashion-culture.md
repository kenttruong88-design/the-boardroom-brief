# Colombia Judges You by Your Shoes Before You Speak; Portugal Judges You by Whether You Overdressed

🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

One poster on an Expat.com business-etiquette thread said that in Colombia the shoes are the first thing people notice, and that looking disheveled is a form of disrespect. In Portugal, whose textile industry employs about 127,000 people and accounts for about 17 percent of manufacturing jobs, according to the investment agency AICEP, clothing is made with great skill and worn with great restraint. A professional moving between the two should pack accordingly: polished and pressed for Bogotá, smart and unfussy for Lisbon, and a general understanding that in neither city is the sweatshirt a neutral choice.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress a notch up, in pressed clothes and leather shoes | Turn up in gym wear or sweatpants |
| Wear a suit and polished leather shoes for formal meetings | Wear sneakers to a meeting |
| Adapt to the region, with a jacket in Bogotá and a pressed guayabera on the coast | Wear the coastal outfit to the capital |
| Expect jeans to be acceptable at a tech firm if they are pressed and paired with leather shoes | Assume casual Fridays are universal |
| Dress neatly as a foreigner, since people judge by appearance at first | Wear shorts or flip-flops in town unless you want to be read as a tourist |
| Women should expect higher standards, with grooming and polish | Choose a revealing neckline for the office |

### In Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to smart casual, with neat grooming | Dress as though for a nightclub |
| Wear a full suit in banking and for first meetings | Assume creative-sector relaxation applies to a first meeting |
| Cover tattoos in customer-facing roles | Show them to a client on day one |
| Keep jewelry minimal | Treat the office as a showcase |
| Dress well but not formally for dinner and social events | Over-dress for the evening |
| Treat unflashy dressing as a standard, not an absence of one | Assume a plain outfit means low expectations |

## Colombia

Colombian dress is organized around the idea that appearance is the first piece of information you offer. A thread on Expat.com about unusual cultural habits noted that business workers and students in Medellín look strikingly uniform and neat, that shorts and flip-flops are frowned upon there but acceptable on the coast, and that dressing neatly helps a foreigner avoid being judged. One poster said Colombians dress well even on modest budgets. TalesMag's Bogotá post-report answers, written by embassy-community expats, say that Bogotanos dress up more than Americans do, even casually, with women in heels, boots, makeup and jewelry and sweats and sneakers rare, and that shorts and flip-flops mark you as a tourist. A cool climate means sweaters and jackets all year, and some offices allow casual Fridays while others discourage them.

The business-etiquette thread gives a sharper account: formal meetings call for a suit and polished leather shoes, the coast accepts a pressed guayabera, and women's standards are stricter. This is a single poster's account and not a code, but it is consistent with the rest.

The industry behind the habit is sizeable. Valora Analitik reports that the Colombian fashion sector billed about 33.3 trillion pesos in 2024, up 3.72 percent on 2023, with prices up 2.93 percent, so that part of the growth is inflation, and that the trade body Inexmoda projects roughly 8 percent growth for 2025, which is a projection and not a result. The sensible conclusion is that Colombians spend on appearance because it works, and that appearance is read as a signal of seriousness before anyone has said anything about their qualifications.

## Portugal

Portugal manufactures its clothes seriously and wears them modestly. AICEP reports that textiles and clothing employed about 127,000 people, or 17 percent of manufacturing, in 2021, with exports of about 6 billion euros, 8 percent of the total, in 2022, and turnover of about 8 billion euros in 2021. An RTP headline reports that exports fell 0.8 percent to 5.499 billion euros in 2025; that figure comes from the headline only. The country dresses the world and has declined to turn it into a personality.

In the office, Expat.com's guide to Lisbon and Expatica describe a baseline of smart casual, conservative in tone, with tattoos covered in customer-facing jobs, minimal jewelry and impeccable grooming. Banking expects a full suit, creative sectors are more relaxed but dress up for first meetings, and a Friday afternoon off is common. TalesMag's Lisbon answers describe the city as well dressed, with offices formal or business casual, suits in business and government, slightly less formal than Washington, and casual dress outside work that is still put-together, with few sneakers or sweatpants in public.

The Portuguese standard is thus not about display. It is about not being noticed for the wrong reasons. A thin Expat.com thread on following fashion in Portugal produced one useful remark, from an expat who stopped wearing much makeup and felt under-dressed after moving there, which suggests the standard is high even where it is quiet.

There are also practical differences in how the two countries buy and wear their clothes. Colombian fashion is local and commercial: the Farfetch style guide to Bogotá, which is promotional and written by a single photographer, describes eclectic street style, local artisanal labels and designers such as Johanna Ortiz and Silvia Tcherassi, and shopping in the Chicó and Rosales districts. Portugal's strength is in manufacturing, and its consumers appear to treat clothing as a matter of quality and fit rather than display. The Lisbon guide's note that a Friday afternoon off is common is a useful reminder that dress codes are tied to the working week: a country with a shorter Friday does not require the same performance on every day of it.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's scores, recalled from the published dataset, give Colombia a masculinity score of about 64 and Portugal about 31, with individualism at about 13 and 27. Colombia is the more assertive and more collectivist, and its dress is a public performance of belonging and effort. Portugal is the softer and more risk-averse, with an uncertainty-avoidance score of about 99 against Colombia's 80, and its dress is a way of avoiding a mistake.

The practical difference is what each country rewards. Colombia rewards the person who looks the part, and punishes the one who underestimates the occasion. Portugal rewards the person who looks appropriate, and punishes the one who over-performs it. A Colombian in Lisbon may be told they look wonderful and a little formal. A Portuguese professional in Bogotá may be told nothing and judged anyway.

For the newcomer, the cost question matters as well. Valora Analitik's reading of Colombian fashion sales, with growth partly explained by inflation, is a reminder that a wardrobe built for Bogotá is an ongoing outlay and not a one-off purchase, while Portugal's smaller, quieter standard makes it easier to build a small set of good pieces and rotate them. Neither country expects a different outfit every day. Both notice when the same one has seen better years, and both are more forgiving of a plain outfit that is well kept than of a showy one that is not.

## Conclusion

Move to Colombia if you enjoy dressing for the occasion and do not mind that the occasion is every day. Move to Portugal if you prefer a quieter standard, in which a good jacket does the work and nobody comments on it.

In both cases, pack for the first meeting, not the tenth. Buy good shoes before you go to Bogotá and a plain, well-cut jacket before you go to Lisbon, and observe what your colleagues wear in the first week before deciding what to leave at home. The wardrobe will not get you the job, but it can lose it before the conversation starts.
