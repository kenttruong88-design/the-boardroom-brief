# The Republic Counts Its Hours, the Emirates Count Its Deadlines

🇫🇷 France · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

France defines a full-time week as 35 hours and treats every hour beyond it as an exception requiring justification, premium pay or a nap in lieu. The United Arab Emirates sets its statutory ceiling at 48 hours, and according to the International Labour Organization, as reported by Business Insider, its workers average 50.9, which puts them second in the world for weekly hours. The gap between the legal ideal in one country and the statistical reality in the other is about fifteen hours a week. Anyone weighing an offer in either place should treat that gap as part of the package, and probably the larger part.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your contract counts hours weekly or on a 1,607-hour annual basis | Assume that "35 hours" means you will leave at 5 pm every day |
| Ask for overtime to be logged and shown on the pay slip, where the law requires it | Work unrecorded late evenings and expect them to be paid retroactively |
| Take your lunch hour with colleagues, in the building or out of it | Eat a sandwich alone at your desk and expect no comment |
| Read your company's right-to-disconnect policy and use it | Reply to a manager's 10 pm email in 10 minutes and set a precedent |
| Book summer holiday early, as colleagues will | Schedule an important meeting in the middle of August |
| Use time off in lieu where it is offered instead of cash | Ignore accrued rest entitlements until they expire |

### In the United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the contract for working hours and the overtime rate before signing | Accept a vague "flexible hours" clause as a substitute for a number |
| Know that overtime is capped at 2 extra hours a day and paid at the basic rate plus 25% | Assume that unpaid late nights are legally standard because they are common |
| Expect two fewer working hours a day during Ramadan | Schedule heavy deadlines in the final week of Ramadan without checking |
| Claim a substitute rest day or 50% extra pay for off-day work | Treat an off-day call as free of charge |
| Agree your remote-work hours in writing, since the employer must set them | Leave "available" undefined and discover it means always |
| Ask colleagues about real finishing times, not the policy | Judge the office by the 8-hour day in the handbook |

## France

The mechanics are published in some detail by Service-Public.fr, the French government's information service. A full-time employee works 35 hours a week, or 1,607 hours a year, and overtime is hours worked at the employer's request beyond that, counted by calendar week. Absent a collective agreement, annual overtime is capped at 220 hours. Without an agreement the premiums are 25% for hours 36 to 43 and 50% from the 44th, and the employer may replace pay with equivalent rest, so that an hour at a 50% premium becomes an hour and a half off. Above the cap, rest becomes compulsory: 100% of the excess hours in companies with more than 20 staff. The state, in short, regards your evening as an asset on its balance sheet.

The legal framework is only half the story, and The Local France, in its guide to working in the country, is candid about the other half. French office workers tend to start later and finish later than English-speaking colleagues, and a recruiter quoted there says clients still phone her at 7:30 or 8:30 pm. An anonymous French executive says many staff stay late so as not to look as though they are leaving early. The 35-hour week is a legal unit of account, not a curfew. What the law adds is a remedy: since the 2017 reform of the labour code, employees have a right to disconnect, with a possible fine of EUR 3,750 for non-compliant companies, according to Connexion France, though it concedes the rules are not always followed where extra effort is part of the culture.

The aggregate picture is moderate rather than monastic. Eurostat reports that the EU average in 2025 was 35.9 hours a week, down from 36.9 in 2015, with Greece highest at 39.6 and the Netherlands lowest at 31.9. The distinguishing features of French working life, on the evidence of the sources above, are less the raw hours than the lunch, the holiday and the paperwork that accompanies working beyond your allotment. An expatriate who arrives expecting a sabbatical in all but name will be corrected by the second week of January.

## United Arab Emirates

The official rulebook is the u.ae government portal, which cites Federal Decree Law No. 33 of 2021: normal hours are 8 per day or 48 per week. Overtime is limited to 2 extra hours a day and paid at the normal hourly rate on basic salary plus 25%, rising to 50% extra between 10 pm and 4 am. Work on an off-day earns a substitute rest day or basic pay plus 50%. During Ramadan the working day shrinks by 2 hours, and from 15 June to 15 September outdoor work is banned between 12:30 and 3 pm. By Gulf standards this is a conscientious body of law, and the portal states it with the calm of an institution that has never had to enforce it on a Thursday night in a glass tower.

Reality is less restrained. Business Insider, citing the ILO, puts the UAE at 50.9 hours a week, second only to Bhutan at 54.4. A 2025 survey by the recruitment firm Halian, reported by Khaleej Times, found that 50% of UAE employees feel pressure to work beyond their scheduled hours, while 45% say their workload fits within a standard week. UBS, in its 2018 Prices and Earnings report, put Dubai at about 2,323 hours a year, sixth in the world. A 2022 index of city work-life balance by the security firm Kisi, reported by Expat.com, ranked Dubai 99th of 100, citing burnout of about 23% and 45 days of parental leave. One may regard these as separate measurements converging on one conclusion, or as the same conclusion arriving in different outfits.

Anecdote fills the margin. A British teacher quoted by Business Insider reports 12 to 14-hour days, and a finance worker describes about ten hours with no proper lunch. One of them adds that the job is conditional: "if you are not good at your job, they'll get rid of you and get someone else." The compensation for this is tax-free income and weather, which has historically been persuasive. It is worth noting that the law is not silent, only unevenly consulted, and that a contract with a stated overtime rate is a better protection than any amount of goodwill.

## The Reckoning

On paper the two countries are not far apart in structure. Both cap overtime, both pay premiums of 25% and 50% in defined circumstances, and both provide rest or extra pay for work on days off. The difference is in how the arrangement is enforced by norms rather than by statute. France has a vocabulary for refusing: the pay slip, the compensatory rest, the right to disconnect, the works council. The UAE has a vocabulary for compliance: the deadline, the client, the colleague who is still there. Hofstede Insights scores France at 68 for power distance and 86 for uncertainty avoidance, a combination that explains a good deal about the volume of rules and the faith placed in them. I could not retrieve a UAE figure from a source I could read, and decline to estimate one.

The practical result is that France trades pay for time, and the UAE trades time for pay. French employees receive, in return for their hours, a statute that treats working beyond the week as the employer's problem, and the long-term residents quoted below speak warmly of holidays and security. Gulf employers, as the Dubai-based posters note, sell tax-free income and let the individual decide how much of the week to surrender. For a 27-year-old with a savings target, this is arithmetic. For someone with a child, a partner and a view about Sundays, it is a different arithmetic, and the Kisi ranking suggests which one the data prefers.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A long-term American resident of southwest France describes a predictable working day with set breaks and a proper lunch window, vacations of two weeks or more taken without apology, and a boss whose temperament matters more than any policy. The system, they concede, is expensive for small companies, but they judge it better than the American one.*

> *Expat.com — A French poster with some fifteen years across several companies pushes back on the romance: midday closures are mostly a small-town habit, and some of their jobs carried workloads so heavy that even a short break was hard to fit in. The 35 hours, in their account, did not guarantee a calm week.*

> *Expat.com — Another long-term resident of France says dinner at 8 pm and Monday closures are now a source of amusement rather than irritation after fifteen years, and that Sunday closure reads as family time. Another notes there is no single French pace, with dinner closer to 6 pm in many provinces.*

> *Expat.com (c. 2010) — An early contributor on Dubai reassures a prospective arrival that business practice resembles Europe, many firms are European-owned and women work and drive normally, but lists "normal long working hours" among the drawbacks, alongside heat and construction.*

> *British Expats (2012) — Posters comparing the Gulf with the UK give mixed accounts: one moved from a 37-hour week to a 48-hour week and adapted, another had a 7 am to 2 pm day and a short commute, while a third said long hours in Doha did not mean good work and that the region eventually burned them out.*

## Conclusion

If you want your evenings protected by something harder than good intentions, France is the better bet, provided you remember that the law protects the hours and not the atmosphere. Ask in interview what time the team actually leaves, read the company's disconnection policy and keep your overtime recorded. If you want the highest net pay and are willing to work close to the 50-hour average that the ILO figures imply, the UAE can deliver, but only if the contract states the hours, the overtime rate and the Ramadan arrangements in writing.

In either country the handbook describes the intended week and the office describes the real one, and the sensible expatriate attends to the second document. France gives you a statute and a lunch break. The UAE gives you a salary and a sunset visible from a conference room.
