# Vietnam Makes You Family by the Second Round of Beer; Switzerland Makes You Fill Out a Membership Form

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Share a plastic stool and a round of bia hơi with a stranger in Hanoi and you may have a dinner invitation by Thursday. Share an office with a colleague in Zurich for three years and you may still be on formal terms, address and all. InterNations' 2025 Expat Insider survey ranks Switzerland 41st of roughly 50 countries for "Finding Friends" — a country that also hosts somewhere between 80,000 and 100,000 registered clubs, one for roughly every 90 residents. Vietnam has no equivalent index, mostly because nobody felt the need to measure something that seems to happen on its own, over a shared plate, by the second course.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the first vague invitation to eat or drink — plans firm up as you go | Treat socializing like a calendar appointment with a fixed agenda |
| Learn to toast properly — "một, hai, ba, dzô" and drink together, not alone | Sip from your glass before the group toast lands |
| Ask early about family, hometown, and age — it's how rapport gets built | Read personal questions as intrusive; they're the opening move |
| Reciprocate hospitality by paying the next round or meal | Insist on splitting the bill evenly down to the đồng |
| Show up, even briefly, to weddings or family events if invited | Decline food or drink offered by an elder without good reason |
| Use language exchange meetups or Facebook groups to meet locals | Camp exclusively in expat bars and call it cultural immersion |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a Verein tied to an actual interest — choir, hiking club, shooting range | Expect friendship to bloom from small talk at the coffee machine |
| RSVP precisely and arrive on time, to the minute | Show up "fashionably late" — there is no such fashion here |
| Treat a home dinner invitation as a milestone, not a starting point | Invite yourself over, or expect one early |
| Pick up a few words of the local dialect, not just standard German or French | Assume textbook Hochdeutsch will get you into the joke |
| Plan for friendship to build over months or years of repeat contact | Take the initial reserve personally — it isn't about you |
| Bring a small gift if you're invited home | Skip the formal Sie/vous until told to drop it |

Vietnamese social life runs on what the *Journal of Mekong Societies* calls "bonding" social capital: dense trust concentrated in a known circle, replenished through near-constant contact, rather than the thinner "bridging" trust extended to strangers elsewhere. Gallup World Poll data shows a fifth of Vietnamese respondents are unsure whether a neighbor would return a lost wallet — moderate trust, but trust that thickens fast once you're inside the circle. Per Vietcetera's coverage of local business etiquette, entry runs largely on alcohol: "nhậu," the after-hours ritual of drinking and talking, functions as unofficial relationship infrastructure — declining the invitation can read as declining the relationship.

That infrastructure runs on time workers technically have less of than before. The ILO put average Vietnamese working hours at 41.74 per week in 2025, down from 45.3 the year prior, though the Labour Code still permits up to 300 hours of annual overtime, 400 in manufacturing. The World Values Survey finds Vietnam registers the lowest share worldwide, 12.8 percent, who call leisure time "very important" — not because relaxation is undervalued, but because relationship-building mostly happens inside categories the survey counts separately: family, obligation, work. The bia hơi table isn't leisure. It's a second shift.

Foreigners tend to notice the warmth before the fine print. Threads on the Vietnam forum at Expat.com describe locals inviting outsiders to a café table within one conversation — with the catch that acquaintance-to-friend takes far longer, since much social life runs on family obligation a newcomer never fully joins.

Switzerland scores in the opposite direction on nearly every relevant Hofstede Insights metric: high individualism (roughly 68, against Vietnam's 20) and low power distance (34, against Vietnam's 70), a combination producing a flat, rule-bound, procedurally fair society — and a famously slow one to let anyone new into its living room. The OECD's *Social Connections and Loneliness in OECD Countries* report notes strong networks provide both emotional support and practical access to opportunity, and where missing, both suffer. In Switzerland's case the networks aren't missing, just sealed at a specific age. Per SWI swissinfo.ch, Swiss friendships are largely cohort-based, formed in school, university, or sports clubs — an adult arriving from elsewhere isn't excluded on purpose, the architecture simply has no side door.

The country compensates with density of formal structure: 80,000 to 100,000 associations nationwide, per reporting under the Federal Statistical Office's cultural-heritage documentation at lebendige-traditionen.ch, with roughly half the population active in one for fourteen-plus years. That's an extraordinary rate of participation — and, per InterNations data, a poor conversion rate for newcomers: 52 percent of expats socialize mostly with other expats, against a 32 percent global average. The clubs exist. Getting past the waiting list is the actual project.

**The Reckoning.** Line the two up and the paradox sharpens: the society with almost no formal social infrastructure produces near-instant warmth, while the society with the densest club culture in the OECD ranks near the bottom for expat friend-making. Formal structure is not an open door — Switzerland's Vereine are easy to find and hard to be let into, while Vietnam has no comparable institution and doesn't seem to need one.

The trade-off is speed against durability. Vietnamese hospitality moves fast and runs on reciprocity that can, over time, feel like quiet debt — every dinner accepted is one eventually owed. Swiss friendship moves at the pace of a multi-year club subscription, but once granted tends to stay stable and low-maintenance for good. Neither country optimizes for the newcomer's convenience; both, in their own idiom, optimize for people already staying.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*One longtime poster on the Expat.com Vietnam forum described being startled by how quickly casual encounters turned into dinner invitations — strangers translating on their phones just to keep the conversation going over coffee.*

*Another thread on the same forum struck a more measured note: meeting people in Vietnam is easy, the poster wrote, but finding people with enough shared reference points for a real friendship takes far longer, since so much daily social life runs through family obligations an outsider never fully enters.*

*A Quora respondent answering why it's so hard to make friends in Switzerland pointed to school-era cliques that calcify by adulthood, arguing most Swiss core friend groups were locked in before any newcomer arrived and simply have no vacancies.*

*A Hacker News commenter based in Zurich offered a small mercy alongside the complaint: Swiss German, they noted, is different enough from standard German to function as its own language, and even Swiss people find each other hard to befriend across cantons — so a foreigner's frustration isn't personal, it's structural.*

*Readers responding to a Local Switzerland survey on friendship largely converged on one piece of advice: the breakthrough, when it came, arrived only after years of showing up consistently to the same choir, running club, or shooting association — treated less as an outing than as a long-term investment with a delayed payout.*

### Conclusion

Neither model is better; they solve different problems. To feel embedded within a month, book the flight to Vietnam, accept every invitation, and budget emotional energy for the obligations that follow. For a friendship that survives decades without upkeep, Switzerland will eventually deliver one — provided you're still showing up to hiking club in year three. The honest version of the brochure would just say: Vietnam hands you a friend and asks you to earn it retroactively; Switzerland makes you earn it first and hands you nothing you didn't specifically apply for.
