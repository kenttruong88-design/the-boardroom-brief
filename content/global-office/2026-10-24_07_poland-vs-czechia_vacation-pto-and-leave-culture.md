# The Leave Ledger: Poland and Czechia Both Say You Can Rest, With an Asterisk

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Every contract in Central Europe contains a small, cheerful lie: a number of vacation days, printed in bold, as though a number were the same thing as rest. Poland's labor code promises 20 or 26 days, depending on seniority credit earned partly by having gone to university. Czechia promises four flat weeks to everyone, no essay required. Both countries, per Eurofound, sit in the bloc of post-2004 EU members averaging roughly five fewer collectively-agreed leave days than the bloc's founding members — a gap of more than a week, quietly baked into the paperwork before you've unpacked a single box.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Count years of education toward seniority — a master's adds 8 years, pushing 20 days to 26 | Assume your 20/26 days includes public holidays — Poland's 14 are separate, on top |
| Use up to 4 "on-demand" days yearly with zero notice, for real emergencies | Treat on-demand days as a scheduling trick — HR tracks the pattern |
| Spend unused leave before September 30 of the next year | Let leave quietly expire — there's a hard deadline, but it's not immediate |
| Expect employer-paid sick leave (80%, often 100%) for the first 33 days | Expect your employer to pay past day 33 — ZUS (state insurance) takes over, and it can lag |
| Ask whether Saturday public holidays were compensated with a weekday off | Assume Saturday holidays are simply lost, as in some Western markets |

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate extra days at offer stage — a third of employers (more in banking, insurance, energy) grant 5+ beyond the legal floor | Assume the statutory 4 weeks is generous locally — most white-collar peers have more |
| Check if probation excludes leave accrual — some contracts hold vacation until month four | Expect accrual from day one by default |
| Expect your employer's final say on timing, including a right to recall you (with pay) | Book non-refundable travel before written sign-off — recalls, though rare, are legal |
| Plan one longer trip rather than several short ones, for the "unwound" effect | Assume short breaks add up the same way — local survey data says they don't |
| Ask about "sick days" versus statutory sickness benefit — many employers offer none extra | Assume paid sick leave works as in Western Europe — early days are often unpaid by the employer |

### Poland: The Credentialed Vacation

Poland's system rewards you for having already lived a slower life somewhere else. Under Article 154 of the Labour Code, an employee with fewer than 10 years of recognized work experience gets 20 paid vacation days a year; cross the threshold — a university degree alone contributes 8 years toward it — and the entitlement jumps to 26. It is less a vacation policy than a résumé bonus, paid annually. On top of that, Poland adds 14 public holidays, including Christmas Eve, folded into the calendar only since 2025.

The more interesting wrinkle shows up off the books. Per discussion threads among IT professionals on Hacker News, a large share of Polish tech workers operate on B2B contracts rather than standard employment — a structure that trims taxes but, as one engineer put it, leaves vacation and sick days "still common in practice" yet "not really enforced," a matter of client goodwill rather than statute. Formal entitlement and lived experience run on separate tracks.

Employers have noticed that workers notice. A Hays Poland survey found roughly one in three employees ranks extra paid time off as their single most valued benefit, ahead of gym memberships and private healthcare, and that one in ten companies now exceeds the statutory minimum — occasionally offering unlimited holiday contingent on team sign-off and maintained output.

### Czechia: The Flat Rate, Unevenly Spent

Czechia's offer is blunter: four flat weeks, no seniority math, once probation ends. It reads as egalitarian until you notice what the flat rate conceals. A 2019 push by the Communist Party to lift the minimum to five weeks — arguing that public-sector staff already get it — stalled against employer-association cost objections, leaving private-sector Czechs behind their own civil service.

What private employers do instead is informally close the gap. Survey data from Platy.cz, cited by Expats.cz, finds roughly a third of Czech employers grant extra leave beyond the legal floor, concentrated in insurance, banking, and energy — sectors where margins can absorb generosity. The result is a market where "the legal minimum" is a number almost no actual contract uses, and where, per one Hacker News developer comparing a Czech employer to a former US one, the smaller entitlement comes with a larger relief: requests rubber-stamped on sight rather than routed through layers of pre-approval.

The deeper wrinkle is what Czechs do once leave is granted. A poll reported by Radio Prague International found only 28% of Czechs feel genuinely refreshed after a holiday, down from 39% in 2008 — attributed partly to staying reachable by phone, partly to cramming too many destinations into too short a trip. The days exist. The disconnection, increasingly, does not.

### The Reckoning

Here is the paradox: Poland legislates generosity by seniority, 26 days for the credentialed and loyal, while Czechia hands everyone a flat 20 and then quietly lets a third of employers top it up off the books. Poland's system looks meritocratic on paper and gray-market in tech; Czechia's looks egalitarian on paper and sector-stratified in practice. Neither official number governs your actual year off — the real entitlement lives in the sector or contract type, not the statute.

The other inversion is psychological. Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimension framework places both countries high on uncertainty avoidance relative to Western Europe, tracking with how rigidly both labor codes read — yet Czech workers, by their own national polling, are the ones who can't switch off once leave is granted, while Polish B2B contractors, with the least formal protection in this comparison, report some of the least overtime pressure. The country with the tidier rulebook isn't obviously the one with the calmer vacation.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a Polish software engineer described vacation and sick days under B2B contracting as "still common in practice" but a gray area that's "not really enforced," left to whichever client is paying that month's invoice.*

> *Hacker News — a Czech developer compared a current employer's 20 vacation days and no company sick days to a prior US role's 25 days and five sick days, noting dryly that "vacation is auto-approved," which mattered more day to day than the headline number.*

> *Hacker News — a Poland-based remote employee reported no pressure to work overtime, and said travel didn't require burning extra vacation time — a flexibility they hadn't expected going in.*

> *Personio Community forum — an HR administrator at a Swiss company with a Polish office was blindsided by the rule that Polish holidays falling on a Saturday must be compensated with an added day off, and by leave being calculated per calendar year regardless of employer, so new hires can arrive already partway through their own entitlement.*

> *expat.com forum — a new hire in Czechia discovered their probation period meant no leave accrued at all in month one, and spent a thread quoting Labour Code provisions back to another member just to confirm the calculation was legal.*

### Conclusion

If the choice comes down to the printed number, Poland wins for anyone with a degree and a decade of seniority; Czechia wins for anyone who wants simplicity without the credential test. But the number on the contract is the least informative part of either offer. The real question is which gray zone you're signing up for: Poland's informal B2B economy, where entitlement is a matter of goodwill, or Czechia's sector lottery, where your industry quietly decides whether the legal floor is the whole story or just the opening bid.

What I'd tell a friend over a drink: ask about the sector before you ask about the number, because in both countries the Labour Code is just the opening offer, and the real contract gets negotiated in the gaps the law left open.
