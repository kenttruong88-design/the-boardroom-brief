**Bonding Over Crickets, Bonding Over Curry: What "Team Building" Actually Means in the Philippines and Malaysia**

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇲🇾 Malaysia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Filipino office will throw you a party with fried crickets and a biscuit-eating relay race just because you're missing a holiday with your own family. A Malaysian office will skip the games entirely and simply expect you to share a meal with a client before either of you mentions business. Both are, by any honest measure, "team bonding." Only one of them comes with a scoreboard.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the Christmas party, the birthday treat, the pizza run — these aren't optional extras, they're how trust gets built | Decline every informal gathering and expect the same warmth in meetings |
| Address seniors as "Sir" or "Ma'am" even after they say first names are fine | Drop honorifics immediately just because someone says "call me by my first name" |
| Give critical feedback privately, gently, and specifically | Correct a colleague's mistake in front of the team |
| Expect a "yes" that really means "I'll try," and check in rather than assume | Take a polite agreement as a firm commitment without following up |
| Return favors — a colleague who helped you expects the relationship to go both ways eventually | Treat a colleague's help as a one-off transaction with no further obligation |
| Let a new local friend know early if you genuinely can't cover a shared cost | Assume every warm invitation is financially motivated, and start being stingy to compensate |

#### 🇲🇾 Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build the personal relationship before the business one — share a meal, ask about family | Launch straight into the pitch or the ask at a first meeting |
| Learn which colleagues are Malay, Chinese, or Indian and adjust small customs accordingly (handshakes, holidays, food) | Treat "Malaysian culture" as one single, uniform thing |
| Give a boss who asks about your wellbeing an honest, if diplomatic, answer | Stay guarded with a manager who's genuinely trying to build rapport |
| Deliver criticism of a superior's idea privately and indirectly | Publicly disagree with or correct someone more senior in a meeting |
| Socialize with local colleagues, not just the other expats in the building | Form an expat-only social bubble and wonder later why local relationships feel thin |
| Let a woman initiate a handshake if you're a man in a mixed professional setting | Extend your hand to a Malaysian Muslim woman by default |

Filipino workplace culture runs on a vocabulary that doesn't translate cleanly into English, which is itself the point. *Pakikisama* — getting along, prioritizing group cohesion over individual credit — sits alongside *kapwa*, a recognized sense of shared humanity that CoDev's guide to Filipino teams describes as creating "extended family within the workplace." *Utang na loob*, a debt of gratitude, means a colleague who mentored you or covered for you once has created an obligation that quietly shapes your effort for years, not just the week after the favor. None of this is soft or incidental — it is the actual mechanism by which teams function, alongside a hierarchy real enough that subordinates keep saying "Sir" and "Ma'am" long after being told first names are fine. Team bonding here isn't a quarterly HR initiative. It's Thanksgiving fried crickets and a biscuit-eating relay thrown together by a Manila team for a homesick foreign manager, the kind of gesture one account from a Western manager described as proof that "perceived differences are easy to overcome" — a nice line, though it undersells how much deliberate effort the team put into making an outsider feel covered for.

Malaysian bonding culture starts from a different problem entirely: a workforce built from genuinely distinct ethnic and religious communities — Malay, Chinese, and Indian populations each carrying their own holidays, foods, and customs, per Expat Focus's guide to the country's office norms — that still has to function as one team without pretending the differences aren't there. The guide's central claim is almost disarmingly direct: "it is the norm to be friends with a person before conducting business with them." A shared meal, a round of golf, genuine interest in a colleague's wellbeing — these aren't relationship-building extras bolted onto the "real" work. They're the precondition for the real work. One Malaysian executive, writing under his own name in the Malay Mail, framed the payoff in blunter terms than most corporate language allows: Malaysia's ability to coordinate across language, faith, and background in hospitals, factories, and startups is a genuine competitive advantage — but, he's careful to add, only when organizations actively nurture it rather than assuming it maintains itself.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real divergence: Filipino bonding culture is loud, celebratory, and built around shared events — a party, a game, a feast, something you can point to and say "that happened." Malaysian bonding culture is quieter and more procedural — a meal before a meeting, a relationship established before a contract, something that happened but that nobody necessarily narrates afterward. A foreign manager walking into a Manila office will be told, pretty directly, when bonding is happening. A foreign manager walking into a Kuala Lumpur office might miss it entirely if they're looking for a party invitation instead of a lunch invitation. Both countries sit near the top of Hofstede's power-distance scale (94 for the Philippines, 100 for Malaysia) and well toward the collectivist end on individualism (32 and 26, respectively) — a shared structural backdrop that helps explain why group bonding, rather than individual standout performance, carries so much weight in both workplaces.

The trap for outsiders is assuming either country's warmth means the same thing a Western "team bonding offsite" means — a one-time event with no lasting obligation. It doesn't, in either place. In the Philippines, accepting help creates *utang na loob*, a genuine and lasting debt of gratitude. In Malaysia, sharing a meal with a colleague or client is understood as the actual beginning of trust, not a box checked before the "real" negotiation starts. Skip the ritual in either country, and you haven't saved time. You've just made every future interaction colder than it needed to be.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum (Philippines) — A poster going by Cyclone Ed said the hardest part of settling in wasn't logistics but relationships: his "biggest challenge has been knowing how to meet local people and move beyond casual commercial or service interactions to something more" — a problem no amount of pakikisama vocabulary solves on its own.*

> *Expat.com forum (Philippines) — Another poster, Garthon Frederick, offered the practical flip side: "Making friends here is easy if you are humble and truly interested in others," but warned that some people "assume you have plenty of money" and expect you to pay — advising new arrivals to learn, over time, to tell the difference between warmth and expectation.*

> *Hacker News — The owner of a small outsourcing business described growing a 10-person Philippines-based team almost entirely through referrals: when he needed more developers, he asked his existing Cebu-based contact, who simply "found 3 more" — hiring, in practice, running through trust rather than job postings.*

> *Prialto company blog — Manager Annie Andre described a Thanksgiving party his Manila-based team threw for him and two colleagues who'd be missing the holiday with their own families: frog legs, fried crickets, Bicol express, and a biscuit-eating relay his team won outright. His own takeaway, that "perceived differences are easy to overcome," reads a little tidier than the amount of planning his team clearly put into it.*

> *Malay Mail (named op-ed) — Elman Mustafa El Bakri, a healthcare recruitment CEO, argued that Malaysia's real workplace advantage is coordinating across language and faith lines in hospitals and factories under pressure — but added pointedly that this advantage "cannot be taken for granted" and has to be actively nurtured, especially with younger staff.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, the honest framing is less "which culture is warmer" and more "which kind of warmth are you better at reciprocating." The Philippines will adopt you fast, loudly, and with real affection — and then expect you to show up for the next colleague's favor the same way everyone showed up for yours. Malaysia will take longer to let you in, through a slower sequence of shared meals and small gestures of interest — and then expect you to have genuinely learned something real about the person across the table, not just logged a lunch.

Neither country's bonding culture is decoration. Treat the party or the meal as optional, and you'll get exactly the shallow, transactional colleagues you assumed were normal everywhere else. Show up for real, and you'll find out why neither country considers "team bonding" a line item.
