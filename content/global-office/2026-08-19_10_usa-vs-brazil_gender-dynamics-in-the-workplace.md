# The US Pay Gap Is 16 Cents. Brazil's Is Worse, and the Culture Around It Is Louder.

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

American workplaces have spent two decades building formal infrastructure — HR policies, pay-transparency laws, DEI programs now under political pressure but still operationally present — around a gender gap that persists anyway. In 2024, US women earned about 83.6 cents for every dollar men earned. Brazil's gap runs wider still, with wage-equality scores suggesting a gap approaching 47 percent for similar work, layered onto a workplace culture where traditional gender norms are discussed far more openly, for better and worse, than the more procedural American version of the same conversation.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use available pay-transparency tools and salary bands where your state or employer provides them | Assume formal policy alone has closed the gap; the numbers say otherwise |
| Document your achievements proactively — data suggests women are promoted to manager at lower rates than men with comparable records | Assume merit alone will be visibly recognized without you tracking and communicating it |
| Build sponsor relationships with senior leaders, not just mentors — sponsorship correlates more with promotion | Rely solely on informal "boys' club" networking access that isn't equally available to everyone |
| Know your rights under formal HR / EEOC frameworks if discrimination arises | Assume workplace culture alone will self-correct without policy backing it up |
| Negotiate directly — American norms expect it, and gaps widen when one side doesn't | Assume raises and promotions are handed out for good work alone; self-advocacy still matters here too |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect more overt discussion of gender roles in daily office life than in the more procedural US | Assume machismo-adjacent norms are universal — attitudes vary sharply by region, generation, and industry |
| Look for employers with explicit parental and family policies; Brazil's formal maternity leave is comparatively strong | Assume strong formal leave law automatically translates into equal career treatment after returning |
| Build a peer network among women in your specific industry — informal support networks matter significantly | Assume HR-style formal recourse mechanisms are as robust or consistently enforced as in the US |
| Watch how flexibly senior leadership is gendered in your specific company or sector before joining | Generalize from one traditional employer to the whole Brazilian economy — urban, younger workplaces vary widely |
| Address dynamics directly and personally if something feels off — relationship-based cultures often respond better to direct, personal conversation than formal complaint processes alone | Assume a formal complaint process will function the way an equivalent one might in the US |

---

The US gender gap in 2026 persists despite decades of formal apparatus built specifically to close it: OECD analysis attributes roughly three-quarters of the gap to unequal pay for similar work within the same firm, rather than differences in occupation choice, pointing to something closer to plain discrimination or undervaluation than a structural explanation. Hofstede's data shows the US scoring relatively high on masculinity (62) alongside high individualism, a combination researchers associate with competitive, achievement-oriented cultures where advancement is framed as an individual meritocratic outcome — even when the actual data shows outcomes diverging sharply by gender within that supposedly neutral system.

Brazil's gap is both wider by some measures and differently textured. Hofstede data places Brazil higher on power distance and closer to average on masculinity than the US, layered onto a culture where traditional gender roles — men as primary earners, women managing home and childcare alongside paid work — remain visible in workplace hierarchies and everyday interaction, particularly outside the largest, most cosmopolitan cities. Machismo as a cultural framework is increasingly viewed as undesirable among younger, urban, educated Brazilians, according to cultural research, but the shift is generational and regional rather than complete, and formal HR-style recourse mechanisms common in US corporate life are less consistently present or enforced across Brazilian employers.

The reckoning is that the US built more scaffolding around a problem it hasn't actually solved, while Brazil has less scaffolding around a problem that's arguably larger and more visible day to day. An American woman moving to a Brazilian office may be startled by how openly gender roles get discussed and assumed compared to the more careful, policy-mediated American workplace vocabulary — and by how much less formal recourse exists if something goes wrong. A Brazilian professional moving to an American office may be surprised that despite all the formal policy, informal "boys' club" dynamics — who gets invited to the after-work drink, whose ideas get credited in the room — persist almost as stubbornly as anywhere else, just with better paperwork surrounding them.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing how gender roles are determined in Brazil said the traditional expectation was still that men should build a career to support a family while women managed a career alongside childcare and household responsibilities — a norm openly discussed rather than left unspoken, unlike in more policy-cautious American offices.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about machismo attitudes across Latin America said the framework was increasingly viewed as undesirable among younger, urban, educated Brazilians, particularly in southern cities and large metros, but remained normalized in more traditional or rural workplace settings.*

> *teamblind.com — A discussion about hiring practices at a major US tech employer described specific roles marked for women candidates and recruiters under pressure to find qualified female applicants in male-dominated pipelines, illustrating that formal diversity mechanisms in American tech exist precisely because informal outcomes hadn't closed the gap on their own.*

> *Lean In & McKinsey Women in the Workplace research — Widely cited annual data found that for every 100 men hired or promoted to manager in corporate America, only 87 women advanced at the same rate — the first rung of the ladder, not the top, where the earliest structural gap in most careers actually opens up.*

> *Quora — A respondent discussing female-dominated workplaces and women reporting to female bosses noted that gender dynamics complaints in either country weren't necessarily about the gender of leadership itself, but about whether a given workplace's specific culture rewarded direct communication and self-advocacy — traits that both American and Brazilian women described navigating differently depending on who they reported to.*

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing either move, the honest advice is the same everywhere: ask specific questions about actual promotion data and parental-leave return rates at your target employer, not the national policy headline. The US will hand you more formal language and legal backing to describe what's happening to you; Brazil will hand you a workplace where the same dynamics are more openly named but less procedurally protected. Neither country has actually closed the gap — they've just built different vocabularies for living inside it.
