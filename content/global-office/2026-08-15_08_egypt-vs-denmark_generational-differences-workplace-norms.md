**Earning Respect vs. Assuming It: Egypt vs. Denmark**

**🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇩🇰 Denmark**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a Cairo office, seniority is still often treated as its own credential — age itself carries a kind of moral authority that younger employees are expected to defer to, mentorship included, whether or not they asked for it. In a Copenhagen office, a landmark generational study found that what looks like Gen Z's rising demands may just be older colleagues misreading normal emotional self-awareness as entitlement — and that the generations agree on what good leadership looks like far more than anyone assumed. Egypt is negotiating a real hierarchy gap. Denmark is mostly negotiating a perception gap.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show visible deference to older colleagues, even ones without formal seniority over you | Publicly correct or contradict a senior colleague, regardless of how confident you are in the answer |
| Expect senior employees to want to mentor you, whether or not you asked for it | Decline mentorship offers outright; frame any pushback diplomatically instead |
| Bring efficiency and digital fluency to the table as a genuine value-add, not a point of friction | Frame your speed or new methods as proof that older colleagues' approach is outdated |
| Be patient with slower-moving processes tied to traditional ways of working | Push aggressively for rapid change without first building trust with senior stakeholders |
| Recognize that respect for elders is taught early and runs deep — it's not performative here | Mistake formal deference for genuine agreement; read between the lines |

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuine equality of voice regardless of age or position in most meetings | Assume seniority alone earns automatic deference from younger colleagues |
| Talk openly about emotional needs and flexibility — it's normalized, not seen as weakness | Dismiss a younger colleague's request for flexibility as generational softness |
| Look for managers who mentor through guidance and inspiration, not just oversight | Expect a purely directive management style; it under-delivers for most age groups here |
| Trust that Danish generations broadly agree on what good leadership looks like | Assume Gen Z and Boomers are locked in fundamental conflict — the data says otherwise |
| Engage with work-life balance conversations as a shared value, not a young-employee demand | Treat balance-focused requests as unique to junior staff; older Danes want it too |

---

Egypt's generational dynamics are still built substantially on inherited respect for age. Fast Company Middle East reports that Gen Z professionals are increasingly leading strategic teams in Egyptian offices, but their values diverge sharply enough from older colleagues to create real confusion about what good leadership even looks like day to day. Respect for elders is taught from childhood, and that ingrained sense of authority shapes daily workplace dynamics well beyond formal job titles — senior employees expect to mentor newer hires as a matter of course, even when younger staff, more digitally fluent and trained to move faster, may not feel they need it (Egyptian Streets). The tension isn't abstract: only about a third of Egyptians report having core digital competencies, and formal company training remains limited, meaning younger, more tech-capable employees are often working inside structures still calibrated to an older generation's pace and expectations (Economic Research Forum).

Denmark's generational story looks different because the underlying hierarchy is already so flat. A major Danish study on leadership expectations across age groups found surprising consensus — the widest gaps in what people want from management sit specifically between Boomers and Gen Z, with much smaller differences among the generations in between (CEC European Managers). Copenhagen Business School's research goes further, arguing that much of what older colleagues perceive as Gen Z being "extra demanding" actually reflects a generational skill in checking in with one's own needs, not entitlement. Danish Gen Z employees are notably less focused on climbing a ladder than global stereotypes suggest — only about 9% cite reaching a leadership position as their primary career goal — and instead want managers who mentor through guidance rather than pure oversight (Deloitte Denmark).

### The Reckoning

The core difference is where the generational friction actually lives. In Egypt, it lives in the formal structure itself — age confers authority by default, and younger employees have to navigate around that structure even when their skills or ideas are clearly stronger. In Denmark, the structure is already egalitarian, so the friction has shifted almost entirely into interpretation — older colleagues misreading younger colleagues' emotional openness as a demand, when research suggests it's simply a different vocabulary for needs every generation has always had.

Hofstede's power distance dimension explains much of the Egyptian dynamic — a higher score there formalizes deference to age and seniority as part of the system itself. Denmark's very low power distance means the generational conversation isn't really about who outranks whom; it's a conversation happening among relative equals about differing communication styles, which is a fundamentally easier gap to close.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone reflecting on cross-generational tension at work admitted a version of guilt familiar in many hierarchical cultures: respecting older colleagues deeply while privately finding their slower pace and older methods genuinely frustrating, and wondering whether that frustration made them unreasonable rather than simply impatient.*

> *thedanishdream.com — A piece on Gen Z workers in Denmark argued that older managers frequently mistake healthy boundary-setting for a lack of work ethic, when in practice the younger employees in question were often just as productive — they simply communicated their limits explicitly instead of absorbing them silently the way older generations were trained to.*

> *Reddit — A manager who'd worked with teams in both Cairo and Copenhagen observed that giving direct, corrective feedback to a younger Egyptian employee in front of others caused visible discomfort in a way it never did in Denmark, where junior staff pushed back on ideas from senior leadership as a matter of course.*

> *Deloitte Denmark GenZ Millennial Survey — Survey data found young Danish employees rank flexibility and personal development alongside, not above, traditional markers of career success, complicating the narrative that this generation values comfort over ambition.*

> *Quora — A younger professional working under an older boss in a hierarchical Middle Eastern office wrote that the fastest way to gain real influence wasn't to demonstrate competence loudly, but to earn trust quietly over time — competence alone, without the relationship, didn't move decisions the way patience and deference eventually did.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two environments, ask yourself whether you build credibility faster through patience or through direct contribution. In Egypt, expect to earn authority gradually, through demonstrated respect for the people already holding it — arriving with a plan to "modernize" the team quickly will likely backfire regardless of how good your ideas are. In Denmark, expect your ideas to be judged on their own merits almost immediately, generational skepticism notwithstanding — but don't mistake the flat structure for the absence of expectations; Danes across every generation, the data shows, actually agree on what they want from you.
