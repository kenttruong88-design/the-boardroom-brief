# Home Office by Written Agreement: Remote Work in Norway and Canada

🇳🇴 Norway · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Norway, neither the employer nor the employee can force a home office arrangement on the other. In Canada, the federal government has told core public service departments that staff will be in the office at least four days a week from July 2026, up from three. One country has treated remote work as a matter for negotiation. The other has treated it as a pandemic-era hobby that has run its course.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put any regular home office arrangement in writing, covering extent, hours and equipment | Demand home office as a right; employers cannot be forced either |
| Agree in advance who owns and maintains the equipment | Assume the employer reimburses everything; tax treatment varies and must be documented |
| Leave on time; overtime is not a status symbol | Stay late to impress, since it may prompt questions rather than praise |
| Admit mistakes early, because colleagues forgive errors faster than cover-ups | Hide a problem and hope the flat hierarchy fails to notice |
| Take your full holiday and disconnect while away | Send messages to colleagues on leave and expect a cheerful reply |
| Expect decisions to take longer because everyone is heard, then follow the outcome | Re-open a decision after it has been made |

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the attendance policy in the offer letter, because the days required may change | Assume a hybrid promise will survive the next leadership review |
| Ask whether the office days are a rule, a target or a suggestion | Treat every hybrid policy as the same thing |
| If you work in the public sector, check what your collective agreement says on telework | Assume federal, provincial and private employers follow one pattern |
| Calculate the commute before accepting a role; Toronto has the longest average among major cities | Ignore the cost of transit, which averages 44 minutes for users |
| Track what your team actually does against the stated minimum | Quit in anger before you have a replacement offer |
| Keep a written record of any informal remote arrangement | Rely on a verbal arrangement made in 2020 |

## Norway: Trust, in Triplicate

Statistics Norway reported that 44 percent of employed people worked from home at least one day in 2022, according to a ScienceNorway summary of the figures. Information and communication professionals were among the heaviest users, and healthcare, hotels and restaurants among the lightest, which is what one would expect of professions in which the patient is in the building. Public employees were also heavy users, although 37 percent of them had no days at home.

The legal framework is polite but specific. Altinn, the Norwegian government portal, states that neither party can force a home office arrangement, that regular arrangements require a written agreement and that most of the Working Environment Act continues to apply. The employer must provide necessary equipment for regular arrangements and, as far as practical, ensure that the workspace is safe. Injuries at home are usually not treated as occupational if the employee chose home office for practical reasons. The labour inspectorate cannot enter private homes. It can, however, ask to see the paperwork.

The evidence on whether any of this works is modest but encouraging. A 2022 piece republished by SINTEF, originally from Dagens Næringsliv, cites in-house research at Telenor in Norway that found no significant change in productivity when employees worked more from home, and well-being at the same level or higher. It is one company and it is now several years old, so it proves less than its enthusiasts suggest. It does explain why Norwegian managers have not felt the need to issue memos.

The culture around the contract matters more than the contract. Hofstede's dimensions, as summarised on Wikipedia, give Norway a masculinity score of 8, which is to say a society that prizes consensus and quality of life over visible ambition. The OECD hours data, tabulated on Wikipedia, put the average Norwegian worker at 1,386 hours a year in the latest year shown, against 1,687 for Canada. An employee-stories page from OsloMet, which is promotional and dates from 2019, says offices empty early on Friday afternoons. Treat it as marketing, then notice that the other sources agree.

## Canada: The Office Strikes Back

Statistics Canada reported that 17.4 percent of employed people mostly worked from home in May 2025, down from 18.7 percent a year earlier, the fourth consecutive annual decline. Another 5.1 percent were hybrid, and 77.6 percent worked only outside the home. Commuters rose for a fourth year, with Ottawa-Gatineau the largest jump at nearly 10 percent.

The public sector is leading the retreat. CP24 reported in February 2026 that executives would be required on site five days a week from May 4, and other core public service employees at least four days a week from July 6, up from the current three. The unions were unimpressed. The Professional Institute of the Public Service said the mandate was about optics rather than performance, while the Canadian Association of Professional Employees pointed to a tribunal ruling that telework can be negotiated. CTV News reported in January 2026 that tens of thousands of provincial staff in Ontario and Alberta were expected back five days a week.

Employees are not delighted. The Angus Reid Institute surveyed 1,918 adults in July 2025 and found that 59 percent would prefer to work mostly or entirely from home. Among those who had been asked to increase office days, 72 percent complied, 23 percent agreed but often did not meet the requested days, and 5 percent quit. Among those currently remote, 24 percent said they would likely quit or start looking right away. Linda Duxbury, a Carleton University professor, told BNN Bloomberg it is easy for people to say they will quit and harder to do it, which is the sort of observation that remains accurate until the labour market improves.

Even the hybrid arrangement is untidy. Among hybrid workers in the Angus Reid sample, a small group of 235, 35 percent had to be in the office more than half the week, 38 percent only one or two days and 28 percent had no set policy at all. The survey also reported that 51 percent of those sent back for more days were upset, 27 percent of them very. As to productivity, 57 percent of respondents told the pollster that remote workers are as productive as office workers or more, a view that appears to be less popular in the offices that issue the mandates.

## The Reckoning

The difference is not the amount of remote work but who owns the decision. Norway has put the arrangement into a written agreement that both sides must accept, inside a legal framework that still applies at home. Canada has mostly left it to the employer, which is why a federal department and a provincial government can reverse course within a year without anyone needing to sign anything. The Canadian worker has preferences, polling and, in some public sector cases, a union. The Norwegian worker has all of these plus a requirement that the other side must say yes.

That said, Norway is not a remote work paradise. It is a society that remains in the office most days for the usual reasons and prefers to settle disputes through consensus. A Canadian arriving in Oslo should not expect permanent home office, only a more reasonable conversation about it. A Norwegian arriving in Ottawa should expect the conversation to be shorter.

There is also the matter of what each country asks of the person at home. Norway's rules assume the home is still a workplace in law, with working-hours rules, equipment obligations and a risk assessment that includes the spare room. Canada's debate, judging by the polling, concerns whether the worker may be there at all. The first approach produces paperwork. The second produces petitions, which are longer and rarely read.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tekna interview, a French researcher in Trondheim says the flat culture means nobody acts like the boss, and that he sometimes leaves early to walk his dog, with colleagues assuming he will make up the time later, a contrast with the long French office day.*

> *Tekna interview, a Chinese professional now in Oslo describes Norway as a trust-based society where family comes first, holidays are long and people almost fully disconnect from work messages while away, though Norwegians can be reserved and slow to warm up.*

> *City of Oslo website, a French lawyer who has worked in Norway for 15 years says trust underpins working life, leaders assume staff are doing their jobs without close supervision, leaving on time is normal and regularly staying late may prompt questions.*

> *Hacker News, a 2023 thread on return-to-office mandates, commenters complain that employers called remote work permanent and then reversed course, others argue for optional offices rather than mandates, and some note that bargaining power shifts back to employers when the job market weakens.*

> *CBC News, a Toronto commuter who travels daily from Alliston says her job involves paper documents that cannot be taken home, and that she would like a balance between in-person and remote work.*

## Conclusion

If flexibility is the deciding factor, Norway offers the more predictable arrangement, because the terms are written down and the framework protects both sides. Canada offers a more variable one, in which the answer depends on the sector, the employer and the month. Neither country guarantees a remote job. Norway guarantees a conversation, which is not nothing.

Before accepting an offer in either country, ask for the attendance policy in writing, ask how it has changed in the past year and ask whether the person describing it will still be there in six months. In Norway, someone will probably say yes to all three. In Canada, someone will probably say they will get back to you.
