**Togo to Ghana: The Lunch Gets Louder, the Hierarchy Doesn't Loosen**

🇹🇬 Togo · 🇬🇭 Ghana

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Togo and Ghana share a border, a coastline, and, per the 2010 Togolese census, roughly 29,416 Ghanaians living in Togo — the third-largest foreign nationality there, after Benin and Niger. What they do not share is an office soundtrack. Cross from a Lomé conference room, where a meeting opens with a formal round of individual greetings in French, into an Accra open-plan floor, where someone's aunt's fried yam is already circulating desk to desk, and you will have travelled about 160 kilometres and landed in two entirely different registers of workplace warmth.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇬 Togo

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually when entering a room, even briefly. | Skip the greeting ritual to save time — walking in and getting straight to business reads as rude. |
| Accept tea or a drink offered before discussing business. | Decline hospitality outright; it can be read as distrust. |
| Expect business conducted primarily in French, including written correspondence. | Assume English will carry you through contracts or official meetings outside a handful of bilingual younger colleagues. |
| Show deference to senior staff through formal greetings and titles. | Challenge a senior colleague's decision openly in a group setting. |
| Expect meetings to open with small talk before the agenda. | Launch directly into the agenda item on arrival. |
| Follow up patiently on approvals moving through the hierarchy. | Assume silence means no — a follow-up is expected, not pushy. |

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join in when a colleague shares snacks or food at their desk. | Repeatedly decline a shared meal in favour of "just working" — it reads as standoffish. |
| Expect the office to double as a social space for football, politics, and family talk. | Be surprised when non-work conversation eats into the workday; it's normal, not a sign of a lazy office. |
| Show up on time yourself, while building in slack for others. | Publicly call out a habitually late colleague — raise it privately instead. |
| Take quiet pride in visible, steady work. | Be the one who's visibly "too eager" to impress the boss — colleagues will notice, and needle you for it. |
| Expect the densest concentration of formal jobs to be in Accra. | Assume the same corporate density exists outside the capital. |
| Greet with a handshake and a few pleasantries before business starts, even in a hurry. | Skip greetings because you're rushed — it reads as disrespectful regardless of your schedule. |

### Togo

Hierarchy in a Togolese office is real but formally choreographed. Government bodies and traditional industries run top-down, with senior management holding key decisions and staff showing deference through formal greetings and careful language, according to Safeguard Global's culture guide; multinationals and startups are the exception, drifting toward flatter, more collaborative structures. French dominates corporate communication — contracts, formal emails, meetings — while Ewe and Kabiye handle informal, regional, and labour-intensive settings. A rising number of young professionals are bilingual in English, but counting on that outside a tech or multinational office is a bet, not a plan.

Relationship-building runs through tea, lunch, and small talk before any agenda item, and follow-up matters because approvals genuinely take time moving up the chain. Communication leans formal and indirect: a flat refusal is rare, and "I will try" or "let me check" are the polite stand-ins for "no," which a newcomer used to blunter cultures can spend weeks misreading as actual progress.

Team events in a Togolese office tend to be smaller and less calendar-disrupting than the regional norm. Funerals, a major social obligation across West Africa, are reported to consume far less money, time, and organisational effort in Togo than in neighbouring Ghana — a genuinely useful thing to know before assuming every colleague's multi-day absence works the same way on both sides of the border.

### Ghana

Ghana's workday runs a statutory 40 hours under the Labor Act, eight hours a day — tidy on paper — but the texture of the office is set less by the clock than by what moves across desks during it. Jobberman's own account of "Ghana Month" describes spontaneous, unscheduled food-sharing as a core office ritual: someone returns from lunch with extra fried yam, a colleague brings leftover groundnuts or sobolo, and a desk briefly becomes a gathering point for conversation about home, family, and National Service years rather than the task at hand. No one sends a meeting invite for this; it simply happens, and it is one of the main ways interns and new hires get folded into a team.

That same informality has a sharper edge once you look past the snacks. A widely shared 2012 piece on Ghanaian office culture catalogued the mirror image of the food-sharing warmth: colleagues who look busy while idle, high performers who get quietly resented and excluded rather than celebrated, and workers who undermine managers who insist on discipline in favour of lenient ones — alongside a note that most formal job offerings cluster overwhelmingly in Accra, which concentrates both the opportunity and the office politics in one city. Ghana also lands among the three lowest-scoring countries worldwide on Hofstede's long-term orientation index, a short-termism that shows up at work as a preference for quick wins and immediate recognition over patient, multi-year career-building.

Team events, Ghanaian-style, run in two very different registers. The planned, after-hours version increasingly happens over a cocktail built on akpeteshie, the local spirit — bars in Accra now serve it mint-and-sugar-smoothed or reworked into caipirinha and martini variations for white-collar workers unwinding after a full day, a trend bar owners hope will spread as a point of regional pride rather than a mark of where the spirit traditionally came from. The unplanned version is heavier: Ghanaian funerals are, by local consensus, expensive, elaborate, and genuinely time-consuming, with an entire supply chain of coffin-makers, caterers, musicians, and even professional mourners built around them, and a colleague disappearing for a multi-day funeral trip is common enough that it barely needs an explanation when they return.

### The Reckoning

Both offices are warm in ways a cold, transactional Western open-plan rarely manages — but the warmth runs on different clocks. Togo's hospitality is ritualized and front-loaded: you perform the greetings, accept the tea, and earn trust before the business conversation even starts, in a sequence that rarely changes once you have learned it. Ghana's warmth is unscheduled and ongoing: it shows up mid-afternoon as shared fried yam with no RSVP, and the social contract gets renegotiated constantly rather than settled once at the door.

The hierarchy underneath both is sturdier than either country's social ease suggests. A Togolese office softens its hierarchy with formality — you can always fall back on the correct greeting and the correct title and know you are on solid ground. A Ghanaian office softens its hierarchy with informality instead, which is friendlier day to day but considerably harder for an outsider to read: knowing when a joke about the boss is safe office banter and when it's actually a warning sign takes months that the formal Togolese script simply doesn't require.

That difference extends to how each country handles the inevitable awkward moment — the missed deadline, the obvious mistake, the colleague who has to be told no. Togo's formal register gives everyone a face-saving script to retreat to; Ghana's informal one relies on the group itself to manage the fallout, through gossip, gentle exclusion, or a well-timed joke, which works nearly as well but offers no instructions for a newcomer who hasn't yet learned to read the room.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *myjoyonline.com — A Ghanaian workplace commentary piece described colleagues who excel being quietly resented, gossiped about, or set up for trouble by peers, and noted that staff who work visibly hard enough to stand out risk being asked, not entirely as a joke, "does the company belong to your father?"*

> *expat.com (Africa forum) — A thread on dos and don'ts in Ghana passed on etiquette many newcomers only learn secondhand: don't point at anyone with your thumb, don't cross your legs so a shoe sole faces another person, and if offered a drink, pour a small amount on the ground first as a libation before you drink.*

> *expatexchange.com — An expat based in Accra summed up their biggest adjustment as a trust problem, not a logistics one: "People here only tell you what you want to hear, not the real situation — leading to not being able to trust anyone."*

> *briana-thomas.com — An American missionary living in Togo described being met, on every visit, with "Bon travail!" — "good work!" — offered to anyone seen doing anything, and came to see the constant verbal encouragement, plus neighbours routinely lending each other money to get by, as a communal reflex she hadn't expected before moving.*

### Conclusion

If the choice is Lomé or Accra, the honest advice has less to do with language — plenty of professionals manage a Togolese posting with serviceable French and a Ghanaian one with none at all — and more to do with your own tolerance for ambiguity. Togo will teach you a script, and once you have it, you can perform competence reliably. Ghana will teach you to read a room, continuously, with no script available, and reward you with genuine warmth for the effort.

Bring a phrasebook to Lomé. Bring patience, and an appetite, to Accra.
