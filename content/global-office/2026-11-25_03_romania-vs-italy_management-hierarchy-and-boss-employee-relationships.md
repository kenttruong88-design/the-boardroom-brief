# Romania's Good Fathers Meet Italy's Dottori

🇷🇴 Romania · 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Gallup's latest workplace survey finds that 31% of Romanian employees are engaged at work, against 11% in Italy and 12% across Europe. The country with the reputation for obedient staff and paternal bosses is, by this measure, nearly three times as enthusiastic as the country with the reputation for charm, long lunches and relaxed offices. Anyone planning a move for work should treat this as the first sign that hierarchy is not a simple dial running from stern to relaxed. It is two different arrangements, with two different sets of unwritten terms.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay formal with senior colleagues until a relationship has clearly been established | Assume first names and a casual tone on day one |
| Build a personal network early, since organisational life leans heavily on it | Rely on a cold application and a strong CV alone to open doors |
| Give precise instructions and confirm they were understood | Wait for staff to volunteer initiative nobody asked for |
| Recognise effort openly, because employees rank it first among what makes a good manager | Play favourites, which is the third most common complaint about tense manager relationships |
| Ask about grading, bonus or approval rules before accepting a post | Assume an honest professional standard will survive contact with a well-connected parent or client |
| Expect decisions to come from the top and plan around that | Challenge a senior decision in front of the team |

### Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles such as Dottore for university-educated managers | Treat titles as quaint |
| Bring data to support your point in meetings | Expect a decision to be taken in the room |
| Accept the coffee, greet colleagues and make small talk | Sit silently at your desk, which is read as odd |
| Answer a reasonable after-hours email | Cite working hours as a reason not to read it |
| Take your holiday in July or August with everyone else | Announce a month off in October |
| Learn Italian before you need it | Criticise Italian bureaucracy in front of Italians who are already doing so |

## Romania: Father Knows Best, Provided Father Is Informed

The best-known description of Romanian management comes from Romania Insider, which in 2011 described authority as highly concentrated, often exercised without checks, with staff expected to show quiet obedience. The same article describes a bargain. Workers accepted strict hierarchy in exchange for protection and clear direction, and the manager was cast as a good father figure. It also reports that managers often tried to protect jobs during restructuring. That piece is more than a decade old, and the author noted that foreign-trained managers were already introducing participative methods, but a second Romania Insider guide to local cultural values still describes a strong hierarchical system in which subordinates rarely question those above them.

The practical consequence is the Dutch manager in that guide, who cannot understand why his Romanian assistant will not take more initiative, and who finds the alternative of closer supervision draconian. The 2011 article offers a diagnosis: middle managers often preferred instructions to independent problem-solving because of a fear of mistakes and punishment. If you are arriving from a flat organisation, expect to spend your first months explaining that you would genuinely like to be told when something is wrong.

The survey data suggests the paternal bargain is not delivering on its half of the deal everywhere. In an eJobs survey of 1,224 Romanian employees in 2017, 35% described their relationship with their manager as tense. Their complaints were lack of respect (89%), lack of communication (88%), favouritism (86%) and under-appreciation (86%). Nearly 80% of respondents said a poor relationship with a manager hurts their productivity. A 2019 Undelucram article, citing a study by the national association of small and medium private enterprises, reports that 40% of respondents believed Romanian management does not meet EU standards, and cites Gallup in saying that only 30% of employees feel their opinions are considered. Gallup's current figures are kinder: 31% engaged, 14% actively disengaged, though that engagement share is down four points from a peak of 36% in 2023. Eurostat puts the average Romanian working week at 38.8 hours in 2024, against 36.0 hours for the EU, so the hierarchy is at least well attended.

Two further Romania Insider pieces fill in the detail. One says the basis of organisational life is the personal network, that newcomers without introductions can struggle to get in, and that traditional firms follow clear hierarchical lines while private firms run leaner processes and adapt more readily to volatile markets. Another, comparing Romanian and Dutch leaders, says authoritative leadership is perceived as a strength in Romania, while a participative style that many Dutch managers consider ordinary has been seen as a weakness by many Romanian firms and leaders. The sensible reading is that the sector matters. A Bucharest software company and a family-owned manufacturer may share a postcode and almost nothing else.

## Italy: A Flat Score on a Steep Staircase

Italy presents a curiosity. The Hofstede framework scores Italy at 50 on power distance, which is the middle of the scale, and at 75 on uncertainty avoidance, which is not. The first number suggests an informal workplace. The second explains why, in practice, rank and procedure keep their grip. HousingAnywhere's guide to Italian work culture says established organisations have a strict rank system that respects power and age, that senior leaders make the final strategic decisions, and that family businesses concentrate decisions among family members. It adds that credit for shared work tends to go to the highest-ranking person present and that senior people often decide in advance, so meetings are used to gather input rather than reach conclusions. Expat.com notes that university-educated managers are addressed as Dottore, though colleagues often soften the protocol over coffee.

The coffee matters more than it sounds. The Local advises against refusing it, on the grounds that coffee breaks are where colleagues build relationships and plan work, and says Italian employees are generally expected to answer email at any hour. Karli Drinkwater, writing about her own job at a family-run Italian school, describes poor communication between departments, plans that changed at the last minute, and take-home pay reduced by taxes and social contributions. She also found a better work-life balance than in her previous jobs. Gallup finds 11% of Italian employees engaged, 25% actively disengaged and 51% reporting a lot of stress the previous day, against 39% for Europe. Eurostat-based figures reported by Euronews put the Italian working week at 36.1 hours, close to the European average. Italians are not working more. They appear to be finding it harder.

Size and ownership also change the picture. HousingAnywhere describes startups in Italy as typically flat, with everyone holding an equal voice and credit, which is a considerable contrast to the family firm next door. It also notes that opinions in Italian meetings are often expressed directly, that raised voices are common, and that a moderate rise in volume is unlikely to seem rude. Directness, in other words, does not imply equality. A junior colleague may be free to disagree loudly and still find that the conclusion was reached before they arrived.

## The Reckoning

On paper, Romania is the more hierarchical country, and the evidence supports this. Decisions flow down, challenge is rare, and the manager is expected to supply protection, direction and some warmth. When the manager does, Gallup's 31% suggests the arrangement can work. When the manager does not, the eJobs complaints list reads like a charge sheet. The risk is not harshness so much as an authority with no effective check on it, and the newcomer who observes it should expect to be told what to do more often than they would like.

Italy's hierarchy is quieter but not softer. Titles, family ownership and decisions made before the meeting create a structure that is easy to miss in the first week and hard to ignore by the fourth. The warmth is real, and so is the stress. The more useful comparison is not which boss is friendlier but where the power sits. In Romania it is visible and personal. In Italy it is often informal and relational, and you will need to understand who the person with real influence is before the person with the title. Both countries also share an unexpected feature: the formal structure is not the whole story. Romanian employees in the eJobs survey were not uniformly aggrieved, since 65% described the relationship as good or very good, and 33% were connected with them on social media. Italian offices run on coffee, lunch and conversation, which soften rank without removing it. The title tells you who signs. The relationship tells you who is listening.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Bucharest forum) — an expat teacher's brother was told by supervisors at an elite private school to stop marking down students who did little work, because some had well-connected parents. One reply advised leaving; another noted that dismissal is hard in Romania, so unwanted staff are pressured to resign or simply not renewed.*

> *Expat.com (Romania forum) — a member described agencies in Romania as frustrating, with weak customer service and a general lack of urgency and punctuality.*

> *Reddit r/Italian — a commenter who had worked with Italian managers called the workplaces hierarchical, though less so than in Poland, and said bad bosses leave employees with no say and are inconsistent about overtime.*

> *Italy Chronicles — the blogger describes freelance bosses who assigned remote jobs without covering travel and expected constant availability, and notes that workers who fear they cannot find other jobs tend to tolerate it; two readers were similarly critical, though the author also knows people with considerate bosses.*

> *The Local Italy — a foreign teacher who joined a family-run Italian school was warned on her first day that the place was crazy, saw founders' relatives employed, and found permanent contracts very hard to obtain.*

## Conclusion

Choose the hierarchy you can read. In Romania, find out who decides, make your instructions clear, and do not mistake silence for agreement. In Italy, use the title, join the coffee, and assume the real discussion happened elsewhere. In both, a person with a local sponsor will do better than one with the better CV.

Before signing, ask to meet the person you will report to, and ask a former employee how that person behaves when something goes wrong. Both countries have good bosses. Neither lets you find out which kind you have until the first mistake.
