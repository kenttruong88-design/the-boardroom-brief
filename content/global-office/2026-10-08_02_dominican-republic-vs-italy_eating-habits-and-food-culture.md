**Lunch at Noon or Lunch at Three: What Nobody Tells You About Eating Your Way Through a Dominican or Italian Workday**

**🇩🇴 Dominican Republic · 🇮🇹 Italy**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Santo Domingo, lunch is rice, beans, and meat — *la bandera*, the national flag plated — eaten around 1pm, often reheated at a desk or fetched from a nearby fonda. In Rome, lunch is a seated, multi-course event that can run close to two hours, and showing up to dinner before 8pm marks you, gently but unmistakably, as a tourist. Both countries will tell a new arrival that food is central to the culture. Only one of them will judge your Tupperware for it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇴 Dominican Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect lunch around 1 to 1:30pm — plan meetings around it, not through it | Schedule a working lunch meeting at noon sharp and expect anyone to be hungry yet |
| Try *la bandera* (rice, beans, meat) before deciding you'll cook every meal yourself | Assume every neighborhood has the same supermarket access — smaller towns may have just one or two options |
| Budget for imported goods at nearly double the US price if you want familiar brands | Expect a Western-style grocery bill if you're buying American-brand products |
| Accept an invitation to eat at someone's home — hospitality runs through food here | Turn down home-cooked food repeatedly without a reason; it reads as rejection, not dietary preference |
| Use cash for smaller, local food purchases | Assume cards or ATMs work everywhere outside the capital and resort zones |
| Keep weekday lunches efficient; save the long, leisurely one for the weekend | Expect a two-hour lunch on a Tuesday the way you might in southern Europe |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat lunch sitting down, with people, even if it takes longer than you'd like | Eat a sandwich alone at your desk and expect no comment from colleagues |
| Save real dinner for 8pm or later, especially outside the smallest towns | Show up to a restaurant at 6:30pm expecting a full dinner service |
| Go to the *cena di lavoro* (work dinner) when colleagues organize one — it's a real relationship-building event, usually on the company's tab | Skip the work dinner repeatedly assuming it's optional small talk |
| Let each course of a multi-course meal arrive on its own time | Ask for the check the moment you finish eating — it can read as rushing your hosts |
| Accept that unfamiliar food combinations from your home country will draw commentary, sometimes daily | Take the teasing personally — it's closer to a ritual than an insult |
| Close shops and plans for Sunday family lunch if you're invited — it can run three hours | Schedule anything you can't move on a Sunday afternoon |

The Dominican workday still runs on a food rhythm that predates office culture and hasn't fully adjusted to it. Dominicancooking.com, a close chronicler of the country's actual eating patterns rather than its tourist-brochure version, puts lunch at roughly 1 to 1:30pm in most households today, drifted later from a traditional noon, with rural areas sometimes still eating as early as 11am. In provincial towns, activity visibly slows between noon and 2pm. But in the capital, where household members are scattered across jobs and schools, lunch has stopped being a home-cooked, sit-down affair for most office workers — it's a packed container reheated at a desk, or *la bandera* ordered from a nearby fonda. The expat experience bears this out plainly: one American family living in Santo Domingo reported paying roughly double the US price for imported groceries, while noting that "if you just eat rice, beans and local fruits and veggies you can do pretty well" on a modest local budget — two very different cost realities sitting inside the same city.

Italy's relationship to food is, by comparison, an institution with its own clock. Lunch, per Wanted in Rome's reporting on the cultural gap with faster-paced countries, is "a meal, eaten sitting down, with other people, at a pace that allows for conversation" — and outside Milan especially, that midday pause can run close to two hours, with streets and businesses visibly quieting down around it. Dinner doesn't really start before 8pm; arriving at 6:30 marks you as foreign in a way no accent will. The Local's reporting on Italian social rituals adds the workplace-specific piece: the *cena di lavoro*, a dinner with colleagues to mark a holiday or milestone, company-paid, that functions as a genuine relationship-builder rather than an optional extra. Skip enough of them and you're not just missing dinner — you're missing the actual informal org chart.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real contrast: the Dominican Republic's food culture has already bent, under economic and urban pressure, toward convenience — a packed lunch, a fast fonda order, a shortened weekday meal that looks almost nothing like the three-generation Sunday table that still exists on weekends and in smaller towns. Italy's food culture has, so far, refused to bend at all. The two-hour lunch and the 9:30pm dinner reservation aren't nostalgia; they are the current, functioning, non-negotiable default, defended by shuttered shops and genuinely empty restaurants before 8pm. A newcomer moving to Santo Domingo for work will mostly need to adjust their grocery budget and their expectations about imported goods. A newcomer moving to Rome will need to adjust their actual circadian rhythm. Hofstede Insights has not published an individual score for the Dominican Republic, but Italy's own profile is a reminder that cultural stereotypes and Hofstede dimensions don't always align: its indulgence score sits at a restrained 30, despite the country's reputation for leisurely, food-centered living.

The irony is which country gets the reputation. Say "Italian food culture" to most people abroad and they picture exactly the leisurely, multigenerational ritual Wanted in Rome describes — and they'd be right, it still mostly holds. Say "Caribbean food culture" and the brochure image is beachside and unhurried. The lived reality for a working professional in Santo Domingo is closer to a reheated lunch between meetings than a beachfront plate — the leisurely version is real, but it's a weekend version, not a Tuesday one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Florentine — Samantha Vaughn, an American who has lived in Italy for nearly eight years, described being teased at her office lunch table so consistently that her boss eventually nicknamed her "impermeabile" (waterproof) — her shrimp étouffée, lentil curry, and a fish stew made with coconut milk all drew the same verdict from colleagues: the peppers, specifically, were "impossible to digest."*

> *The Florentine — Vaughn also relayed a colleague's experience: a British coworker's cottage pie went over well with Italian locals, but her mother-in-law still insisted, "You have to eat this with bread" — a reminder that even approval comes with amendments.*

> *Expat.com forum (Dominican Republic) — A poster going by classygringo said plainly that "the biggest adjustment I had to make was the food," adding simply that they weren't a fan — a rare admission in a thread otherwise full of beach-and-weather enthusiasm.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Santo Domingo, the practical adjustment is mostly logistical: budget for imported goods, learn to love rice and beans on a weekday, and save your expectations of a long, unhurried meal for Saturday. If you're moving to Rome, the adjustment is closer to a full schedule rebuild — your workday, your dinner reservations, your sense of when a "quick bite" is even a culturally available option. Both countries use food to tell you something true about their priorities. The Dominican Republic is telling you it's still figuring out how to fit tradition around a modern workday. Italy is telling you, calmly and at length, that it has no intention of doing so.

Eat the lunch your hosts are eating, on their schedule, before you decide you know better. In both places, the food was never really the test. It's whether you're willing to sit still for it.
