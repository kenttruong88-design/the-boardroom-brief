# Flexible Hours, Rigid Minds: What India and the Philippines Actually Mean by "Remote Work"

🇮🇳 India · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Here is the paradox at the heart of this week's briefing: India has no law whatsoever governing flexible or remote work, and its employees fight for it like it's a constitutional right. The Philippines, by contrast, actually has a law addressing remote work, and its workers still spend a suspicious amount of time being summoned back to a cubicle in Bonifacio Global City. One country treats flexibility as an insurgency; the other treats it as a policy that occasionally gets overruled by a landlord's lease on office space. If you are relocating for either, the paperwork will tell you less than the group chat.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your hybrid schedule in writing before you sign, not in a slide deck | Don't assume "hybrid" survives the next town hall; several IT majors have reversed it mid-year |
| Budget for an inverter or UPS; power cuts do not pause video calls | Don't rent far from the office on the strength of a remote-work promise |
| Stay active in the office WhatsApp group even on remote days | Don't skip the informal 10am "chai check-in"; absence from small talk reads as absence from the team |
| Clarify whether appraisal cycles penalize remote staff before accepting an offer | Don't negotiate only on salary; ask about work-mode terms as a separate line item |
| Confirm your manager's actual meeting density, not the stated one | Don't expect flexible hours to mean fewer hours; Indian managers report longer, not shorter, days |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask if your remote status depends on a PEZA or IT-park tax incentive tied to onsite seats | Don't assume BGC or Makati rent and pay reflect the rest of the country |
| Get a backup prepaid data plan; home broadband outages are common and widely reported | Don't sign a BPO contract assuming permanent WFH; many retain a "hybrid if required" clause |
| Ask about night-differential pay if you'll cover US-hours shifts from home | Don't expect silence at home; household sizes are larger, and noise is the most cited WFH complaint |
| Confirm which specific hybrid tier applies to your team (onsite, on-demand, or fully remote) | Don't treat the national remote-work law as a guarantee; enforcement varies by employer |
| Check your building's generator or backup power situation before you lease | Don't plan your workday around flawless connectivity; build in a manual failover |

### India: The Permission Nobody Wrote Down

Indian professionals did not wait for legislation to decide they liked remote work. According to the Atlassian "Reworking Work" survey, 57% of Indian employees say they would prefer full-time remote arrangements, and 94% say they feel well prepared to work remotely, the highest readiness figure among the countries surveyed, ahead of the global average of 86%. That conviction has not been met with equal enthusiasm from the other side of the table. An Indeed survey covering roughly 1,200 job seekers and 561 employers found 59% of Indian employers oppose remote work after the pandemic, with opposition running higher among large firms (67%) and mid-size ones (70%) than their global counterparts (60% and 34%, respectively).

The standoff persists because, legally, there is nothing to stand on. Per the World Bank's Women, Business and the Law dataset, South Asia is the one region worldwide with no flexible-time or remote-work legislation at all on the books, which puts India's booming hybrid culture in the odd position of running entirely on vibes, HR memos, and whichever CEO tweeted something generous that quarter. A later Indeed survey, reported by CNBC, found 71% of Indian job seekers now rank flexibility above pay when job hunting, with 63% wanting hybrid arrangements that only 51% of employers actually offer. Everyone agrees remote work is popular. Almost nobody agrees on who gets to keep it.

There's a further irony worth noting: according to the ILO's 2021 report on digital labour platforms, India supplied roughly 20% of the world's home-based online "gig" work in 2019, more than any other country measured, worth an estimated $26 million of a $135 million pool. A country with zero remote-work legislation is nonetheless one of the planet's largest suppliers of informally home-based digital labour. Make of that what you will; the government has, so far, declined to.

### Philippines: The Policy That Outran the Pipes

The Philippines' relationship with remote work reads almost like a controlled experiment. BusinessWorld reported that productivity in the BPO sector cratered to 50% in the early 2020 lockdown, with only 40% of staff working from home and 10% onsite; by November that year, productivity had recovered to 95%, powered by a workforce that was now 70% remote. The industry body IBPAP, through Accenture Philippines, has since quantified the upside directly, reported by Malaya Business Insight: hybrid operations lifted productivity by 15% to 40%, cut absenteeism by roughly 40%, trimmed attrition by 10% to 15%, and let firms cut real-estate and resourcing costs by more than 20%. Those are not the numbers of an industry quietly tolerating remote work. Those are the numbers of an industry that has seen the future and keeps calling it a "short-term solution" anyway, per IBPAP's own characterization of the current emergency work-from-home authorization.

What makes this odder is that, unlike India, the Philippines actually has remote-work legislation on the books, per the World Bank's Women, Business and the Law dataset, which notes only a handful of East Asia-Pacific economies, the Philippines among them, cover remote work in law (though not flexible scheduling). On paper, Filipino workers have more legal standing to ask for remote arrangements than their Indian counterparts. In practice, the limiting factor isn't permission, it's plumbing. Cisco's Broadband Index, surveying 60,000 workers across 30 countries, found that 91% of Filipino respondents said internet services needed to "dramatically improve" to support hybrid work, with 83% reporting three or more household members online simultaneously and 78% calling affordable, reliable broadband a major problem. The law says you may work from home. The bandwidth has opinions of its own.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and you get a tidy inversion. India's obstacle to remote work is institutional: no law, skeptical employers, a corporate culture that, per Hofstede Insights' country comparisons, scores high on power distance, meaning hierarchy and visible presence still carry real weight even when the work itself travels fine over a laptop. The Philippines' obstacle is infrastructural: a comparatively hierarchical culture too, by the same Hofstede metrics, but one that has already granted remote work both a law and a glowing productivity case study, only to run into unreliable broadband and a BPO sector still deciding whether "temporary" authorization should become permanent policy.

The result is that an Indian employee's fight for flexibility is mostly a fight for trust, waged in group chats and exit interviews, while a Filipino employee's fight for flexibility is mostly a fight for throughput, waged against a modem. Neither country has actually solved remote work. India has decided it likes the idea and is negotiating the terms informally, one return-to-office memo at a time. The Philippines has written the idea into policy and discovered that policy doesn't fix a blackout.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/developersIndia — a Bengaluru-based engineer with over a decade of experience described his employer's new mandatory five-day office rule as a "massive step back," noting that moving his family back into the city would wipe out most of his savings despite the salary, after years of remote work with no productivity complaints.*

> *Instagram, via BusinessToday coverage — an Indian engineer working remotely for a UK firm for five years said the biggest benefit of his setup "isn't getting paid in pounds, it's the trust," describing a workplace with minimal meetings and managers who actively discourage after-hours messages, which he said he still misses the absence of in Indian corporate culture.*

> *Expat.com forum — a long-time remote worker in the Philippines recounted that a request to continue working remotely for a large US employer was denied outright, with the company citing a narrow internal definition of "remote," while other posters on the same thread flagged frequent power and internet interruptions as a recurring obstacle to reliable client calls.*

> *Reddit, r/digitalnomad — commenters warned a prospective arrival that Bonifacio Global City feels like a safe, air-conditioned American bubble, but is "walled off" from the rest of Manila, which they described as having heavier traffic, worse sidewalks, and a much higher cost of living than most of the country, advice they said they wished more newcomers received before signing a lease based on BGC alone.*

### Conclusion

If there is one difference that should actually change your packing list, it's this: in India, assume any remote-work arrangement is provisional and get it in writing, because the data shows employer sentiment is genuinely split and company policy has reversed mid-tenure for people who did nothing wrong. In the Philippines, assume the arrangement is legally plausible but technically fragile, and build your life around a backup data plan and a realistic view of what BGC-adjacent rent actually buys you outside BGC.

Neither country will hand you the flexible, borderless job the recruiter described. India will hand you a negotiation. The Philippines will hand you a law and an apology about the Wi-Fi. Pick the headache you'd rather manage, and for what it's worth, over drinks I'd tell you: bring your own router either way, and never, ever believe a remote-work offer that doesn't name a specific day count in the contract.
