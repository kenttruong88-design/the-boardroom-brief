# The Law Says Disconnect, The Badge Reader Says Otherwise: India and Portugal on Remote Work

**🇮🇳 India · 🇵🇹 Portugal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Portugal has a law that bars your boss from emailing you after hours and makes them help pay your home internet bill. India has employees averaging 4.4 office days a week — more than almost any comparable market — while 88% of Indian employers openly admit remote flexibility is what keeps people from quitting. One country legislated the answer. The other is still actively fighting about it, out loud, on Blind.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask specifically about in-office day requirements before accepting a role — they vary widely | Assume "hybrid" means the same number of office days at every company |
| Research a company's actual RTO track record, not just its stated policy | Trust a fully-remote offer without checking whether the policy has already reversed once |
| Expect flexibility to be treated as a retention tool, not a default right | Assume flexibility policies are stable — many have tightened significantly since 2023 |
| Watch for the gap between what leadership says publicly and what teams experience | Be surprised if "hybrid" quietly becomes "mostly in-office" within your first year |
| Check whether your specific function (tech, sales, ops) has different norms than the company average | Assume policy uniformity across a large company's global or even India-based offices |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know your rights under the 2021 "right to disconnect" law — it's real and enforceable | Expect employer contact outside working hours except in genuine emergencies |
| Ask whether your employer covers a share of home electricity and internet costs — it's a legal norm for remote staff | Assume digital nomad visa holders and formally remote-employed staff have identical protections |
| Understand the D8 visa's income threshold if you're relocating as a remote worker | Assume the "right to disconnect" applies the same way to foreign remote workers on all visa types |
| Expect these protections to be culturally reinforced, not just legally present | Assume a legally mandated boundary means every manager respects it in practice |
| Clarify from day one which specific remote-work rights apply to your exact employment structure | Treat the law as a guarantee that replaces the need to check your actual contract |

### India: Flexibility as Bargaining Chip, Not Policy

India's remote work story right now is a genuine tug-of-war, and both sides of it are well documented. On one hand, Forbes Advisor India and multiple workforce surveys report that 88% of employers see remote flexibility as central to retention, with 72% of remote workers and 68% of hybrid workers reporting real work-life balance improvements, and about 83% of women specifically wanting more flexibility — over 70% having quit or considered quitting jobs over inflexible policies. On the other hand, India is simultaneously leading the global return-to-office movement: employees there average 4.4 office days per week, well above the roughly two days per week common in the US and UK, with major employers including Wipro, Infosys, and TCS formally mandating three to five office days.

Blind threads capture the friction in real time — active, ongoing discussions with titles like "Return to office India" and "Why companies are not remote friendly in India," describing managers pushing employees back into buildings partly, some posters allege, to justify newly built or leased office space, and workers threatening to quit over reversed remote arrangements. Hofstede's data offers a partial explanation for why the tension resolves toward the office: India's collectivist orientation and its business culture's continued emphasis on in-person mentoring and visible presence mean flexibility functions less as an entitlement and more as a negotiated perk — real, valued, and precarious all at once.

### Portugal: Flexibility as Codified Right

Portugal took the opposite approach and simply wrote the answer into law. Since 2021, Portuguese labor law has prohibited employers from contacting remote staff outside working hours except in genuine emergencies, and — in a detail that regularly surprises newcomers — requires employers to contribute toward remote workers' home electricity and internet costs. RemoFirst and Global Citizen Solutions both confirm these aren't symbolic gestures: remote employees are entitled to these protections from day one, regardless of what an individual contract says.

The country has paired this legal foundation with genuine institutional openness to remote and location-independent work: the D8 digital nomad visa, introduced in 2022, offers a clear legal pathway for non-EU remote workers who meet an income threshold (four times Portugal's minimum wage, roughly €3,680 monthly as of 2026), with a track toward permanent residency after five years. Hofstede's uncertainty-avoidance score for Portugal sits extremely high at 104, and the codified, rules-based approach to remote work fits that pattern precisely — where India's flexibility gets negotiated informally and can shift with a single leadership decision, Portugal's gets locked into statute, reducing ambiguity even if it doesn't eliminate every manager's instinct to test the boundary anyway.

### The Reckoning

The uncomfortable truth for anyone comparing these two countries is that legal protection and lived experience don't automatically track together. Portugal has arguably the strongest remote-work legal framework in this comparison, and yet Quora threads about the "right to disconnect" reveal genuine, ongoing debate about whether the law is fully honored in fast-moving or foreign-managed workplaces. India has comparatively weak formal protection — flexibility exists at employer discretion, with no equivalent statutory backstop — and yet the sheer intensity of employee pushback, visible daily on Blind, is functioning as its own kind of informal enforcement mechanism, forcing companies to justify RTO mandates or risk visible attrition. Portugal legislated the floor. India is negotiating it in public, one Blind thread at a time.

> *Quora — Responding to a question about Portugal's right-to-disconnect law, someone pointed out the less-discussed provision requiring employers to cover part of a remote worker's home electricity and internet costs, arguing it did more to normalize remote work culturally than the disconnect clause itself.*

> *Reddit — A worker in an Indian tech firm described the specific frustration of a stated "hybrid" policy that technically allowed remote days but was enforced through visible manager disapproval of anyone who used them regularly.*

> *Quora — Someone weighing a move to Portugal for a remote US-based role asked pointed questions about whether the right-to-disconnect law would realistically apply to a foreign employer operating outside Portuguese jurisdiction, reflecting real uncertainty about how far the legal protection actually extends for non-traditional remote arrangements.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to India for a remote or hybrid role, treat the stated policy as a starting position, not a guarantee, and watch what similarly-titled employees are actually doing rather than what the offer letter says. If you're moving to Portugal, know your legal rights specifically — the electricity stipend surprises almost everyone — but don't assume the law fully protects you if your employer sits outside Portuguese jurisdiction. The honest version for a friend: India will make you fight for flexibility every year; Portugal already fought for you in 2021, but it's still worth checking whether anyone told your actual manager.
