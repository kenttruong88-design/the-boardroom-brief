**Dubai Will Hand You a Number and Walk Away. Sydney Will Now Tell You What Everyone Else Is Making.**

**🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

One of these countries just made it illegal for your employer to stop you comparing paychecks with the person next to you. The other has, by multiple long-term residents' own account, quietly moved from a place where you negotiated your package to a place where you're handed one and asked to sign. Both will pay you well by global standards. Only one of them will tell you, with a straight face, what "well" actually means relative to your colleagues.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the whole package — housing allowance, flight tickets, medical cover, school fees — not just the base number | Fixate on salary alone; for a family posting, schooling and housing can dwarf the base-pay difference between two offers |
| Benchmark against current, company-specific data before you talk numbers; general online salary figures run noticeably high | Quote a six-figure AED range you saw on a salary-aggregator site as your opening ask without checking if it reflects today's market |
| Accept that government and quasi-government roles have little to no negotiation room — pay scales are often set by regulation | Push hard on a public-sector offer expecting private-sector-style flexibility; it usually isn't there |
| Ask directly whether annual flights, gratuity, and medical insurance are included — some employers quietly skip them | Assume these benefits are automatic just because "everyone gets them" in the region; several long-term residents report going without |
| Remember the headline appeal — no personal income tax — when comparing a lower gross offer to a higher-taxed one elsewhere | Compare a UAE gross offer directly against a take-home figure elsewhere without running the actual net-of-tax math |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask colleagues what they earn if you want to — pay secrecy clauses in contracts are now banned nationally | Sign a new contract assuming you're legally barred from discussing your pay; that restriction can no longer be enforced |
| Use salary-band transparency where it exists (especially in larger, more structured employers) to anchor your ask | Lowball yourself out of politeness; Australian workplace culture increasingly expects you to know your market value |
| Factor superannuation contributions into any offer comparison, not just the headline salary | Compare a "total package" figure to a "base salary" figure from a different offer without checking what's actually bundled |
| Raise pay-gap concerns directly if you suspect bias — the legal and cultural tide now favors disclosure | Stay quiet about a suspected gap "to keep the peace"; recent reform was explicitly designed to make that silence unnecessary |
| Expect younger, more senior-level colleagues to already know internal salary bands informally | Assume pay information is uniformly locked down; in practice, how much gets shared still varies a lot by seniority and company |

The UAE's defining pitch has always been the number on the page: no personal income tax, and for decades, real room to negotiate it upward. Long-term expat voices increasingly describe that second part fading. On the expat.com forum, a Greek resident posting under the handle Aris M put it starkly: "The old time in UAE has passed when an employee can negotiate his salary," describing offers as increasingly take-it-or-leave-it. A Jordanian commenter with 44 years in the country, adnanahussain, drew a sharper line between sectors — government roles have essentially no negotiation room because pay is set by regulation, while private-company flexibility depends heavily on the firm's own finances and the specific candidate's leverage.

What hasn't changed is that "salary" in the UAE is really shorthand for a bundle. Multiple forum contributors flagged housing allowance, flight tickets home, medical cover, and children's school fees as the line items that actually determine whether an offer works for a family, since accommodation and schooling alone can consume most of a base salary in cities like Dubai. Not everyone gets the full bundle, either: one commenter, posting as Sabir123, reported receiving no annual ticket, gratuity, medical insurance, or leave salary at all — a reminder that "standard UAE expat package" is aspirational language, not a guarantee.

Australia just legislated its way to the opposite instinct. The "Secure Jobs, Better Pay" reforms banned pay secrecy clauses outright, with federal employment minister Tony Burke framing it plainly: "Banning them will improve transparency and reduce the risk of gender pay discrimination." The number driving the push was Australia's 22.8% gender pay gap, and the international evidence cited in support was notable — U.S. states without pay-secrecy protections show women's wages running 4 to 12% higher than in states that allow secrecy, and a Canadian transparency law was credited with narrowing the gender pay gap there by 20 to 40%, depending on how it's measured. Employers' long-standing defense, sometimes called the "jealousy hypothesis" — that openness breeds workplace conflict — gets fairly short shrift in the research: employees tend to accept pay differences they see as justified by contribution, and transparency is more often associated with higher satisfaction, not less.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real divergence: the UAE's negotiation culture is becoming more opaque right as its historical selling point — generous, flexible pay — gets harder to verify in real time, because so much of the compensation conversation still happens one candidate at a time, behind closed doors, informed mostly by aggregator sites several contributors flagged as overstating real market offers. Australia is moving in the opposite direction on principle, legislating the right to compare notes specifically because closed-door, one-at-a-time pay conversations were shown to entrench gaps along gender lines.

The counterintuitive twist: income tax, usually the headline difference between these two labor markets, barely matters to this particular comparison. The real gap is informational. A UAE candidate negotiating alone, using stale benchmark data, is structurally worse positioned than an Australian employee who can now legally ask a colleague what they make — even though the UAE candidate's take-home number, tax-free, might still be larger in absolute terms.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A Dubai-based Senior Product Manager candidate, posting as oonn83, said companies weren't matching the figures circulating online: "Careem is an exception willing to pay 35k [AED/month]," they noted, after finding most other offers clustered closer to 30k against online estimates nearer 50k for similar roles.*

> *expat.com forum (Middle East/UAE board) — Aris M, a Greek expat with long UAE tenure, offered the bluntest verdict on the shift away from negotiation: "The old time in UAE has passed when an employee can negotiate his salary," describing most current offers as effectively fixed.*

> *Fishbowl (professional community app) — One participant in a salary-transparency discussion described a direct, concrete payoff from candor: "I helped a coworker gain a 30k raise by us having a candid convo" after the colleague discovered she was paid significantly less than peers in the same role — exactly the scenario Australia's reform is designed to make easier to catch.*

> *expat.com forum (Middle East/UAE board) — Sabir123, an Indian expat in the UAE, pushed back on the assumption that the "full expat package" is standard: "Even I dnt recieve annual ticket gratuity medical insurance and leave salaries," a reminder that the generous-benefits narrative doesn't hold for every employer.*

> *Fishbowl (professional community app) — Another commenter described a more cautious middle ground that's common in markets without a legal transparency mandate: sharing their own pay "only when asked," comfortable with a figure they believe is fair even though they suspect it sits slightly below market — transparency as an opt-in practice rather than a right.*

### Conclusion

If negotiating leverage matters more to you than the headline number, read the actual mechanism each country gives you, not the reputation. The UAE still pays well and tax-free, but multiple long-term residents describe the negotiation itself as increasingly theater — a number handed down, not built up. Australia pays less dramatically but now backs you with a legal right to find out exactly where you stand against everyone else in the room.

What I'd tell a friend: in Dubai, negotiate the whole bundle on day one, because you likely won't get a second real chance at it. In Sydney, ask your colleague what they make — not because it's polite, but because, as of a few years ago, it's finally entirely your legal right to ask.
