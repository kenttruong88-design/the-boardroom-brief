# More Than Half of Nigeria Wants to Quit This Year. America Just Wants a Better Offer.

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Fifty-two percent of Nigerian professionals say they're considering quitting their job and leaving the country entirely within the year, according to a Phillips Consulting survey — not switching employers, switching continents. In the United States, median job tenure has merely slipped from 4.6 years to 4.1 over the past decade, and the biggest controversy on a popular workplace forum is whether a 1.9-year average at Google counts as job-hopping or just normal churn. Both countries have given up on the idea that loyalty gets rewarded. Only one of them is leaving the country over it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect ambition and a near-term exit plan to be openly discussed among colleagues, not hidden. | Assume a colleague's five-year plan includes staying at your company, or in the country. |
| Ask directly about benefits beyond salary — paid leave, healthcare, bonuses — since pay alone is widely seen as insufficient. | Assume a competitive salary alone will retain good people; it rarely does on its own. |
| Expect younger colleagues to prioritize peace of mind and flexibility over raw pay more than older generations do. | Dismiss a toxic-culture complaint as generational softness; it's consistently cited as a top reason people leave. |
| Treat an employee's stated plan to relocate abroad as a real possibility, not idle talk — survey data backs it up broadly. | Be surprised when a strong performer leaves for Canada, the UK, or the US with little warning. |
| Build genuine development paths into a role if you want to keep talent. | Assume loyalty will outlast a lack of visible career growth. |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to be asked, directly, about your last three job changes and the reasoning behind each. | Treat a candidate's multiple short stints as automatically disqualifying — fast-growing firms skew every average down. |
| Expect compensation negotiation to happen through outside offers more than through internal advocacy. | Expect loyalty alone to move your pay meaningfully; switching jobs is the more reliable lever. |
| Expect tenure expectations to vary sharply by company stage and sector — a two-year stint reads differently at a startup than at a utility. | Apply one tenure number to the whole economy; declines of over 40% in some industries sit next to real increases in others. |
| Ask pointed questions about management turnover and burnout reputation before joining a fast-growing team. | Assume a famous employer's self-reported retention numbers are vetted or adjusted for headcount growth. |
| Expect a counteroffer to be a normal, expected negotiating tool if you're seriously considering leaving. | Be shy about naming a competing offer in a pay conversation — it's a standard, accepted lever here. |

### Nigeria

The Phillips Consulting numbers are stark even by the standards of a global talent market: 52 percent of Nigerian professionals are weighing an exit within the year, with participation near 50 percent across finance, professional services, education, healthcare, and IT alike, and 88 percent of the would-be leavers under 40. More than half said they'd reconsider if the country itself changed — better pay, less economic uncertainty, more security — which makes this less a story about disloyal employees than about a labour market that's stopped being able to out-argue the exit.

A separate Jobberman survey of over 2,700 Nigerian workers found the same pattern from the inside: people cite insufficient benefits beyond base pay, toxic workplace culture, a lack of career growth, and a desire for genuine flexibility as their reasons for leaving current roles, with younger workers explicitly ranking peace of mind above money. Tolu Olusina, who left a Nigerian engineering and NGO career for a scholarship in London and eventually a climate-finance role in South Korea, put the trade-off in terms few recruiters would ever say out loud: "I feel like I did more 'real work' in Nigeria. But when I left Nigeria, real money started to come." Her own trajectory tracks the broader pattern almost exactly: a roughly 100 percent raise moving from a UK consultancy to the World Bank, and a further jump of about 300 percent by the time she reached a climate-finance role in South Korea, proof that for a Nigerian professional with the right credentials, mobility isn't a last resort, it's the primary career strategy, executed in stages across three continents rather than three employers. Ambition, in that framing, is simply measured in borders crossed rather than years logged at a single desk.

### United States

America's version of the same instinct is quieter and far better compensated. Median job tenure fell from 4.6 to 4.1 years between 2012 and 2022, per Bureau of Labor Statistics data, with the decline hitting women harder than men (down 17.4 percent against 8.5 percent) and some industries — motion pictures, furniture manufacturing, publishing — losing well over 40 percent of median tenure in a decade. A handful of sectors moved the other way entirely, with petroleum and mining tenures actually rising, a reminder that "job hopping" is really several different stories wearing one national average.

Inside the tech industry specifically, the debate is almost sport: a widely discussed Blind thread cited self-reported average tenures of 1.9 years at Google and 1.8 at Amazon, and the replies split cleanly between people blaming burnout and poor management, people pointing out the numbers are skewed by rapid hiring rather than real attrition, and people simply arguing there's no reason to tolerate a big company's downsides when a better offer is one recruiter call away. Nobody in the thread treated loyalty itself as a virtue worth defending on its own; the only live debate was whether the churn numbers were accurate, not whether churn was a problem. A handful of veterans pushed back with their own counterexamples, a former Microsoft employee who'd stayed five years, a Google commenter reporting the same, but even they were arguing about measurement, not about whether staying put was admirable in itself. Even the pushback, in other words, conceded the premise: nobody on either side treated a long tenure as something to aspire to for its own sake.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real divergence: both cultures have essentially concluded that loyalty doesn't pay, but Nigeria's version of that conclusion routes through an entire country's labour market failing to compete, while America's routes through a functioning one simply pricing switching costs correctly. A Nigerian professional weighing an exit is usually asking "is there a better life available to me at all," while an American engineer debating a Blind thread is asking "is 1.8 years a good enough story to tell my next interviewer" — both are mobility questions, but one has stakes the other doesn't.

What that means in practice is that "job loyalty" as a virtue has quietly died in both places, just for different reasons and at different price points. Nigeria's workforce isn't disloyal, it's priced out of loyalty by an economy that can't currently out-bid the alternative. America's workforce isn't disloyal either, it's simply operating inside a system that has made switching the financially rational default, a system efficient enough that even its own workers argue about the data rather than the ethics of the behavior. On Hofstede's framework, Nigeria scores relatively high on collectivism paired with notable uncertainty avoidance, which makes the scale of the Phillips Consulting numbers more striking, not less — leaving is a bigger social step there than the raw percentage implies, which is exactly why half the workforce doing it anyway is the real headline.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Zikoko (Nigerian personal-essay platform) — Tolu Olusina, tracing her path from Lagos to London to Washington to Seoul, described the actual mechanics of the American pay premium bluntly: "the US pays more. It's a capitalist society; they always want to know the value you bring" — advice that reframes mobility as a negotiation skill rather than a betrayal of anyone back home.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — One commenter on a tenure-debate thread offered the cultural misread newcomers to big tech most often make: a famous company's low self-reported average tenure usually reflects explosive recent hiring, not mass dissatisfaction, so "smells fishy and seems too low" was the correct instinct, not cynicism.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A former Facebook employee on the same thread gave the practical frustration side plainly: the culture was "a pressure cooker" that expected constant output even from strong performers, which several replies agreed was a more honest explanation for short stints than any abstract loyalty gap.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A former Microsoft employee pushed back on the whole premise, noting their own experience ran closer to five years on average and that a Google-side commenter reported the same — a reminder that national tenure debates often collapse the moment someone actually names their own team.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the US to Nigeria, recalibrate what "ambitious" looks like in a colleague — openly planning an exit to another country within the year isn't disloyalty here, it's the single most common career strategy in the room, and treating it as a betrayal will just make you look naive. If you're moving from Nigeria to the US, recalibrate the stakes in the other direction — nobody is leaving because the country failed them; they're leaving because the spreadsheet said a different employer pays better, which is a much smaller, much safer kind of leaving than the one you just did.

Loyalty, it turns out, isn't dead in either place. It just moved jobs, and in one of the two countries, it occasionally moved continents to do it.
