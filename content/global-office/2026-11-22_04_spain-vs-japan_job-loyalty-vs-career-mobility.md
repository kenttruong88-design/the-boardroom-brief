# In Japan They Stay for Twelve Years; in Spain They Quit by the Million, With Better Paperwork

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Japan's regular private-sector employees have been with their current employer for an average of 12.4 years, according to the 2024 Basic Survey on Wage Structure of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. In Spain, Social Security data reported by La Voz de Lanzarote counted 2.88 million voluntary resignations in the first eleven months of 2025 alone. One of these countries is famous for lifetime employment and is quietly dismantling it. The other is famous for precarious contracts and has, since a 2021 reform, started handing out permanent ones, which employees then promptly resign from. Loyalty, it turns out, is easier to describe than to measure.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your contract is indefinido, temporary or fijo discontinuo, since the type determines your severance and your stability | Assume that a permanent contract and a stable contract are the same thing |
| Learn the dismissal rules: Eurofound lists 33 days' pay per year of service, up to 24 months, for unfair dismissal on an open-ended contract | Sign anything on dismissal without checking the entitlement |
| Read the probation clause carefully, because failed probations are rising | Treat the first months of a new contract as a formality |
| Budget time for recognition of your qualifications if your profession is regulated | Assume your licence arrives at the same speed as your furniture |
| Build relationships in person, as promotion is said to reward being personable | Expect a flawless spreadsheet to speak for you |
| Resign openly when a better offer arrives, since many permanent employees do | Treat resignation as a social disgrace, as the record figures show it is not |

### In Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay roughly three years at a traditional employer before moving | Stack stints shorter than a year, especially consecutively |
| Ask in writing which criteria determine your grade and what the next grade requires | Assume the base salary tracks your performance |
| Prepare a positive account of every move, since you will be asked in every interview | Explain a departure by criticising your former manager |
| Give at least two weeks' notice and leave on good terms | Treat departure paperwork as something you will not need |
| Line up the next job before resigning | Resign into a gap, as time in Japan without work is limited |
| Consider foreign-owned firms and start-ups if your résumé already shows moves | Expect a resignation agency to negotiate leave or overtime for you |

## Spain

Spain's labour market spent decades defined by the temporary contract. CaixaBank Research reports that the temporary employment rate among Social Security affiliates in the general regime fell to 12.7 percent at the end of 2024, from an average of 29.7 percent in 2014 to 2019, which it attributes to the December 2021 labour reform. Its September 2025 analysis adds that 41.2 percent of contracts signed since January 2022 have been permanent, against 9.0 percent in the earlier period. A related category, the fijo discontinuo contract, now accounts for 21.2 percent of departures from registered employment, up from 3.5 percent, a reminder that the reform changed the label on instability before it changed the instability.

The consequence is a labour market in which leaving is no longer a tragedy. According to the same CaixaBank analysis, voluntary departures nearly doubled to over 141,000, or 12.6 percent of all departures, and about three in four resignations in 2025 came from permanent contracts, where before the pandemic 63 percent came from temporary ones. The La Voz de Lanzarote report cites the same direction of travel, and notes that 76 percent of the 2025 resignations came from workers on permanent contracts. Protections explain part of the confidence: Eurofound lists 33 days' pay per year, up to 24 months, for an unfair dismissal on an open-ended contract.

The rules still distinguish sharply between kinds of exit. Eurofound's country profile notes that severance for the end of a temporary contract rose from 8 days' pay per year in 2010 to 12 days from 2015, against 20 days per year, capped at 12 months, for a fair individual dismissal. The arithmetic explains why employers prefer to hire flexibly and why employees prefer to hold the better contract. It also explains why the reform mattered: it moved the argument from whether a contract ends to what it costs when it does.

None of this makes Spain a meritocratic job market. The Local Spain, summarising HSBC Expat Explorer, reported that Spain ranked last of 33 countries for career progression, and that 29 percent of expats in an InterNations survey were disappointed with their career prospects, against 24 percent globally. A Barcelona economics professor quoted there says Spanish firms do not promote on merit. The conclusion for the newcomer is that mobility is real, but it tends to be sideways, and the usual way up is through someone who likes you.

## Japan

Japan's lifetime employment is now closer to a long-running rumour. The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Labour Force Survey, as reported by Nikkei, found that about 990,000 regular employees changed jobs in 2024, the highest since comparable statistics began in 2012, and 62 percent more than a decade earlier. Nippon.com, citing the same survey, puts the total of workers changing jobs in 2024 at 3.3 million, with ten million wishing to. The Mynavi mid-career recruitment survey of 1,600 personnel managers, also reported by Nippon.com, found that 54 percent of companies plan to be more proactive in mid-career hiring and only 7.4 percent less so.

The old structure has not vanished. A tenure of 12.4 years is long by most standards, and the JILPT databook shows the figure rising to 19.8 years among those aged 55 to 64. Seniority-based pay, the nenko joretsu system, still shapes base salary in traditional firms, according to a HackerNoon explainer by a former engineer at a Japanese automaker, with performance reviews affecting bonuses rather than the underlying curve. Leaving also carries a social cost that Japan has monetised: Nippon.com reports a Mynavi survey of 800 people in which 40.7 percent said their employer tried, or would have tried, to stop them from quitting, which is the market that resignation agencies serve.

Foreigners experience the system at close range. A Singaporean who moved to Tokyo in 2016 on an internal transfer, writing for the Singapore Global Network, describes a first employer where he stayed past midnight preparing documents for nine o'clock meetings and met a hierarchy-driven culture, which he came to regard as a boot camp. He moved on to a foreign-owned firm where he felt valued for his skills rather than for fitting the existing system. That trajectory, tolerate, learn, then leave, is increasingly common.

For foreigners, the practical picture is more flexible than the cultural one. Japan Dev, a recruitment site, advises that about three years is the safe minimum at a traditional company, that two to three years is usually acceptable in tech, start-ups and foreign firms, and that traditional banking and auditing are less forgiving. The advice is one site's experience rather than a rule, but it matches the Mynavi finding that employers are now hiring the very people they once suspected.

## The Reckoning

The head-to-head is awkwardly symmetrical. Japan has high tenure and rising mobility; Spain has rising stability and high mobility. A Japanese worker who changes jobs is a statistic that Nikkei calls a likely positive for growth, and one who stays is the median. A Spanish worker who resigns from a permanent contract is increasingly the norm, and one who stays is, by CaixaBank's figures, doing so on a contract that is more likely than before to survive. Hofstede Insights classes both societies as uncertainty-avoiding, and the paradox is that each solves the problem differently: Japan by embedding the employee in the firm, Spain by embedding the protection in the contract.

What distinguishes them is the price of leaving. In Spain it is mostly financial and bureaucratic: severance rules, probation clauses, credential recognition. In Japan it is mostly social: an employer who resists, a recruiter who counts your résumé, and a seniority curve that rewards remaining. For the expatriate, the Spanish system protects you while you decide, and the Japanese system pays you handsomely, in some sectors, for deciding quickly. Neither will do the deciding on your behalf.

> *DEV Community — a software engineer in Japan changed jobs several times with significant pay increases each time, says you can change jobs once a year and be fine in tech, and describes pressure to quit and non-renewal of contracts as the way firing really works there*

## Conclusion

If your priority is a career that moves fast, Japan in 2026 is more permissive than its reputation and Spain is more permissive than its pay scales. Tech workers and those at foreign-owned firms in Japan can switch every few years and be paid for it, provided they can explain each move. In Spain, you can resign freely and rely on a stronger contract framework, but you should plan on a slower road to promotion, a longer wait if your profession is regulated, and a workplace where relationships matter more than a polished résumé.

For a newcomer, the rule is to check which system you are in before you decide whether to leave it. Staying put is a strategy in both countries, merely one with different excuses.
