**Equal on Paper: Gender at Work in the Netherlands and the UAE**

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇦🇪 UAE**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The Netherlands has one of the most gender-equal legal frameworks in Europe and one of the most gender-unequal outcomes in hours worked, because Dutch women have quietly opted into part-time work at a rate nearly three times the OECD average. The UAE has neither the Netherlands' feminist branding nor its history, and yet female labor force participation there now exceeds the global average, women dominate government-sector employment, and mandatory board quotas outdate several supposedly more progressive economies. Assumptions about which country is "ahead" on gender should be held loosely.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect part-time work (four days a week is common) to be culturally normal, not a career penalty | Assume a colleague working part-time is less ambitious or less senior |
| Negotiate hours explicitly when you're hired — it shapes your whole trajectory | Expect frequent full-time hours to be the unstated default for career-track roles |
| Ask direct questions about parental leave and childcare norms before you need them | Assume gender equality in law translates automatically into equal outcomes at work |
| Notice that management tracks skew male even in famously egalitarian workplaces | Read a colleague's part-time schedule as evidence the company undervalues them |
| Push for full-time hours early if that's your goal — the "trap" compounds over years | Wait for your employer to proactively offer you more hours or a leadership track |

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect strong female representation in education, government, and STEM-adjacent roles | Assume workplace norms mirror conservative social stereotypes about the region |
| Know that equal-pay-for-equal-work is a legal requirement, not just an aspiration | Ignore that experiences vary sharply between free zones, government, and private sector |
| Look to networks like the Dubai Women Establishment for mentorship and support | Assume all industries and companies enforce gender equity policy equally consistently |
| Ask about board and leadership quotas at your specific employer — they vary by sector | Overlook that a meaningful minority of expat women report workplace sexism directly |
| Take confidence and directness as functional workplace currency, regardless of gender | Assume expat and Emirati women have identical workplace experiences — they often don't |

The Netherlands built one of the world's most generous part-time labor markets, and Dutch women walked through the door it opened — arguably too far through it. Nearly 60 percent of women in the Dutch labor market work part-time, according to OECD research, compared with roughly a fifth of women across the OECD on average, and Dutch women's average paid hours sit around 27 a week against men's 37. The OECD calls this the "part-time trap": a self-reinforcing system where part-time work is genuinely well-supported and non-stigmatized day to day, yet accumulates into a large annual earnings gap (32 percent versus a 10.5 percent hourly gap), a stalled pipeline into management, and one of the largest gender pension gaps in the OECD, with women's retirement income averaging 45 percent lower than men's. Hofstede's framework has the Netherlands scoring 14 on masculinity — among the most "feminine" cultures measured, prioritizing quality of life and work-life balance over competitive achievement — and the part-time trap is arguably that value system's least examined side effect.

The UAE's picture runs almost opposite to its reputation. Female labor force participation reached 54.1 percent in 2024, above the global average, and women now represent close to two-thirds of the government-sector workforce and 45 percent of the country's skilled labor. Emirati women are, on average, more educated than Emirati men — 43 percent hold a bachelor's degree versus 23 percent of men — and federal policy has required listed companies to include women on boards since 2012. None of this erases the UAE's high Hofstede score on power distance and masculinity, which still shapes a workplace culture that rewards visible assertiveness and hierarchy regardless of who's exhibiting it. But the data complicates any assumption that Gulf conservatism maps cleanly onto Gulf workplaces.

What both countries share, oddly, is a gap between formal equality and lived experience that surprises newcomers in opposite directions. In the Netherlands, the surprise is how normalized part-time work is and how quietly it caps careers. In the UAE, the surprise is how present and credentialed women are in the workforce despite the country's external reputation — tempered by real, documented experiences of workplace sexism reported by a meaningful minority of expat women, alongside a majority who report none at all.

**The Reckoning.** The paradox worth sitting with: the Netherlands, which scores as one of the most gender-egalitarian cultures in the world on paper and in law, produces some of the worst structural gender outcomes in earnings, management representation, and pension income among wealthy nations — a self-inflicted trap built from generous, well-intentioned labor flexibility. The UAE, which carries a global reputation for gender conservatism, has engineered specific, top-down interventions — mandatory board quotas, targeted female-education investment, government-sector hiring — that have measurably outpaced the Netherlands on raw labor force participation and management representation in less than two decades. Neither system should be graded on reputation. Both deserve to be graded on what actually happens to a woman's paycheck and title by year ten.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — A thread comparing Dubai and Netherlands job offers found posters generally rating Dutch roles higher on long-term career development and quality of life, but flagging the Netherlands' higher tax burden and, separately, describing career advancement to senior leadership as unexpectedly difficult for professionals outside the traditional Dutch network, regardless of gender.*

> *Quora — Someone answering why 75 percent of Dutch women work part-time explained it as a deliberate lifestyle choice tied to valuing family time over income, but noted the flip side rarely discussed abroad: employers offer little incentive for women who want more hours to get them, and management pipelines have stagnated as a result.*

> *Gulf News (UAE workplace survey reporting) — A survey of expat women in the UAE found 29 percent reporting direct experience of workplace sexism and 43 percent knowing a friend who had, a notably higher rate than respondents recalled experiencing in their home countries — a detail that complicates the region's frequent "best place for career women" branding.*

> *Gulf News (UAE workplace survey reporting) — The same body of reporting found a clear majority, 63 percent, of expat women in the UAE saying they'd faced no workplace sexism at all, and roughly three in four believing job offers were based on qualifications rather than gender — two findings that sit uneasily but simultaneously true alongside the sexism figures above.*

> *Quora — A response comparing European and Middle Eastern work cultures noted that Dutch offices feel unusually low-stress and non-hierarchical day to day, which the poster attributed partly to flexible hours culture — but added that the same flexibility, taken up disproportionately by women, quietly does the work of a glass ceiling without anyone having to build one on purpose.*

### Conclusion

If you're a woman moving to the Netherlands for work, negotiate your hours as carefully as your salary, because the part-time default is comfortable enough to slide into and hard enough to climb back out of. If you're moving to the UAE, look past the country's external reputation and toward your specific employer and sector — the structural support for women's careers is real and measurable, but it isn't evenly distributed, and your actual experience will depend heavily on where you land.

The version I'd tell a friend: the Netherlands will hand you a four-day week and call it enlightened; ask what it costs you by year ten. The UAE will hand you a title and a board seat quota; ask who's actually in the room when the real decisions get made.
