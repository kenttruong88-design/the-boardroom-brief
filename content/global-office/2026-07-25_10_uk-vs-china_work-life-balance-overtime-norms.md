**Britain Legislated a 36-Hour Week and Then Quietly Worked Through It Anyway; China Legislated Against the 72-Hour Week and Is Only Halfway There**

**🇬🇧 UK · 🇨🇳 China**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Full-time UK workers officially average 36.7 hours a week, comfortably inside a civilized-sounding number — and yet 93% of UK workers say they work outside normal hours, 55% of them every single day, and 43% regularly work unpaid overtime. China's legal cap sits at 44 hours a week, with real average hours running closer to 48.5 — a country still working measurably more than its own law allows. On paper, Britain looks like the clear winner. In practice, both countries have built a gap between the number on the contract and the number in real life; they've just built it in opposite directions.

### UK: A Legal Number That the Culture Quietly Ignores

Britain's formal work-life balance framework looks solid on paper: a 36.7-hour average full-time week and a legal minimum of 5.6 weeks paid annual leave, according to [StandOut CV's 2026 data](https://standout-cv.com/stats/work-life-balance-statistics-uk). But the lived reality diverges sharply from the contracted one. Ninety-three percent of UK workers report working outside normal hours, with 55% doing so daily, and 43% regularly working unpaid overtime — a genuinely "always-on" workforce despite formal hours that look modest by global standards, per [MyPerfectCV's Always-On Workforce Report](https://www.myperfectcv.co.uk/blog/always-on-workforce-report).

The disconnect is driven less by explicit policy than by ambient workplace expectation — 88% of UK workers say their employer claims to respect their right to disconnect, and yet 92% report genuinely struggling to switch off, a European-topping figure well above the 78% continental average. Finance and law remain the sectors where visible long hours function as an unofficial loyalty signal, per the same reporting, while the UK sits a middling 30th out of 41 countries on the OECD Better Life Index's work-life balance measure — a country whose formal labor protections have not translated into a culture that fully honors them.

### China: A Legal Number the Culture Is Still Catching Up To

China's story runs in the reverse direction: strong recent legal action against overwork, with the actual culture lagging behind the ruling rather than ahead of it. The Supreme People's Court's 2021 decision explicitly declared the 996 schedule — 9am to 9pm, six days a week — a labor law violation, capping the legal workweek at 44 hours, per [MSAdvisory](https://msadvisory.com/working-hours-china/). Enforcement has intensified meaningfully through 2025 and 2026, with major firms like Midea and DJI adopting earlier, publicly visible clock-out times as compliance signals.

Even so, real average hours in China remain around 48.5 a week — above the legal cap — with workarounds like "big week, small week" rotations persisting at firms unwilling to fully abandon long-hours culture. The generational undercurrent is significant: China's "lying flat" movement and organized pushback campaigns against grinding overtime reflect an active, contested cultural negotiation happening in real time, not a settled resolution — the law changed abruptly in 2021, and the workplace norm it targeted is still, gradually, being renegotiated beneath it.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely striking parallel is that both countries have a real gap between contracted hours and lived hours — Britain's gap is driven by informal expectation despite reasonable formal limits, while China's gap is driven by residual cultural habit despite an unusually forceful legal intervention. A UK worker's overtime is largely unpaid and quietly normalized; a Chinese worker's overtime is, at least on paper, illegal and increasingly the subject of public corporate compliance theater. Neither gap has closed, but they're being pressured from different directions — Britain would need cultural change to catch up to its already-generous formal protections, while China would need cultural change to catch up to its already-strict formal law.

Hofstede Insights scores the UK dramatically higher than China on individualism, and it shows up here in an ironic way: British overwork is framed as an individual failure to set boundaries — your problem to manage — while Chinese overwork, even amid reform, is still substantially framed as a collective obligation to the company or team, which is precisely why top-down legal intervention has had more visible (if incomplete) effect in China than the UK's more individualized "right to disconnect" framing has managed so far.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved from Shanghai to London for a finance role described genuine culture shock at how normal it was to leave the office at a reasonable hour, but was equally surprised to find colleagues still quietly checking email well into the evening from home — the visible hours had changed more than the actual workload.*

> *TeamBlind — A professional comparing European and Chinese startup cultures noted that "work-life balance" claims at UK firms often didn't survive contact with an actual busy quarter, with unpaid extra hours becoming expected in practice even at companies that advertised flexible, boundaried cultures during hiring.*

> *Quora — A Chinese respondent addressing why overtime persists despite the 996 ban wrote that many employees still fear appearing less committed than colleagues who stay late, meaning the legal change removed the requirement but not yet the peer pressure that originally sustained it.*

> *TeamBlind — Someone who'd worked at both a London and a Shenzhen-based tech company said the Shenzhen office's hours had visibly shortened in the past two years, but described the culture as now split — some teams genuinely left by 7pm, others quietly continued near-996 patterns under new, softer names.*

> *Quora — A Chinese worker who'd spent time in the UK described being envious of European colleagues leaving on time every day, but noted that when the same colleagues discussed their actual workload later, it became clear the hours had simply moved home and gone unlogged rather than disappearing.*

### Conclusion

Moving to the UK means treating the "right to disconnect" as something you'll likely have to enforce yourself, since the formal protection is stronger than the ambient culture around it. Moving to China means the opposite bet: the law is now unusually strict on your side, but the office culture around you may take years to fully catch up to it, so ask real questions before you rely on the ruling alone. The uncomfortable truth, over a drink, is that neither country's actual work-life balance matches its official one — you're better off asking a future colleague what time they really log off than reading either country's labor code.
