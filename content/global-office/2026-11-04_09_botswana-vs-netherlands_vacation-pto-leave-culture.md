# The Fifteen-Day Problem: What Botswana and the Netherlands Actually Mean by "Time Off"

🇧🇼 Botswana · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Botswana, the law gives you fifteen days of annual leave and then, helpfully, a legal mechanism to sell a third of them back to your employer instead of using them. In the Netherlands, the law gives you twenty days, most companies hand over closer to twenty-eight, and roughly four in ten Dutch employees still finish the year with some unused ([IamExpat.nl](https://www.iamexpat.nl/)). Neither country, it turns out, has actually solved the problem of getting its citizens to rest. They have simply built two very different bureaucracies around failing to do so — one that pays you for the rest you skip, and one that legislates an expiry date on the rest you were supposed to want. If you are weighing a move for work, the entitlement line on the offer letter is the least informative number in the whole document; what it costs you to actually use those days is the real figure, and almost nobody puts that one in writing.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇼 Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm which public holidays your sector actually observes — mining employees get three, everyone else gets eight ([WageIndicator](https://wageindicator.org/en-bw/work-in-botswana/labour-law/annual-leave-and-holidays)) | Don't assume all eight national holidays apply to you; read your sector's leave schedule first |
| Take at least eight of your fifteen days within six months of accrual, as the Employment Act requires | Don't let leave balances sit for more than three years — the backlog gets forcibly cleared |
| Put leave-scheduling disputes in writing early | Don't expect an automatic cash payout if your employer simply "forgets" to schedule your leave |
| Build the relationship with your supervisor before you need the favor | Don't submit a leave request cold and expect a fast yes in a hierarchy-first workplace |
| Ask about the new partial-encashment rule if scheduling genuinely fails twice | Don't rely on encashment as a routine income top-up — it caps at 35% of leave value ([Business Weekly Botswana](https://businessweekly.co.bw/columns/hr-employment/employment-labour-relations-act-leave-encashment-now-recognised-by-the-law)) |
| Budget leave around extended-family obligations, which are a real and expected draw on it | Don't assume your fifteen days map cleanly onto a beach holiday calendar |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your statutory days before July 1 of the following year | Don't assume unused leave carries forever — it legally expires ([Business.gov.nl](https://business.gov.nl/staff/terms-of-employment/holiday-entitlement-for-employees/)) |
| Expect a holiday allowance (vakantiegeld) of roughly 8% of salary, usually paid in May | Don't mistake it for a bonus — it's earmarked, structurally, for the trip you're supposed to take |
| Block real calendar time and tell colleagues in advance | Don't keep answering Slack on holiday out of habit — over a third of Dutch workers admit doing exactly that ([IamExpat.nl](https://www.iamexpat.nl/career/employment-news/workers-netherlands-need-18-days-off-year-recharge-study-finds)) |
| Plan around the school-holiday calendar if colleagues have children | Don't expect flexible scheduling during peak summer weeks |
| Ask your contract's exact day count — company offers range widely above the legal floor | Don't assume the 20-day legal minimum is what you'll actually get; the real average is closer to 27.5 |
| Treat rule-bound leave as the norm | Don't import an "unlimited PTO, use your judgment" mindset — it isn't how the system is built |

Botswana's Employment Act sets annual leave at fifteen working days a year, accruing at 1.25 days a month, with no increase for seniority ([WageIndicator](https://wageindicator.org/en-bw/work-in-botswana/labour-law/annual-leave-and-holidays)). Workers also get eight paid public holidays — New Year's Day, Good Friday, Easter Monday, 1 May, President's Day and the day after, Botswana Day, and Christmas — unless they work in mining, where only three of those count, a carve-out that quietly tells you which industry wrote the exception into the rulebook. At least eight of the fifteen days must be taken within six months of the year they were earned, and anything left unclaimed can roll over for up to three years before the system forces a reckoning: at that point, the entire backlog plus the latest year's accrual must be cleared at once, whether the calendar or the workload cooperates. It is a structure built for a workforce assumed to need prompting, not restraint, and a three-year grace period that functions less like generosity than like a loan coming due.

That assumption got a legislative update in 2026. Previously, employers only had to pay out accrued leave when a contract ended; during active employment, practice simply varied — some firms voluntarily cashed out unused days, others ran a strict use-it-or-lose-it line and let the clock run out on workers who couldn't get leave approved. Under the amended Employment and Labour Relations Act, if a worker and employer can't agree on leave timing after two failed scheduling attempts, the employee can now claim 35% of the leave's cash value while still employed — a formal "plan B" for leave that keeps getting deferred by operational demand ([Business Weekly Botswana](https://businessweekly.co.bw/columns/hr-employment/employment-labour-relations-act-leave-encashment-now-recognised-by-the-law)). The law frames this as a safety valve. It also, more quietly, concedes that a meaningful share of Botswana's workforce was never actually taking its leave in the first place — a pattern echoed informally by expats describing a workplace culture where getting anything scheduled, leave included, runs on relationships, patience, and persistence as much as on policy ([Expat.com Botswana forum](https://www.expat.com/en/forum/africa/botswana/578231-in-retrospect-would-you-move-again-to-botswana.html); [DigitalNomadExchange.com](https://digitalnomadexchange.com/gd/12/23/Botswana/Digital-Nomad-Botswana)).

The Netherlands, by contrast, has over-engineered the opposite problem. The statutory floor is four times your weekly working days — twenty for a standard five-day week — and most employers clear that easily, with Loket.nl payroll data putting the real national average at 27.5 days once collective agreements are folded in, ranging from roughly thirty days in the metal industry down to noticeably fewer in construction and retail ([Business.gov.nl](https://business.gov.nl/staff/terms-of-employment/holiday-entitlement-for-employees/); IamExpat.nl). Even generational habits diverge: Baby Boomers average 29.9 days and leave 2.3 unused, Gen Z workers average 26.6 and leave 1.7 unused — a workforce that, whatever else it disagrees on, has broadly inherited the same mild reluctance to actually book off the last few days. Employees also receive a mandatory holiday allowance, vakantiegeld, worth at least 8% of annual salary and usually paid out every May — a structural nudge toward actually booking the trip, since statutory days cannot legally be cashed out while you're still employed, and even generous extra-statutory days only roll over for up to five years before they, too, vanish. And yet an SD Worx survey found Dutch workers believe they need eighteen days just to recharge, one more than the European average, while 45% book the identical weeks off every year and 37% keep working anyway while supposedly on holiday ([IamExpat.nl](https://www.iamexpat.nl/career/employment-news/workers-netherlands-need-18-days-off-year-recharge-study-finds)).

That contradiction shows up in the Gallup numbers. Dutch workplace engagement sits at roughly 14%, slightly above the European average of 12% but well below the global figure of 20%; wellbeing, by contrast, is unusually high, with 68% of Dutch employees describing themselves as thriving overall, against 49% regionally and 34% globally ([Gallup, State of the Global Workplace](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/706490/state-global-workplace-netherlands-country-level-data.aspx)). Translated: the Dutch are not particularly engaged by their jobs, but their jobs are not ruining their lives, which is a more modest and perhaps more honest ambition than most workplace cultures admit to having. It is also, notably, an ambition the leave system seems to support more than the office culture does — the days are there whether or not anyone is especially enthusiastic about clocking in to earn them.

**The Reckoning:** put the two systems side by side and the irony sharpens. Botswana's leave regime is stingy on paper and increasingly willing to let workers cash out rest for money — a system that, however reluctantly, trusts people to decide for themselves what they need. The Dutch regime is generous on paper, legally hostile to cashing leave out at all, and still can't stop more than a third of its workforce from quietly working through the holiday it mandated. One country under-provides and lets you monetize the shortfall; the other over-provides and polices how you're allowed to spend it.

Neither failure is really about the number of days. It's about what each culture assumes a day off is for. Botswana's framework treats leave as a debt the employer eventually has to settle, in time or in cash, in a labor market where expats and locals alike describe advancement as running on relationships more than process. The Dutch framework treats leave as a right so sacred it legislates an expiry date on neglecting it — and then watches nearly half its workforce book the same fixed weeks, every year, like clockwork, because true spontaneity turns out to be its own kind of effort. The stress numbers back this up obliquely: just 30% of Dutch employees report high stress on a given day, against a European average of 39%, which suggests the rule-bound rigidity is, perversely, doing its job even when the leave itself goes half-used.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/antiwork — a Dutch commenter breaking down an actual annual total: 32 standard days, 8 for medical leave, and 12 more from a collective bargaining agreement, landing at 52 paid days off a year — well above anything the statutory minimum implies.*

> *Blind — a tech employee comparing offers across EU offices concludes the Netherlands sits alongside Germany and France in a cluster of roughly 30 PTO days plus public holidays, a tier apart from US "unlimited" policies that colleagues describe as unlimited in name only.*

> *DigitalNomadExchange.com — an expat in Botswana notes that the local concept of time is "more flexible" than what newcomers are used to, and warns that getting anything bureaucratic processed, leave requests included, can be slow regardless of what the Employment Act technically promises.*

> *Expat.com (Botswana forum) — a long-term UK expat describes hiring and workplace advancement as running on a "who you know" culture, with his Motswana partner unable to find anything beyond freelance work without full employee benefits after returning home.*

> *r/AmerExit — a US-to-Netherlands mover reflects that life before relocating was "a bunch of stress," crediting the change less to any single policy than to a cumulative sense that time off is structurally protected rather than negotiated case by case.*

### Conclusion

If the brochure for either country promises you "excellent work-life balance," read the fine print as a function, not a feature: Botswana's fifteen days plus a cash-out clause for a workforce the law assumes won't get to use them, versus the Netherlands' near-thirty days plus an expiry clause for a workforce that, survey after survey shows, has trouble actually stopping. Hofstede Insights' broader cultural data frames part of why — the Netherlands scores among the most individualist and indulgence-oriented societies measured, the kind of profile that predicts people booking their own holiday and guarding it fiercely, while Botswana doesn't even appear as a standalone entry in Hofstede's database, a small, telling fact about whose working lives get modeled by the instruments everyone cites.

Choose Botswana if you want a leave system honest enough to let you take the money instead of the rest, and a workplace where who vouches for you still counts for something. Choose the Netherlands if you want a system generous enough to guilt you into resting whether you want to or not, with a payout every May to make sure you can afford to. Either way, pack a book you never quite finish reading — some things, it turns out, really are universal.
