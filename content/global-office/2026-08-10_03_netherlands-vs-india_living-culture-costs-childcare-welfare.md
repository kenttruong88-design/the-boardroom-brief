# Bare Walls, No Curtains, €385 Excess: Netherlands vs. India on the Cost of Living Well

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇮🇳 India**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Move from Bangalore to Amsterdam and your salary might triple; move into your new Dutch apartment and you may find it has no floor, no light fixtures, and no curtains, because Dutch landlords rent "kaal" — bare — and expect you to supply a home from scratch. Move the other direction, from Amsterdam to Mumbai, and the math inverts just as sharply: your rent drops by roughly two-thirds, but so does the safety net underneath you, and "welfare system" stops being a phrase you can rely on and starts being a phrase you negotiate into your own employment contract.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for mandatory health insurance (zorgverzekering) within four months of arrival | Miss the deadline — fines run up to €1,584 before the government forcibly enrolls you in a pricier plan |
| Budget €1,150–€2,300+ a month for rent depending on city and bedroom count | Assume an apartment comes furnished, or even lit — "kaal" rentals often lack floors and light fittings |
| Register with your local gemeente (municipality) immediately — it unlocks nearly everything else, from insurance to a bank account | Delay registering for a BSN (citizen service number) — almost nothing administrative works without it |
| Ask HR about the 30% tax ruling if you're a skilled migrant — it meaningfully changes take-home pay | Assume take-home pay maps cleanly to gross salary; Dutch taxes are high and the deductions are numerous |
| Look into UWV (unemployment benefit office) as a real safety net if between jobs | Assume the welfare system moves fast — Dutch bureaucracy is thorough, not quick |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate private health insurance directly into your compensation — expect $40–$90/month baseline for decent private coverage | Assume public healthcare will meet expat expectations; most professionals and expats use private hospitals |
| Compare cities carefully — a 1BR in Mumbai can run 3-4x the same size in Kochi or Jaipur | Assume "cost of living is low" applies evenly; Mumbai and Bangalore rents have climbed sharply in recent years |
| Budget separately, and significantly, for household help — cooks, cleaners, drivers — a near-default for professional households | Expect a government safety net comparable to Northern Europe; India's welfare system is thinner and more fragmented |
| Ask whether your employer provides a furnished or company-arranged apartment — common for relocating professionals | Skip renter's/tenant due diligence; documentation and deposit norms vary a lot by city and landlord |
| Treat childcare and eldercare as a family-and-market solution, not a state one — plan the household staffing accordingly | Assume every city has equal infrastructure; power, water, and traffic reliability vary enormously by neighborhood |

### Netherlands

The Dutch model is built on mandatory, standardized coverage funded by high but predictable costs. Per nlcompass.com, basic health insurance (basisverzekering) runs about €142–185 a month, is legally identical in coverage across insurers, and carries a fixed €385 annual excess in 2026 — the same figure as 2025. It is, in other words, a system designed to remove variance: you cannot shop your way into worse basic coverage, and you cannot be denied it. The tradeoff shows up elsewhere, chiefly in housing. The OECD's 2025 Economic Survey flags an accessibility crisis, with average home prices reaching roughly €537,661 and rents climbing 6%+ annually; private rental listings routinely arrive "kaal," a term that catches newcomers off guard when they realize it means no flooring, no light fixtures, sometimes no kitchen.

Childcare and welfare are similarly structured but bureaucratically dense. The OECD notes the Dutch government is increasing childcare allowance spending by roughly €2 billion through 2028, but the broader tax-and-benefit system is, in the OECD's own words, "overly complex," discouraging some parents — disproportionately single parents — from working more for fear of losing benefits. The generosity is real; the system for accessing it is not built for speed.

### India

India's living-cost advantage is real but comes with the safety net stripped out and re-sold, item by item, as a private good. Per Remitly's 2026 cost-of-living data, a comfortable single professional life runs $800–$1,500 a month depending on city, and a family of four can live on roughly ₹270,000–₹450,000 ($3,000–$5,000) a month covering housing, food, transport, education, and healthcare — figures that would barely cover Dutch rent alone. Housing is the biggest single variable: a one-bedroom in central Mumbai can run €400–600 a month versus €100–200 in smaller cities like Jaipur or Kochi, a spread with no real Dutch equivalent given how compact the Netherlands is geographically.

What India doesn't offer, by comparison, is a state-run substitute for the things Dutch residents take for granted. Health insurance is functionally private for most professionals and expats — basic private coverage runs $40–90 a month, per Aetna International — and childcare, eldercare, and household labor run through the market and extended family rather than the state, which is why professional households in cities like Bangalore and Mumbai default to hiring cooks, cleaners, and drivers as a near-standard part of managing a working life, not a luxury.

### The Reckoning

The Netherlands sells predictability at a premium: fixed insurance terms, a real unemployment safety net through UWV, and a bureaucracy that, while slow, rarely leaves you with genuinely nothing. India sells flexibility and lower absolute costs, but every safety net — health, childcare, eldercare — has to be assembled individually, usually by negotiating it into a compensation package or hiring it directly, which means your actual quality of life depends heavily on your specific employer and specific city rather than a national floor.

The Hofstede Insights framework is instructive here in one dimension: India scores notably higher on power distance than the Netherlands, and that shows up institutionally as much as socially — Dutch systems are flatter and more standardized by design, Indian systems more variable and more dependent on where you sit in the hierarchy of a given employer or city. A relocating professional comparing a Dutch offer to an Indian one is not just comparing salary; they're comparing whether the state or the household assembles their safety net.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone weighing an €8,000-a-month Amsterdam offer against a 19-lakh Indian salary worked through the math candidly: after Dutch taxes, that figure nets closer to €4,600, with roughly €1,200 of it gone to rent before anything else is budgeted.*

> *Quora — A respondent who had recently moved from India described the Netherlands as unlike almost any other expat move, saying the cultural distance shows up in small daily mechanics — how you buy groceries, how directly you're expected to speak to your boss — as much as in the big financial numbers.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — Someone comparing a Bengaluru salary to an Amsterdam offer noted that the Dutch 30% tax ruling for skilled migrants meaningfully changes the calculation for the first five years, citing a friend who moved on a modest euro salary and was still able to buy a house.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A different thread bluntly cautioned that for anyone with a family, staying in India can be the more practical call, since managing kids, cooking, and daily household logistics without paid help is a real adjustment in most of Europe.*

> *financialmechanic.com — An expat writing about their first encounters with the Dutch healthcare system described the mandatory insurance and fixed excess as taking some getting used to, but ultimately found the flat, no-surprises billing a relief compared to systems where costs vary by provider.*

---

### Conclusion

If predictability matters more to you than raw savings — a fixed insurance bill, a real unemployment safety net, a bureaucracy that's slow but rarely abandons you — the Dutch system is worth the higher cost of entry, bare walls and all. If you want your money to go further and don't mind assembling your own safety net through your employer, your neighborhood, and your household staff, India's lower costs come with real tradeoffs in state-backed security. Either way, don't compare the headline salary numbers without pricing in what each country makes you buy separately. The honest version I'd tell a friend: in the Netherlands you pay for peace of mind up front; in India you pay for it later, one negotiated benefit at a time.
