**The Quiet Objection and the Loud One: Feedback in Norway and Poland**

**🇳🇴 Norway · 🇵🇱 Poland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Norwegian manager who says "we might want to think about that" is not musing aloud — she is telling you your plan is wrong, just at a volume calibrated to a culture that considers open criticism a mild act of aggression. A Polish engineer who tells you the same plan is flawed will very often just say so, plainly, in the room, tone still professional. Both countries think of themselves as direct. Only one of them actually sounds like it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for understatement — "not sure that's the best approach" is a real objection, not a maybe | Don't mistake soft phrasing for lack of conviction; Norwegian hedging is a delivery style, not ambivalence |
| Expect a structured annual review (medarbeidersamtale) focused on growth and alignment, not just scoring | Don't publicly praise or single out one employee over the team — janteloven discourages standing out |
| Keep feedback focused on specific behaviors and processes, not personal traits | Don't expect the same directness on personal performance that you get on technical or commercial topics |
| Use a probation period (often up to six months) as a genuine, clearly communicated feedback runway | Don't assume flat hierarchy means feedback flows freely upward without prompting — ask for it directly |
| Take quiet, brief feedback seriously — Norwegians don't repeat a point they've already made once | Don't over-explain praise; excessive enthusiasm can read as insincere in an equality-minded culture |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, sometimes blunt feedback, especially in technical and engineering fields | Don't take blunt critique personally — the tone is meant to stay professional even when the words are sharp |
| Deliver sensitive or critical feedback privately, one-to-one, never in front of the team | Don't publicly correct a manager or senior colleague — hierarchy is real and visible |
| Recognize that upward feedback (employee to manager) is a newer, still-developing practice | Don't assume junior staff will volunteer criticism of a decision unprompted |
| Build trust with your manager — trust levels are reported as high once established | Don't treat formality as coldness; Polish workplaces pair hierarchy with genuine employee-manager communication |
| Come prepared with data and specifics — pragmatic, evidence-based feedback lands best | Don't expect Western expat managers to be automatically preferred; reactions to expat leadership are genuinely mixed |

---

Norway's feedback culture is shaped by two forces pulling in slightly different directions: a genuinely direct communication norm on substantive, technical matters, and janteloven, the unwritten social code that discourages self-promotion and standing out from the group. Hofstede scores Norway low on both power distance and uncertainty avoidance, consistent with an egalitarian workplace where junior staff are expected to question ideas openly — but multiple relocation guides, including CultureFlow's expat-facing breakdown, note a crucial asymmetry: Norwegians are plainly direct about numbers, timelines, and process, while personal feedback about an individual's performance is delivered far more obliquely, often as a suggestion or a question rather than a flat statement. The annual medarbeidersamtale, a structured performance dialogue, formalizes this into a designated space for reflection and goal-alignment rather than a scored report card.

Poland sits at nearly the opposite end of Hofstede's map — 68 on power distance and a striking 93 on uncertainty avoidance, among the highest scores globally, reflecting a workplace culture built on formal hierarchy and a strong preference for clear rules over ambiguity. Yet that hierarchy coexists with a communication style multiple sources describe as directly, sometimes bluntly, honest — particularly in technical and engineering fields, where feedback on the work itself is delivered without much cushioning. The Cultural Atlas and Commisceo Global both flag the same nuance Poland shares with many high-power-distance cultures: criticism of the work can be blunt, but criticism of a person, especially delivered publicly or across a seniority gap, is handled privately instead. Notably, per workplace research summarized by Sorwe's Poland report, upward feedback — employees critiquing managers — remains a comparatively new and still-developing practice, a legacy of the more top-down structure.

**The Reckoning**

The irony here is that Poland, the higher-hierarchy, higher-formality culture on paper, is often the more legible one for feedback on the actual work — you will generally be told plainly, sometimes bluntly, that something is wrong. Norway, the flatter and more egalitarian culture, requires more careful listening precisely because equality-consciousness pushes personal feedback toward understatement rather than volume. A newcomer used to reading directness as a reliable signal will do fine with Polish technical critique and badly with Norwegian personal feedback, mistaking "we might want to think about that" for a mild aside rather than the substantial objection it actually is.

The hierarchy question flips the comparison again. Poland's structure is visible and stable — you know where feedback is supposed to originate and who it's supposed to flow to, even as upward feedback slowly becomes more normal. Norway's flatness theoretically invites feedback from anywhere, but janteloven's discomfort with standing out means that theoretical openness doesn't always translate into someone actually telling you, unprompted, what they think of your work.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *cultureflow.no (relocation guide) — A guide for newcomers working with Norwegian colleagues described the volume mismatch directly: expats coming from more expressive cultures consistently underestimated how much a mild phrase like "we could look at that again" was actually meant to convey, and missed real objections as a result.*

> *Quora — A contributor describing Polish workplace communication noted that engineering teams in particular would tell you bluntly and immediately if your approach was wrong, in a tone that outsiders sometimes read as harsh but that Polish colleagues considered simply honest and time-saving.*

> *Blind — A tech employee comparing Nordic and Central European offices observed that Norwegian teams were far more comfortable challenging a technical decision out loud than an American colleague's individual output, which took real recalibration to read correctly during review season.*

> *Substack (Nordic culture commentary) — A piece on janteloven's grip on Norwegian workplaces argued that its real effect wasn't suppressing ambition but suppressing the visible celebration of it, meaning strong individual performance was still noticed and rewarded, just rarely discussed out loud.*

> *Sorwe Global Report (Poland) — Workforce research cited in the report found that while formal hierarchy remains strong in Polish companies, trust in direct managers is comparatively high, and communication about company decisions flows down more consistently than the formal structure alone would suggest.*

---

### Conclusion

If you need to be told clearly when your work is wrong, Poland will do it for you, sometimes more bluntly than you'd like, and you'll survive it. If you need someone to say, out loud, that you personally did well, Norway will make you work for it — the compliment exists, it's just filed under something that sounds like a suggestion. Neither system is broken; both are protecting something real, hierarchy-legible clarity in one case, hard-won equality in the other. If a friend asked me over drinks: in Warsaw, brace yourself and move on; in Oslo, learn to hear the quiet part.
