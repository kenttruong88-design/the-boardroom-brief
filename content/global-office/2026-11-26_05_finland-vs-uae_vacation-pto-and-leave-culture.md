# Finland Closes for July, the UAE Closes Only When the Moon Says So

🇫🇮 Finland · 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Finland, the law guarantees most employees a month of summer holiday and regards an answered work email during it as work. In the United Arab Emirates, the labour law guarantees 30 days of annual leave, while the International Labour Organization, as cited by Business Insider, puts the average employed person at 50.9 hours a week, second in the world. Both countries therefore offer a respectable number of days off. Only one of them has a culture that believes in the number, and it is not the one that needed a statute to say so.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan summer leave early and expect dates to be settled around Midsummer, when the season begins | Schedule client deliverables or meetings between Midsummer and early August, when business largely stops |
| Take at least one uninterrupted two-week block, which the rules require | Ask to be paid cash instead of taking leave while employed, which Finnish forum members say the law does not allow |
| Check your collective agreement for a holiday bonus, typically half of holiday salary where it exists | Assume a holiday bonus is statutory, since Finnish legislation does not define one |
| Remember the leave year runs April 1 to March 31 and accrues monthly | Expect a full allowance in your first months, because leave builds at 2 or 2.5 days per month |
| Ask whether Saturdays count as leave days in your sector, because a week can be five or six days | Assume 30 days always means six calendar weeks |
| Treat leave as genuinely off, since answering work email counts as work | Message a colleague on holiday and expect an answer, or an apology |

### UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit leave requests in writing and early, especially for school holidays and festive periods | Assume approval, since employers can defer or reject leave for operational reasons |
| Check whether your contract counts leave in calendar days (mainland default) or working days (DIFC and ADGM) | Plan clever holiday bridging on a calendar-day contract, where weekends already count |
| Confirm Eid dates with HR close to the time, since they depend on moon sighting | Book non-refundable flights around predicted Eid dates |
| Ask for a day in lieu or a 50% uplift on basic salary if required to work a public holiday | Assume holiday work is simply part of the job |
| Agree carry-over in writing, since unused leave rolls over only with employer consent | Assume unused days survive the year |
| Check your final settlement, because unused leave is payable on termination | Resign before counting your accrued days |

## Finland: A Nation That Closes for July

Finland does not so much grant holiday as schedule a national absence. According to the payroll firm Azets, the leave year runs from April 1 to March 31, with employees earning two or two and a half days per month depending on length of service. A summer season runs from May 1 to September 30, within which 24 days must be taken, and the remainder goes to winter. The Finnwards blog, written from inside the country, summarises the arrangement as 30 days a year, usually 24 in summer and six in winter, with at least one unbroken two-week block. Over a third of the workforce is away in July, the same source says, and coworkers and clients vanish for four weeks, which it concedes can be annoying to newcomers before it becomes admirable.

The details reward a careful reader. Azets notes that whether Saturdays count as leave days depends on the collective agreement, so a week may consume five days or six. The holiday bonus, a Finnish institution that foreigners tend to hear about only after arrival, is not defined in legislation at all. It comes from collective or local agreements and is typically half of the holiday salary where it exists. A newcomer on the Finland Forum complained that the accrual rules disadvantage people in their first year, and other members replied that departing workers still receive proportional compensation and that cash in lieu of leave while employed is not permitted. Business Tampere adds the surrounding context: a workday of about seven and a half to eight hours, overtime that is uncommon, and punctuality treated as a moral position. Hofstede Insights scores Finland 33 on power distance, which helps explain why a manager is unlikely to refuse a holiday request with a straight face. No UAE score was retrievable, and this columnist declines to invent one.

None of this is flawless. Leave accrual is slow for new arrivals, and the system presumes that you understand it before you sign. But the underlying proposition is simple and, to anyone raised elsewhere, faintly suspicious: the time off belongs to you, the employer arranges around it, and nobody expects a reply from the lake.

## UAE: Thirty Days, Subject to Approval

The UAE government portal states that employees receive fully paid annual leave of 30 days after one year of service, and two days per month after six months. The employer sets the dates, with one month's notice, in agreement with the employee or by rotation. Unused leave carries over only with the employer's consent, and it is paid at basic salary. Gulf News, drawing on a lawyer and an HR director, reports that employers may defer or reject leave for operational reasons, provided they say so in writing, and that frequent attempts to bridge holidays are a standard ground for refusal. Mainland contracts count calendar days, so weekends and public holidays inside your leave are already consumed, while the DIFC and ADGM free zones count working days.

The public holidays have a character of their own. Morgan Lewis, summarising Cabinet Decision 27/2024, lists three days for Eid al Fitr, one for the Day of Arafah, three for Eid al Adha, one each for the Islamic New Year and the Prophet's birthday, and two for National Day on December 2 and 3. Several depend on moon sighting, so the calendar is confirmed late, and the Cabinet may move non-festive holidays to the start or end of a week. Anyone required to work on a holiday is owed a day in lieu or a 50% uplift on basic salary. That is a sound rule, and the existence of the rule is itself a small piece of evidence about how often the situation arises.

Then there are the hours. Business Insider, citing the ILO, puts the average at 50.9 a week. A British finance worker interviewed there described a day running roughly from 8 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. with lunch at his desk and colleagues calling during dinner. Two academics quoted in the same piece offer the structural explanation: a surplus of expatriate labour weakens individual bargaining power, and a high-context culture expects availability beyond the contract. Ramadan is the pleasant exception, since working hours shrink, and a long-term Sharjah resident told Khaleej Times he has never taken annual leave during the month because the shorter day suits him.

## The Reckoning

Finland and the UAE both put a generous figure on paper, which is where the similarity ends. Finland's 30 days are protected by norms as well as by law: the block of two weeks must be taken, email during leave counts as work, and the surrounding economy slows in sympathy. The UAE's 30 days are a legal ceiling on what you may claim, not a guarantee of when you may take it. The employer chooses the dates, can defer them for operational reasons, and holds the carry-over key. The distinction between an entitlement and a permission is, in practice, the entire comparison.

The compensating factors are real. A Finnish newcomer waits months to accrue a worthwhile allowance and may find that the summer has been scheduled without them. A UAE worker enjoys a shorter Ramadan day, a public holiday calendar of near-continuous festive blocks and a statutory payout of unused leave on exit. But a worker who averages nearly 51 hours a week is not short of days off in the abstract, only of the afternoon on which to use them. If rest is the product you are shopping for, Finland sells it by default and the UAE sells it on request.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing on leave alone, Finland is the straightforward answer, with the caveat that you should read the accrual rules before arrival and learn what your collective agreement says about bonuses and Saturdays. If the UAE is the offer, negotiate the leave clause as if it were the salary: ask whether days are counted in calendar or working days, how carry-over is approved, and whether leave is realistically granted in school holidays. Write the answers down, because the portal says the employer decides, and verbal assurances are not a leave policy.

Both countries will give you roughly a month off. In Finland you will take it, and in the UAE you will have been granted it, which is not the same, as the ILO figure quietly suggests.
