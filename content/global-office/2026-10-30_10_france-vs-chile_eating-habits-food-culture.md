**France Spends Two Hours a Day Eating. Chile Spends Its Evenings Pretending It's Having Tea.**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇨🇱 Chile**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The French eat for 2 hours and 13 minutes a day on average — more than double the American figure — and for a century had an actual law on the books forbidding anyone from eating lunch at their desk. Chile took a British colonial import, a short late-morning tea break called elevenses, and over two centuries quietly dragged it all the way to early evening, where it now functions as an entire second dinner disguised as a snack. Neither country is really eating more food than anywhere else. Both are simply refusing to let eating become incidental to the rest of the day.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect lunch to genuinely pause the workday — many offices and shops close roughly from noon to 2pm | Schedule a working lunch meeting assuming colleagues will treat it as thirty efficient minutes; the French average is 2 hours 13 minutes of eating and drinking per day, the highest in the OECD |
| Eat in the break room, a café, or a canteen — the social and physical separation from your desk is the actual point | Eat at your desk out of habit; a century-old French law technically forbade it, and though it's been suspended, the cultural expectation to step away largely persists |
| Let restaurant service feel slow — it's deliberate, designed so you're not rushed through a meal with company | Ask for the bill the moment you finish eating, the way some visiting Americans report doing; in France, you ask for it because otherwise the table is yours for the evening |
| Treat a long family lunch, especially with older relatives, as entirely normal — some run from noon to four | Plan anything time-sensitive immediately after a family meal with grandparents involved |
| Accept the cheese course as a real, time-budgeted part of dinner, not an optional extra | Rush through it; it reliably adds real minutes to a meal, and skipping it can read as odd at a family table |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect "once" (oh-seh) — Chile's adapted version of British elevenses — to happen in the early evening, roughly 5 to 7pm, not late morning | Take the name literally; once has drifted hours away from its 19th-century British timing and function |
| Treat once as a real, bread-heavy meal — Chileans eat more bread than any other Latin American country, often with avocado, cheese, or manjar-filled pastries on weekends | Assume once is a light afternoon nibble; in busy cities it increasingly functions as dinner itself |
| Recognize once's egalitarian streak — it's a ritual rich and poor families share in similar form, per documentarian Maite Alberdi's framing: "Here, it's all about abundance" | Treat it as a formal, delicate tea service in the English style; Chilean once is deliberately more generous and casual |
| Expect lunch prices and value to vary noticeably between restaurants, even on a seemingly standard prix fixe menu | Assume a fixed-price lunch deal is automatically competitive; it's worth comparing before committing |
| Build in flexibility around long urban workdays cutting into the once tradition | Assume every Santiago household still keeps the full, leisurely version of the ritual; city schedules are visibly compressing it |

---

France's relationship with mealtimes is, more than almost any other country's, actually legislated. A 2026 NPR report on the subject noted that France's ban on eating lunch at your desk carried roughly a "100-year history" before being suspended during the pandemic, and that ordinary lunch breaks can run up to ninety minutes, typically at sidewalk cafés or office canteens rather than at a keyboard. The cultural weight behind the number is real: OECD time-use data, as reported by The Local, found French people spend 2 hours 13 minutes a day eating and drinking — the highest figure of any country tracked, and roughly double the American 1 hour 2 minutes. Not everyone welcomes the enforced ritual equally. Kaitlin Plachy, an American teacher living in France quoted in that same NPR piece, described herself as "relieved" the desk-eating ban was suspended, having quietly used solo desk-lunches while grading papers as what the article called a "quiet rebellion" against the expectation to dine with colleagues — a reminder that even in a culture built around communal eating, not everyone wants the company.

Chile's signature eating ritual took the opposite historical path: instead of institutionalizing a midday pause, it imported a British teatime habit and let the clock drift on it almost completely. "Elevenses" arrived with British immigrants in the 1800s as a late-morning tea break; two centuries later, as Atlas Obscura's coverage details, "once" (pronounced oh-seh) typically happens in the early evening, loosely around the hours a Briton might now call happy hour. What hasn't drifted is the generosity: Chileans eat more bread than any other Latin American nation, building once around marraqueta or hallulla rolls with jam, avocado, or scrambled eggs on weekdays, and sponge cake, lemon pie, or manjar-filled pastries on weekends — with real regional variation, from kuchen in the Patagonian south to sopaipillas inland. Documentarian Maite Alberdi, quoted in the same piece, drew the cultural contrast directly against the ritual's English ancestor: "Here, it's all about abundance."

### The Reckoning

The real divergence is where each culture chose to protect mealtime from the workday's encroachment. France legislated the protection directly into labor culture — close the shop, leave the desk, take the ninety minutes, and do it with company if you can manage it; the law existed specifically because eating alone at work was considered a problem worth regulating against. Chile never built that kind of midday legal scaffolding, so the protection migrated instead into the evening, where once increasingly does double duty as both snack and dinner, especially in cities where "busy urban workdays," as Atlas Obscura notes, make the traditional leisurely version harder to sustain.

There's a genuine irony buried here: France's protection is institutional but increasingly resented by at least some residents (Plachy's "quiet rebellion" is a small but real data point), while Chile's protection is purely cultural, unenforced by any law, and yet arguably more resilient — once survived a British colonial import's complete temporal relocation and gained renewed interest during pandemic lockdowns, according to the same reporting, precisely because people cooking at home reconnected with it voluntarily rather than by mandate.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *NPR (2022/2026 coverage) — Kaitlin Plachy, an American English teacher living in France, admitted the quietly rebellious upside of the desk-eating ban's suspension: she was "relieved to see it go," having spent years eating lunch alone at her desk while grading papers rather than joining colleagues — a small, honest crack in the stereotype that everyone in France wants the long communal lunch.*

> *The Local France (reader-sourced feature on French meal length) — One young French woman described her own family's extended lunches with grandparents running "from noon to 4pm," followed by an afternoon snack — an ordinary Sunday by her account, not a special occasion.*

> *The Local France (same feature, visiting American's account) — An American visitor captured the service-pacing culture shock directly: in France, "you have to ask for the bill," because tables are routinely held for the whole evening unless a diner actively signals they're done — the opposite default from most American restaurant service.*

> *Atlas Obscura (quoting documentarian Maite Alberdi) — Alberdi's single line draws the clearest contrast between once and its English ancestor: "Here, it's all about abundance," describing a ritual that, unlike delicate English afternoon tea, is shared similarly across rich and poor Chilean households alike.*

> *Yale Globalist travel-reporting blog — A visiting American researcher noted the practical side-effect of adapting to Chilean dinner timing: eating around 8:30pm meant her group was consistently "the first people to arrive at a place for dinner," loud, large, and early by local standards — a small, specific embarrassment most visitors to Chile eventually share.*

### Conclusion

If food is going to be part of your daily rhythm wherever you land, ask which protection you'd rather have: a legal one that occasionally breeds quiet resentment, or a cultural one that nobody enforces but almost everyone still honors. France will build the pause into your contract, more or less, whether or not you want the company. Chile will just quietly move its signature meal three hours later than its name suggests, and trust you to show up anyway.

What I'd tell a friend: in France, take the full lunch, with people, even on the days you'd rather not — it's doing more for you than you'll notice at the time. In Chile, don't show up to dinner at an American hour expecting company; come back at nine, and bring an appetite for bread.
