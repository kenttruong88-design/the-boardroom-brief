**Singapore vs. Hong Kong: Two Different Ways to Never Quite Leave the Office**

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Singapore and Hong Kong compete for the same regional headquarters, the same finance and tech talent, and, less flatteringly, the same real estate near the top of every "most overworked city" index published in the last decade. Singapore's workforce logs 44.6 average weekly hours in the latest International Labour Organisation figures; Hong Kong's comes in at 43.1 — both well above the 40-hour line the ILO treats as a sane baseline, and both enough to land Singapore second on the Kisi Work-Life Balance Index's worst-performers list. What should actually concern the professional weighing a relocation package is not the hours themselves, but what the law does, or conspicuously declines to do, about them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your contract puts you under Part 4 of the Employment Act — most professionals earning above S$2,600 a month fall outside it entirely, with no statutory overtime pay. | Don't assume the Act's 44-hour week and 72-hour monthly overtime cap applies to you; it was written for workmen and lower-paid staff, not most PMETs. |
| Ask at offer stage whether Saturday half-days are contractual — still standard in law, trading floors, and retail or F&B head offices. | Don't be the first to log off at 6pm on a hierarchical team without reading the room first; visible presence still often outranks visible output. |
| Clarify your regional reporting lines before you sign — overlap with London, New York or Sydney routinely pushes both start and finish times later. | Don't expect a strict 9-to-6 if you're joining an APAC or global HQ role; "flexible hours" there often means flexible start times stacked onto long days. |
| Track your own hours for the first month against MOM's published average (45.8 usual hours, 2.5 hours paid overtime weekly) as a sanity check. | Don't book a dentist or a child's medical appointment assuming it comes out of anything but annual leave — it almost always does. |
| Put flexible-work terms in writing; since December 2024, employers must formally consider and respond to a request within two months. | Don't mistake that 2024 guideline for a right to approval — employers can, and often do, say no. |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate overtime pay and hours limits directly into your contract — the Employment Ordinance guarantees a weekly rest day and statutory holidays, nothing more. | Don't assume Hong Kong mirrors Singapore's capped-and-paid overtime system; there is no equivalent statute for any salary band. |
| Ask bluntly in interviews about evening and weekend call culture, especially in finance and consulting, where a self-described "996" rhythm is common. | Don't expect legislation to fix it soon — a 2017 plan for mandatory overtime premiums was shelved in 2018 for non-binding, sector-specific guidelines instead. |
| Budget socially for a later start to the working day; Hong Kong offices trend toward later mornings than Singapore's. | Don't base your expectations on a tourist trip; people who move there describe daily working life as "very different" from visiting. |
| Compare your minimum paid-leave entitlement before signing — Hong Kong's statutory floor is as low as Singapore's. | Don't bring a trailing spouse without discussing travel norms first; frequent business travel by one partner is repeatedly linked to family strain in expat surveys. |
| Find out which public holidays your specific employer actually substitutes or honours, since enforcement varies firm to firm. | Don't assume a finance-sector salary buys you out of long hours; pay and hours are negotiated separately, and often independently. |

---

Singapore's Employment Act does regulate hours — up to a point recruiters rarely volunteer. Covered employees get an 8-hour day, a 44-hour week, overtime capped at 72 hours a month, and a 150% pay premium beyond that. The catch is who counts as "covered": workmen earning above S$4,500 a month and non-workmen — effectively most office-based professionals, managers, executives and technicians — earning above S$2,600 fall outside Part 4 entirely. That means the bulk of the white-collar workforce a relocation agency is trying to attract has no statutory overtime pay and no legal hours cap whatsoever. The Ministry of Manpower's own figures show usual hours running at 45.8 a week, with paid overtime averaging 2.5 hours weekly as of mid-2021 — a number the Ministry frames as encouraging, since it has fallen fairly steadily since 2006, though it counts only hours someone formally logged as overtime in the first place.

December 2024 brought Singapore's Tripartite Guidelines on Flexible Work Arrangement requests, which made it mandatory for employers to formally consider, document, and respond to a request within two months. They are not required to approve it. That gap between a process right and an outcome right matters more than it sounds: Hofstede's power-distance score for Singapore sits at 74, among the highest measured anywhere, which tracks closely with a culture where deference to the person who decides your flexible-work request still shapes who actually gets to use it.

A Morgan McKinley Working Hours Survey of 1,000 professionals found roughly two-thirds felt obliged to work beyond their contracted hours, and nearly 90% of those doing so were not paid for the extra time. One in eight were contracted to work more than 46 hours a week to begin with — already above the 44-hour legal ceiling, a reminder that the ceiling applies to contracted hours, not actual ones. The same survey found most professionals did not resent the extra time itself, so long as it felt productive, which says less about Singapore's culture than about what a competitive labour market will train anyone to tolerate.

Hong Kong offers less law, not more. The Employment Ordinance guarantees one rest day in seven and statutory holidays, and stops there — no statutory maximum working week, no overtime-pay requirement, for anyone, at any salary. A 2017 government proposal would have mandated higher overtime rates for employees earning up to HK$11,000 a month; it was dropped in 2018 in favour of voluntary, sector-specific guidelines the administration said it would roll out for eleven industries by 2020 (Legislative Council Secretariat). The snapshot behind that decision: in 2018, full-time employees' median hours stood at 44.3 a week, 37% of employees worked 48 hours or more, and 9% worked 60 hours or more — improved slightly from 2011, and still high by any international standard. A 2016 UBS survey still cited in expat coverage put typical Hong Kong hours above 50 a week, among the longest of any market it surveyed.

A 2022 study by Lingnan University's Institute of Policy Studies and Oxfam Hong Kong, surveying workers aged 18 to 29, found average weekly hours ranging from 43 to nearly 47 — against an international benchmark the researchers put at around 40 — with some respondents reporting weeks as long as 60 hours. Sixty percent said their employer simply did not pay for the overtime at all; only 30% received an allowance or time off in lieu. The researchers also noted many young respondents worked without signed contracts or specified hours in the first place, which makes "overtime" a somewhat theoretical concept when the baseline was never written down.

Put side by side, Singapore has more legal architecture around overtime and applies it to almost no one in a typical relocation package; Hong Kong has barely any legal architecture and makes no pretence otherwise. Counterintuitively, that can make Hong Kong easier to plan around: your hours are whatever your contract and manager decide, and everyone involved knows it from the start. In Singapore, the visible 44-hour cap creates an expectation of protection that most arriving PMETs discover — usually around their first unpaid Saturday — simply does not apply to them.

Both cities tie for the lowest statutory minimum annual leave among major financial hubs, at seven days, and both score poorly in employer rankings on work-life balance even while scoring well on pay and job security — a pattern the Randstad Workmonitor survey flagged as early as 2017 and which the more recent InterNations Expat Insider data suggests has not closed much since: Hong Kong's expats rank it near the bottom worldwide specifically on work-life balance, even as a clear majority say the move improved their careers. The practical upshot for anyone negotiating an offer: get leave and flexible-work terms written into the contract itself, in both cities, because neither government is going to do it for you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A commenter who worked in Singapore for three years described expecting roughly 60-hour weeks, mandatory Saturday office days at their employer, and having a child's medical appointment deducted straight from annual leave rather than treated separately; they called genuine flexible or work-from-home arrangements "nearly nonexistent" in practice.*

> *Blind — A poster describing a Hong Kong tech employer summed up the culture there as effectively "996" — long days, six-day weeks — and added it was "not worth it for average salaries," citing limited paid time off alongside the hours.*

> *Fishbowl — A Singapore-based financial analyst noted that actual hours vary more by employer type than people expect: at multinational companies with overseas reporting lines, staff often start and finish later than standard hours because of time-zone overlap, which quietly eats into evenings rather than mornings.*

> *The Independent (Singapore) — A local professional recounted that across three different employers, bosses routinely expected overnight turnarounds on reports and after-hours calls framed as "urgent," and that pushing back on deadlines risked a worse performance appraisal, so colleagues kept working past official hours even when exhausted.*

> *South China Morning Post, via the American Women's Association of Hong Kong — A community spokesperson linked poor work-life-balance survey results partly to "trailing spouse" pressure to find local work, and noted that frequent business travel by expat husbands added extra strain to family life for those with children.*

### Conclusion

If the question is which city's law delivers a better work-life balance, the honest answer is neither. Singapore's statute book reads better on paper and applies to fewer of the people reading this than its marketing suggests; Hong Kong's reads worse on paper and is at least consistent about having almost no protections to begin with. The more useful question for someone actually moving is which kind of overwork they would rather negotiate against directly: a system that gestures at limits it will not enforce for most professionals, or one that never pretended to have limits in the first place. Ask about real start times, real Saturday norms, and real flexible-work approval rates before you ask about base salary — the number that determines your evenings was never the one printed on the offer letter.

Pick your city based on commute, tax, and family logistics, not on the fantasy that either one will let you leave at six.
