# South Korea vs India: Where Women Either Hit a Glass Ceiling or Can't Find the Elevator

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇮🇳 India

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In South Korea, women have made it into the office in respectable numbers, only to discover their pay slip was rounded down by nearly a third. In India, the fight is often simply to get through the door: the latest Periodic Labour Force Survey puts female labour force participation at 41.7 percent, up from 23.3 percent less than a decade earlier, but still meaning more than half of working-age women are not counted as economically active at all. One country has spent thirty consecutive years topping the OECD's gender pay gap rankings. The other is still building the on-ramp. Neither is a template for anything except patience, and possibly a stiff drink, though as we'll see, only one of these countries will make that drink mandatory.

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up, even briefly, to the first few *hoesik* (after-work dinners) — that's often where informal decisions get made | Assume the flat org chart on the website reflects how bluntly you can speak to someone two ranks up |
| Learn the honorific speech levels and use job titles, not first names, with seniors | Decline every group dinner outright; opting out entirely can quietly cost you "in-group" standing |
| Ask a trusted senior colleague (*sunbae*) to explain the unwritten rules before you break one | Be shocked by interview questions about marriage or pregnancy plans — illegal since 2001, still common in practice |
| Present and receive business cards with both hands | Take last-minute schedule changes personally; it's structural, not personal, but plan around it anyway |
| Ask directly about promotion timelines and named examples of women who've been promoted | Assume the 52-hour workweek law means the culture of visible long hours has actually gone away |
| Keep business attire conservative, especially in client-facing roles | Expect flexible work arrangements to be standard outside a handful of large, foreign-facing firms |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify in writing what "flexible hours" means at your specific employer — it varies wildly by manager, not just company | Assume urban corporate India generalizes to the rest of the country; practices differ sharply by city and sector |
| Ask about the Internal Complaints Committee required under the POSH Act, and whether it has ever actually met | Assume a POSH policy on paper means one is trusted or used in practice |
| Arrange verified transport for late calls with global time zones | Schedule solo late-evening travel without checking local norms first |
| Get maternity leave terms (26 weeks under the Maternity Benefit Act) confirmed in writing before you sign | Be surprised by direct questions about marital status or children in interviews — illegal, common, worth a prepared deflection |
| Build your network deliberately; women report smaller informal circles and less access to sponsorship | Assume remote or hybrid access is distributed evenly — some managers still equate visibility with presence |
| Use respectful address (sir/ma'am, or a "-ji" suffix) with clients and seniors | Skip reading the fine print on notice periods and non-competes, which differ meaningfully from Western norms |

### South Korea

According to the OECD, South Korea's gender wage gap stood at 29.3 percent in 2023 — 2.6 times the OECD average of 11.3 percent, and, per the Korea Herald, a full seven points ahead of Japan, the runner-up. Korea has held the OECD's last-place position on this metric every year since it joined in 1996, which is the kind of consistency most organizations would frame as a strength. McKinsey's research paints the leadership pipeline in similarly stark terms: Korean women hold roughly 10 percent of managerial roles, under 2 percent of director-level positions against a 10.5 percent global average, and just 13 of the CEO seats across 1,787 listed companies.

What makes this notable is that the participation gap itself has narrowed; Korean women are, by regional standards, reasonably present in the workforce. The trouble sits higher up and further into the pay packet. A recent Korea Herald-cited survey found 73.1 percent of female workers perceive gender disparities in workplace treatment, compared with 49.1 percent of male workers — a 24-point gap in what is ostensibly a shared workplace. Gapjil 119's Gender Discrimination Workplace Culture Index scored Korean offices at 67.4 out of 100 this year, a D grade, with leadership representation the weakest category. Respondents to that same body of research cited male-dominated promotion practices and career breaks tied to childbirth as the top two culprits, ahead of outright bias against competence. The incoming Lee administration has promised a wage transparency mandate; Korea has promised similar things before.

### India

India's story runs in something closer to the opposite direction. The PLFS shows female labour force participation climbing steadily — 41.7 percent in 2023–24, though the trend is uneven regionally, with Delhi's female participation rate reported at just 14.8 percent against Himachal Pradesh's considerably higher figure. Getting counted, in other words, is itself a live and unevenly distributed achievement, before any conversation about pay or promotion begins.

Once inside, the ambition is not the problem. KPMG's 2026 report on women's leadership in corporate India found that 79 percent of women professionals aspire to leadership roles, against just 1 percent currently occupying board-level positions — a gap that makes Korea's broken rung look almost proportionate. There has been real movement: companies with a woman MD or CEO rose to 119 in 2026 from 79 in 2021, though that's still only 5.21 percent of the 2,285 companies KPMG tracked. Deloitte's Women @ Work survey adds a dimension rarely discussed in the leadership-pipeline conversation: 46 percent of Indian respondents cited personal safety at work or during the commute as a top concern, and 31 percent reported experiencing workplace microaggressions in the past year. The Prevention of Sexual Harassment (POSH) Act has mandated Internal Complaints Committees since 2013; whether a given one meets, or has ever met, is a separate and locally variable question.

### The Reckoning

Here is the detail that should unsettle anyone treating "culture" as destiny. Hofstede Insights scores India at 56 on the Masculinity dimension — more competitive, more achievement-oriented, by the theory's own definition — against South Korea's 39, a comparatively consensus-driven, modesty-favoring score. If the theory held cleanly, India's boardrooms should look more like an obstacle course and Korea's should look like a garden party. In practice, Korea has the OECD's worst pay gap and a near-invisible female boardroom presence, while India — despite its steeper cultural competitiveness score and its own well-documented safety and access problems — has been adding women CEOs faster in percentage terms and has a female workforce whose participation rate, while still low by global standards, has nearly doubled in under a decade.

The uncomfortable read is that neither country's structural gap tracks tidily with its stated cultural values. Korea integrated women into the workforce and then built an entire economy of after-hours networking, seniority-based promotion, and childbirth-triggered career breaks that quietly does the discriminating instead. India, working from a lower participation base and real safety constraints, has produced a smaller but faster-moving cohort of women who, once inside the door, appear less willing to wait their turn. Whether that's cause for optimism about India or pessimism about Korea depends entirely on which country you're about to move to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a respondent who worked at a large Indian IT services firm described how, contrary to the usual narrative of bias against women in the promotion pipeline, female associates were sometimes prioritized for coveted onsite client postings when performance ratings were tied, which the respondent read as firms visibly chasing diversity optics on flagship assignments — welcomed by some, resented quietly by others left behind.*

> *Quora — another respondent detailed South Korea's after-work drinking hierarchy, noting that seniority determines not just who buys the next round but who gets consulted before a decision is finalized; declining the invitations consistently, for family or personal reasons, was framed by management as a personal choice, but the respondent noticed the actual influence and informal promotion chatter happened almost entirely at the table they weren't at.*

> *Blind — a thread started by a foreign employee planning a work trip to Bengaluru asked whether it was safe to bring his wife and daughters. Local commenters offered reassurance, pointing to women-only cab services available at the airport for late-night pickups; other commenters countered that the existence of a dedicated women-only transport category is itself the answer to the original question, not a rebuttal of it.*

> *Glassdoor Community, "Women in Tech India" bowl — a 28-year-old software engineer, the sole earner in her household, asked fellow members whether she should try for a baby now or push for a higher-paying role first, since she'd observed that once she disclosed a pregnancy, the trajectory conversation at her company tended to end rather than pause.*

> *Expat.com — an Indian professional named Meena described leading a fifteen-person team in Dubai while being the only woman on it; she was formally in charge of the project but routinely excluded from the after-work dinners where her male colleagues built the relationships that made her authority easier to exercise on paper than in the room.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two on gender dynamics alone, the honest answer is that you're choosing between two different kinds of waiting. In South Korea, you'll likely get hired, promoted at a plausible-looking pace, and then discover the compounding cost of a wage gap and a networking culture that runs mostly on dinners you weren't quite obligated to attend but couldn't really skip. In India, you may spend longer establishing that you belong in the room at all, navigating safety logistics that colleagues rarely have to think about, before running into a boardroom door that a genuinely fast-growing minority of women are now, at least, managing to open. Bring a mentor in either country, read the maternity policy before you sign anything, and go into the first *hoesik* or the first late client call with your eyes open — because the brochure, as ever, describes the office nobody actually works in. If a friend asked me over a drink which one to pick, I'd say: pick the one whose specific costs you can actually live with, because both of them are going to bill you.
