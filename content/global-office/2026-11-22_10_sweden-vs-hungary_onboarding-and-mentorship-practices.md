# Sweden Trusts You on Day One, Hungary Reserves Judgment Until Month Three

🇸🇪 Sweden · 🇭🇺 Hungary

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Gallup reports that only 12 percent of American employees say their company does a good job of onboarding, a figure that suggests badly run onboarding is a universal problem rather than a regional one. Sweden and Hungary fail to solve it in opposite directions. In Sweden, a new graduate is handed responsibility early and then left to ask for help, while in Hungary the survey data says only one worker in five has access to a formal mentor programme at all, and one in three needs more than six months to feel comfortable. One country treats the newcomer as a competent adult. The other keeps the newcomer on trial and the hierarchy intact.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for what you need, since inclusion is offered but initiative is expected | Wait to be assigned a task or a mentor |
| Accept coffee invitations; colleagues are reserved but friendship, once formed, tends to last | Read the reserve as hostility or expect personal conversation from day one |
| Learn Swedish; free SFI courses exist for anyone with a personal identity number | Assume English at lunch means English in the social conversation |
| Expect decisions to take time, as consensus and equality are the house style | Escalate to a senior person to bypass a decision you dislike |
| Check your probation terms, which can run up to six months | Forget that the employer still owes you at least two weeks' notice during probation |

### In Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be formal at first, use titles and shake hands | Open with first names and jokes in front of a senior manager |
| Show respect to senior managers and older colleagues | Expect to be consulted before a top-down decision is announced |
| Ask on day one who your mentor or point of contact is | Assume a formal programme exists, particularly in a smaller company |
| Attend the weekly Monday meeting, where tasks and schedules are set | Rush meetings or negotiations, which Hungarians prefer to weigh from every side |
| Confirm the probation length in writing; three months is standard, six if a collective agreement says so | Expect paid overtime; it is uncommon and usually unpaid |

## Sweden

Swedish onboarding is built on a premise that foreigners tend to find either flattering or alarming: that you were hired because you can do the job. Expat.com describes workplaces with relatively flat hierarchies and a strong emphasis on consensus and equality. A Latvian design strategist writing for The Local, who began as an intern in Gothenburg in 2014, recalled being given real responsibility as a new graduate and noted that everyone's voice is assumed to matter regardless of seniority. She also found that inclusion was offered readily, but that she had to ask for what she wanted. Expat.com is blunter about the social cost: work conversations are often in Swedish, newcomers who do not speak it will probably miss a good deal of the office talk, and many foreigners initially feel rejected, even as it describes colleagues as friendly but reserved.

The system around the newcomer is formal even when the office is not. According to the Swedish Mediation Office, 88 percent of Swedish employees were covered by a collective agreement in 2025 and 66 percent were union members. Business Sweden notes a maximum probationary period of six months and a minimum of two weeks' employer notice during it. Training is also a habit rather than a perk: Eurostat reported that the Stockholm region had an adult participation rate in education and training of 38.1 percent in 2022, the highest in the EU, against an EU average of 11.9 percent on the same four-week measure.

What Sweden lacks is structured human attention. The Local reported on a Stockholm municipal programme that pairs newly arrived migrants with experienced locals for monthly meetings over eight months, with up to 30 percent of participants finding work before it ends; it exists, in other words, because the workplace will not teach the social codes on its own. One participant, an Italian lawyer, valued the unwritten-rules coaching while noting that her mentor could not answer legal questions. Gallup's American research finds that employees are 3.4 times as likely to rate onboarding as successful when managers actively take part, which sits awkwardly with a culture in which the manager's hands-off posture is a point of principle.

## Hungary

Hungarian onboarding is thinner on ceremony and heavier on hierarchy. Expat Arrivals describes business structures as hierarchical, with status highly valued and decisions flowing from the top down, often without consulting subordinates. Expat.com's guide to Budapest, last updated in 2019, says the first days are mostly training and general information, at a relaxed pace, after which you are expected to do your job with some freedom over how and when. Weekly meetings, often on Mondays, set the tasks, and overtime is uncommon and typically unpaid, although eight accumulated hours may earn an extra day off. The Budapest Business Journal also noted that hybrid arrangements leave much less time for knowledge transfer, which lengthens onboarding.

The numbers describe a country where support depends on who happens to be next to you. A Profession.hu survey, reported by Világgazdaság in December 2021, found that only one respondent in five works somewhere with a dedicated onboarding mentor programme, that another 26 percent have an assigned mentor, and that in about a third of cases new staff can ask anyone. Some 4 percent said newcomers must settle in without any help. Those who had experienced a programme were largely convinced: 64 percent said it gets new colleagues into the workflow fastest and 57 percent said it supports long-term commitment to the employer. Mentoring typically lasts to the end of probation for a third of respondents, and for a quarter it lasts two to three weeks. The Budapest Business Journal, reporting a BonsaiX survey in September 2022, found that every third employee needs more than six months to feel comfortable in a new position, that only about a third of job changers are satisfied with their onboarding, and that roughly 70 percent of integration success depends on superiors and colleagues.

Size matters. An academic study of 13 Hungarian organisations and 161 employees, published by Enpress, found that all large companies in its sample had formal mentoring programmes, while smaller ones relied on informal or ad hoc arrangements. The legal backdrop is lighter than Sweden's: Eurofound cites collective bargaining coverage of about 21.8 percent in 2019 per the OECD and AIAS, conducted mostly at company level, and CEE Legal Matters notes a standard three-month probation, extendable to six by collective agreement, during which termination generally requires no reason.

## The Reckoning

The pairing looks like a contest between freedom and structure, but it is closer to a contest over who explains the rules. Sweden gives the newcomer autonomy, a high-trust flat office and a dense layer of collective agreements, and then withholds the explanation, so the mentorship has to be invented by the municipality, the union or the colleague who will eventually become a friend. Hungary supplies hierarchy and a clear chain of command, and then inconsistently supplies the mentor, so a formal programme exists at a large employer and a pointed question to a colleague is the substitute elsewhere. In both countries the first three months are a legal trial period; in Sweden the trust is extended in advance, in Hungary it is earned in the interim.

Hofstede Insights quantifies the gap: Sweden scores 31 for power distance and 5 for masculinity, Hungary 46 and 88, with uncertainty avoidance at 29 against 82. Sweden's 5 for masculinity points toward consensus and balance, and Hungary's 88 toward achievement and rules. Hungary's 46 for power distance is only moderate, and the same source describes its superiors as accessible, which fits the pattern of a boss who gives orders but does not hide.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local — a Latvian design strategist who started with an internship in Gothenburg said she received responsibility early as a new graduate, found slow bureaucratic decision-making frustrating but came to see it as sound, and had to push to be included in the social circle of older colleagues*

> *The Local — an Italian lawyer in Stockholm joined the municipal mentorship scheme to learn how Swedish law firms operate, and said she valued discussing unwritten workplace rules though her mentor could not answer legal questions*

> *Blind — a commenter on a thread about designing in Sweden said pay is low compared with other places but work-life balance is excellent*

> *Verge Magazine — an unnamed Canadian who had worked in Hungary for about a year and a half said English is the business language though most colleagues are not native speakers, offices are relaxed, open-plan and casual, and statutory vacation is at least 20 days*

> *Expat Arrivals — an Irish-American expat in Hungary said the work culture is very different, things are generally less efficient, and projects can move more slowly than in America*

## Conclusion

If you take a job in Sweden, assume that nobody will tell you what you are meant to be doing and that this is a sign of confidence rather than neglect. Ask for a mentor, learn the language, accept the coffee, and treat the probation period as a mutual audition with notice rights attached. If you take a job in Hungary, find out whether your employer is large enough to have a formal programme, and if not, locate the colleague most willing to explain the unwritten rules in your first week, since the data suggest that colleagues and superiors account for most of the outcome.

In both cases, the most useful question for the interview is the same: who, specifically, will I ask. Sweden will assume you already know. Hungary will tell you eventually.
