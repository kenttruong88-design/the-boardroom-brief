# Poland Says What It Thinks, on Time; Taiwan Says "We Will See" and Messages You at Nine

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇹🇼 Taiwan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries work long weeks. Eurostat puts the average Pole at 38.7 hours in 2025, against an EU mean of 35.9 and a range running from 31.9 in the Netherlands to 39.6 in Greece, and Taiwan's Ministry of Labor found roughly one in four employees receiving work messages after hours. What differs is what happens in the room. In Warsaw the meeting begins on time, follows an agenda and ends with someone having said exactly what they thought. In Taipei the meeting may end with everyone agreeing, and the disagreement arrives later, privately, by text message.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time; Expat Arrivals says meetings usually start and end on schedule | Be late without warning, which is read as disrespect |
| Bring an agenda and detailed information to formal meetings | Improvise a presentation and rely on charm |
| Use titles and surnames until invited to use first names | Switch to first names early; titles and education matter |
| Expect direct questions and frank feedback, and answer plainly | Read bluntness as hostility; Poles tend to say what they think |
| Open with small talk about sport or family | Open with money, Polish history or relations with neighbouring countries |
| Dress formally at first; tech firms are more relaxed | Assume casual dress until you have seen the office |

### In Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Praise colleagues in front of the group and criticise only in private | Give negative feedback in a team meeting, even constructively |
| Carry out a senior colleague's request exactly as instructed | Propose improvements to a new manager early in the relationship |
| Read "we will see" as a probable no | Press for an explicit yes in the room |
| Follow up written feedback with a face-to-face conversation | Rely on text messages to settle a delicate point |
| Set deadlines early and repeat them clearly | Assume a stated deadline is as fixed as it sounds |
| Agree on after-hours messaging expectations at the outset | Assume leaving on time is neutral; forum posters report it can be read as lazy |

## Poland

Expat Arrivals describes Polish business communication as direct but respectful. Formal meetings tend to follow clear agendas and begin and end on schedule, preceded by small talk that lets trust form before business does. Organisations are typically hierarchical, with decisions made at the top and senior executives highly respected, although international companies and tech start-ups, particularly under younger managers, are flatter. The same guide advises against first names until invited, and notes that native English speakers may find Polish forms of address uncomfortably formal while Poles regard them as basic respect.

Careers in Poland adds texture. Newcomers may be surprised by what it calls Polish grumbling, a habit of discussing what is going wrong, and by colleagues who focus on problems before a proposal's strengths. J.J. Singh, interviewed for the piece, says Polish companies are becoming more open to employee input, though the head still makes the final decisions. He recalls a meeting in Poland with his former Indian boss in which key staff presented their own views and the group planned jointly, something that in India would once have been a meeting of two bosses. The meeting, in other words, is where Polish opinions are expected to surface, and the decision is made elsewhere.

Poles are also expected to look the part and keep the distance. Expat Arrivals advises erring on the side of formality in business dress, notes that smiling at strangers can draw suspicion rather than warmth, and observes that eye contact in negotiations is read as respect. It also records that Poles generally say what they think and address issues head-on while keeping professional courtesy, so the frank question in a meeting should be answered in kind rather than deflected.

The hours are not trivial. An SD Worx survey of about 18,000 European employees, reported by Polskie Radio in June 2024, found that 43 percent of Polish workers regularly stay late, the highest share in the countries surveyed, and 47 percent of them gave extra income as the main reason. ADP data cited in the same report put Polish unpaid overtime at 4 hours and 43 minutes a week, against a global average of 7 hours and 7 minutes. Labour researcher Alicja Kotłowska says the minimum wage often makes overtime necessary. The Polish manager's directness, then, is not a luxury of idle time; it is how a group conserves hours it cannot spare.

## Taiwan

The Taiwan Gold Card Office, a government body courting foreign professionals, is candid about the arrangements. Taiwanese workplaces avoid confrontation, and criticising a colleague in a team meeting is a serious misstep even when constructive, whereas praise in front of a group is welcome. Feedback often arrives by text or email, so the guide recommends a prompt face-to-face conversation. Seniority matters: a task from a senior colleague should be carried out exactly as instructed, and suggestions to a line manager early on may be taken as arrogance. It also describes a presenteeism culture in which office time and performance targets are heavily weighted.

The guide also says that working through lower-level staff can slow matters, so a visitor should identify the person who can actually make the decision. Personal connections, known as guanxi, affect influence and the ability to get things done, and personal questions from colleagues may signal a wish for a closer relationship rather than idle curiosity.

Expat Arrivals supplies the grammar of the indirect style. Preserving honour is central, so locals may answer a question even when they do not know the answer, avoid saying no outright, and may not say directly when upset, leaving the listener to read the cues. Criticism, the guide says, belongs in private. Forumosa posters add that important messages are expected to be answered during holidays, often by checking a work phone at set times, and that some staff arrive exactly on time as a quiet assertion of autonomy. A foreign manager in a Forumosa advice thread is told the opposite emphasis: raise problems directly, because staff may sit on a blocker for a week assuming the manager is too busy to hear it.

The after-hours figures reflect the same culture of availability. The Ministry of Labor survey reported by the Taipei Times in 2023 found 25.2 percent of employees had done company business after leaving work, averaging 4.7 hours a month, and 72 percent said working from home was not offered. A January 2026 poll reported by Focus Taiwan put the share at 24.6 percent. In August 2025, the Taipei Times reported, total working hours averaged 172.4 for the month, including 8.8 of overtime. The ministry's own data suggest 84.1 percent of those working overtime were paid or given time off, which is the part the brochure can fairly advertise.

## The Reckoning

The distinction is between a culture that puts disagreement into the meeting and one that moves it out of the meeting. Poland tolerates and even expects open challenge between peers while keeping a firm hierarchy over decisions; Taiwan protects the group's face in public and conducts the real discussion in corridors, chat threads and one-to-one conversations. The expatriate who fails to adapt will either bruise Polish colleagues by hedging, which reads as evasion, or bruise Taiwanese ones by bluntness, which reads as aggression.

Hofstede Insights, as reproduced in course materials, scores Poland at 68 for power distance and 93 for uncertainty avoidance, and Taiwan at 58 and 69. The telling gap is individualism: 60 for Poland and 17 for Taiwan. Poles are hierarchical and individualistic, so they argue as individuals; Taiwanese are hierarchical and collectivist, so they accommodate as a group. Neither style is inefficient on its own terms, though each looks that way from the other's conference room.

The practical consequences are modest but real. A Polish team will resolve a disagreement in a single meeting and move on; a Taiwanese team may take three conversations to reach the same place, with the middle one conducted over text. The Polish approach costs some feelings and saves some hours, which matters in a country where, as Polskie Radio reported, a large share of workers are already giving up evenings. The Taiwanese approach costs hours and saves feelings, in a labour market where the ministry's surveys suggest the evenings are partly spoken for anyway.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *NYCU Global MBA student blog — A European student in Taiwan finds the local style indirect and inefficient, with many polite emails passing back and forth, and reports that many employees work unpaid late hours, sometimes twelve-hour days, and are proud of it.*

> *Forumosa (office culture) — Posters describe junior staff waiting for the manager to leave before going home, leaving on time several days running being read as lazy, overtime done for appearances or the free meal, and urgent meetings sometimes rewarded with food.*

> *Forumosa (Taiwanese company) — One poster says the standard day is nine to six and that some firms expect staff to stay until the boss leaves; another calls the hierarchy stereotypes exaggerated, and a third says overtime rules are now enforced more strictly than they once were.*

> *Forumosa (managing in Taiwan) — A manager advises raising problems directly rather than protecting face, since staff may sit on a blocker for a week, and treating a signed contract as a starting point for renegotiation.*

> *Careers in Poland — An interviewee recalls a Polish meeting with his former Indian boss in which key staff presented their own views and the group planned jointly, a scene he says would once, in India, have been a meeting of two bosses.*

## Conclusion

In Poland, be punctual, prepare an agenda, use titles until told otherwise, and say plainly what you think; the bluntness you meet is not an insult. In Taiwan, praise in public, criticise in private, treat "we will see" as an answer, and follow any delicate written exchange with a conversation. In both, ask early who actually makes the decision, because in neither is it necessarily the person speaking.

A Polish meeting will tell you what everyone thinks. A Taiwanese meeting will tell you what everyone is prepared to be seen thinking. Learn to read the second, and you will understand the first with ease.
