# Same Payday, Different Planet: What China and Jordan Actually Pay You For

🇨🇳 China · 🇯🇴 Jordan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Beijing, the real value of your contract is often hiding in a bonus check that arrives around Chinese New Year, sized so generously that missing it can feel like a demotion. In Amman, roughly half the national workforce draws a salary from a public sector that pays, on average, 21 percent more than the private sector for comparable work, according to Jordan's Department of Statistics. Neither country will tell you upfront that your headline monthly figure is a subplot. In both, it is.

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask explicitly whether the "13th month" is contractual or a discretionary year-end bonus | Assume a "12+1" or "12+3" offer means guaranteed cash — the "+" is often performance-linked |
| Compare your package by function and Mandarin fluency, not by passport | Expect a blanket "foreigner premium" — pay is increasingly role-driven, not nationality-driven |
| Time job changes around Chinese New Year payout cycles | Resign in November hoping to collect the year-end bonus in January — many contracts forfeit it |
| Clarify whether housing and relocation allowances are taxed as salary | Treat verbal promises of bonus size as binding — banking and finance bonuses swing hugely with performance |
| Check whether your employer is an SOE, since senior bonuses there are capped by regulation | Compare an SOE offer directly against a tech-sector offer without adjusting for bonus structure |

#### 🇯🇴 Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the full allowance breakdown (housing, transport, education) before comparing base salaries | Compare only base salaries — allowances can be a third or more of total take-home |
| Budget on roughly 600 JOD a month as a baseline for rent, food, and utilities in Amman | Assume a "respectable" entry salary of 450–550 JOD will comfortably cover city rents |
| Ask whether the role is benchmarked to public-sector, private-sector, or international NGO scales | Assume public-sector-adjacent postings are the weaker option — they often carry better job security and benefits |
| Expect visible pay gaps if working alongside local staff at an INGO or UN agency | Discuss your salary casually with local colleagues — the disparity is a known sore point |
| Get written confirmation of your wage before arrival, given a legal minimum wage of JOD 290 | Rely on informal "wasta" promises about pay or promotion — access without documentation later becomes a dispute |

## China

China's compensation system is built less around what you're paid monthly and more around when the rest of it lands. According to China's National Bureau of Statistics, the average annual wage for employees in urban non-private units rose to 129,441 yuan in 2025, up 4.3 percent nominally on the year — steady, unremarkable growth. What that headline number omits is the "13th salary," an extra month's pay clustered around Lunar New Year that isn't legally mandated but is practiced by the overwhelming majority of employers, according to multiple payroll compliance guides tracking the practice. In tech and internet firms, the informal shorthand is "12+1" or "12+3," where the "+" figure is sometimes a guaranteed contractual month and sometimes a discretionary bonus tied to KPIs — a distinction employees are wise to nail down in writing before they need it.

Sector matters enormously. In finance and banking, base salaries can look modest while year-end bonuses run to several months' pay, heavily weighted to individual and firm performance. In state-owned enterprises, a 2009 government regulation caps total bonus payouts to senior staff at no more than 15 times the average annual income of the company's own employees — a rare instance of pay-ratio populism written directly into policy. Mercer's Total Remuneration Survey projects average salary increases of 4.0 percent for 2026, with first-tier cities like Shanghai and Beijing tracking at or slightly above that figure and several second-tier manufacturing hubs trailing behind, evidence that China's labor market has stratified by geography as much as by industry.

For foreign hires, the era of an automatic "expat premium" is fading. Compensation is increasingly a function of role, seniority, and Mandarin fluency rather than nationality — a Chinese-speaking engineer from Tokyo and one from Toronto now land in roughly the same band at most multinationals, per compensation guides tracking the shift. The bonus calendar, not the base salary, remains the detail worth interrogating.

## Jordan

Jordan's pay structure runs on a different axis entirely: the line between public and private employment. The Department of Statistics puts the average monthly wage at JOD 551 as of 2023, but that average flattens a real split — public-sector employees earn an average of JOD 646 against JOD 493 in the private sector, a gap of roughly 21 percent that has made government jobs the aspirational default for a generation of Jordanian graduates. The World Bank has flagged that the public sector employs close to half the national workforce, well above the 22 percent typical of developed economies, a structural imbalance that successive Jordanian governments have tried, with limited success, to unwind.

What complicates any salary comparison is that Jordanian pay is rarely just a number. According to Jordan's Department of Statistics, wages are built from a basic salary plus a stack of allowances — family, cost-of-living, academic qualification, and profession-specific supplements for fields like engineering and medicine — meaning two workers with identical "base pay" can take home meaningfully different totals. Layered on top is wasta, the informal system of personal connections that research on Jordanian public administration describes as shaping access to public-sector positions in ways that outlast any individual pay negotiation; academic work on the subject has found that Jordanians themselves often discount the competence of colleagues perceived to have used it. For foreign workers, particularly in the NGO and international-organization sector that has grown around Jordan's role hosting regional operations, salaries pegged to international scales can sit multiples above local private-sector norms — a gap that is financially convenient and socially delicate in roughly equal measure.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the irony sharpens: China's compensation culture obsesses over timing — when the bonus lands, whether it's contractual, how the calendar year closes — while treating the base number as relatively fixed and comparable across employers. Jordan's culture obsesses over composition — what counts as salary versus allowance, which scale you're benchmarked against — while treating timing as a non-issue, since pay simply arrives monthly, allowances and all. A Chinese employee agonizes over one all-important December; a Jordanian employee agonizes over one all-important spreadsheet line.

Hofstede's cultural-dimension research, which scores China at 80 on Power Distance and a notably low 20 on Individualism, offers a partial explanation for China's comfort with hierarchical, opaque bonus discretion — deference to the employer's year-end judgment is culturally unremarkable in a way it would not be in a flatter, more individualist system. Jordan's own values research, meanwhile, describes a society with pronounced uncertainty avoidance, which tracks neatly with its preference for guaranteed, itemized allowances over the kind of open-ended performance bonus China's employees have learned to live with.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — an American hire in China described assuming the old "foreigner premium" would apply everywhere, only to learn that a colleague with the same title but no Mandarin was offered noticeably less than she was — pay, it turned out, tracked language and function, not passport.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — an engineer comparing a China offer to a US one found the base salary underwhelming until the "13+" structure was explained, then warned other foreign hires to get it in writing whether the extra month was contractual or discretionary, since his own bonus quietly evaporated during a slow quarter.*

> *Quora — a new arrival in Amman found a "respectable" entry salary of around 500 JOD disappeared fast against real rents, and only broke even once budgeting to the roughly 600 JOD a month locals themselves cite as the baseline for rent, food, and utilities.*

> *Quora — someone taking an international-organization role in Amman was startled to find their salary sat multiples above Jordanian colleagues doing similar work on local pay scales, and found navigating that visible gap socially trickier than negotiating the number itself.*

> *Quora — a longtime Amman resident advised newcomers to ask upfront whether a role is benchmarked to public-sector, private-sector, or international-NGO pay scales, since the headline monthly figure means little without knowing which allowance stack sits behind it.*

## Conclusion

Neither country's pay structure is more "honest" than the other — they simply hide the complexity in different places. China compresses the ambiguity into a single high-stakes date on the calendar; Jordan spreads it across a permanent, itemized negotiation over what counts as pay at all. A new arrival to China should spend less time comparing base salaries and more time reading the fine print on bonus timing. A new arrival to Jordan should do the opposite: ignore the fine print on timing and interrogate every line item on the payslip.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say this: in China, get the bonus clause in writing before you sign; in Jordan, get the allowance breakdown in writing before you accept — and in neither country should you believe the number on the job posting is the number you'll actually take home.
