# Malaysia Dresses for the Meeting, Singapore Dresses for the Air Conditioning

🇲🇾 Malaysia · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In April 2026, Malaysia's Public Service Department made batik a daily option for civil servants and mandatory on Thursdays, while ordering government air conditioning to a minimum of 24 degrees. In Singapore, a month later, a columnist proposed that offices might respond to the same energy anxieties by permitting shorts. Two neighbouring countries are therefore conducting the same experiment on a rather different premise. One treats clothing as a statement of respect and identity. The other treats it as a thermal engineering problem with a dress code attached.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to modest, formal office wear: collared shirt, closed shoes, tie or suit for senior meetings | Wear flip-flops or shorts to work; Expat.com's guide is blunt about both |
| Choose tops with sleeves reaching at least three-quarters of the arm and no see-through fabric | Assume that modesty norms are the same across every employer and industry |
| Check what staff actually wear on the company website or in local news photos | Guess the dress code from a job advertisement |
| Treat Thursday batik, casual Friday and festive traditional wear as legitimate calendar items | Ignore them and turn up in a suit while everyone else is in batik |
| Use titles and courteous forms of address, such as Encik or Puan, until told otherwise | Adopt informal terms like abang with senior colleagues before you are sure of the room |
| Bring a cardigan, because offices are often cold despite the climate | Mock the batik, the honorifics or the air conditioning in a first meeting |

### In Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear long trousers and a short-sleeved or light long-sleeved shirt for most offices | Arrive in shorts unless the company has clearly said they are fine |
| Ask about attire before day one, because formal firms exist and will not announce themselves | Assume that every large firm is casual just because a tech company is |
| Carry a light layer for office air conditioning that can be genuinely cold | Wear a heavy jacket on the street in the heat; locals do not |
| Match footwear to the sector: closed shoes for finance, law and accounting | Treat sandals or Birkenstocks as neutral; they still draw opinions |
| Follow the company's dress-down day rules and note when the rule changes | Confuse a casual Friday with a casual attitude to deadlines |
| Use first names once colleagues do, and keep email polite and brief | Over-explain your dress choices; no one asked |

## Malaysia

Malaysian workplace formality is anchored by hierarchy and religion, and clothing is the visible edge of both. Rework's guide to Southeast Asian business culture tabulates Hofstede's power distance for Malaysia at 100, the top of the scale, with individualism at 26. Expat.com's guide to working in Kuala Lumpur describes a strong sense of hierarchy, a preference for settling disagreements privately and workplace dress that is modest and formal: a well-fitting suit or skirt, a collared shirt, a tie and closed footwear. Many companies allow a casual Friday with polo shirts and jeans. Flip-flops and shorts are described as not acceptable.

The state reinforces the signal. The Public Service Department's April 2026 circular, reported by Malay Mail, makes batik mandatory on Thursdays and encouraged on other days, allows corporate attire on every day except Thursday, and excludes uniformed officers. The same department told The Star in 2022 that its dress code for customers at government offices supports the Rukun Negara principle of courtesy and morality, and compared it with practices in Japan, Britain and Australia. Dress, in this reading, is civic behaviour rather than personal expression. The theme is not new: in 2019 Human Resources Online reported that a Sabah ministry already applied traditional-outfit Thursdays, with its minister describing the aim as strengthening cultural heritage.

Private employers are more varied. A 2019 Human Resources Online survey of Malaysian employers found iCar Asia using smart casual from Monday to Thursday and casual on Fridays, Schaeffler allowing smart casual on Fridays and the eve of public holidays, and PwC Malaysia using flex-wear with clear guidelines. TIME dotCom wondered whether too much comfort changes how people approach work. Sectors with Muslim-majority staff add further variation, including long skirts, tunics and headscarves, and a newcomer is advised to observe before assuming. Tales Magazine's post reports from Kuala Lumpur add a note of historical drift: older respondents from 2008 to 2011 describe shirts and ties as the norm, while recent ones describe business casual, with the dressy batik shirt as a respectable alternative to a suit.

Formality extends beyond clothing into address and pace. A debate reported by The Sun in Malaysia turned on whether women should call older male colleagues abang, an informal term, or the more formal encik, with some commenters arguing for first names to keep boundaries clear and others for formal titles. Expat.com's guide adds that the fast pace often means long hours, and cites the Employment Act ceiling of 48 working hours a week with exceptions. The newcomer should plan for a workplace where politeness is elaborate and the day is not short.

## Singapore

Singapore scores lower than Malaysia on power distance, at 74 in the same Rework table, with individualism at 20, but it has a harder edge on formality than its climate would suggest. A 2017 YouGov survey of more than 9,000 workers across Asia Pacific, reported by Human Resources Online, found that only 39 percent considered shorts acceptable on men at work, while 87 percent accepted going tieless. Singapore was among the more tolerant locations on leggings, at 67 percent acceptable, whereas Malaysia was among those finding them unacceptable at 52 percent.

The tie has gone, though not the trousers. Mothership notes that in the 1960s Lee Kuan Yew allowed civil servants to drop jackets and ties for short-sleeved shirts, and that Singapore police wore shorts with button-up shirts until 1969. A May 2026 column in the same outlet argued that if Singaporeans must raise their air conditioning by at least one degree, shorts should become the office default, citing Meteorological Service Singapore data showing a decadal temperature rise of about 0.25 degrees. The column also noted that Tokyo's government had recently urged office workers towards shorts so that offices could run warmer. It remains a proposal in Singapore, and one that would require a great many people to agree about knees.

Formality is also sectoral. A Stomp feature on Birkenstocks at work quoted a civil servant who found casual footwear sloppy and a software employee who thought casual dress encourages casual behaviour, against a tech worker who said comfortable clothes help her focus. ManpowerGroup's Linda Teo said that industry, company culture and role decide what is appropriate, and that even traditionally formal sectors are loosening. The accurate summary is that Singapore is formal by default, relaxed by negotiation and entirely willing to tell you which is which if you ask in advance. The Independent Singapore's account of the new accounting hire shows what happens otherwise: a formal firm, a casual assumption and a first day spent revising expectations. FlyerTalk contributors, meanwhile, agree that local men generally wear long trousers with short-sleeved shirts, and warn that indoor air conditioning can be cold enough to require a layer.

## The Reckoning

The two countries disagree less about trousers than about the source of the rule. In Malaysia the rule is communal and often official: the department issues a circular, the company has a code, the batik day appears on the calendar, and compliance is a way of showing respect to the people around you. In Singapore the rule is local and pragmatic: the employer decides, the sector adjusts, the weather is blamed, and compliance is a way of not being noticed. Neither is more rigid. One is merely more public. The Hofstede gap, 100 against 74 on power distance, points the same way: in Malaysia, dress also signals position and deference, while in Singapore it signals fit with the sector and the employer.

For the newcomer, the practical consequence is where to look. In Kuala Lumpur, look at the calendar and the staff photographs, then add modesty. In Singapore, look at the sector and the first-day email, then add a cardigan. Both cities punish the person who confidently guesses, and both forgive the one who asks. Seasoned expats on Expat.com add a related warning: do not correct a senior colleague, even indirectly, and keep presentations simple for senior audiences. Formality in Malaysia is a whole-day practice, not a garment, and the person in the correct batik who corrects the director in a meeting will still have dressed wrongly.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/SGexams — A new accounting graduate said he disliked his firm after one day, partly because he was expected to dress formally every day when he had assumed it would be relaxed, and admitted he should have asked beforehand. Commenters sympathised, said adjustment takes a few months, and one said their own workplace allowed shorts and singlets.*

> *Expat.com (Kuala Lumpur) — A resident advised that most employers have a dress code, often with logos on unisex, men-leaning cuts. Her advice for women was to check the employer's website for what staff really wear, keep sleeves to at least three-quarters length and avoid see-through fabric, because modesty is strongly valued.*

> *Expat.com (Malaysia) — An expat on business etiquette warned never to correct someone, even indirectly, because the most senior person usually explains the problem and others agree. He added that presentations should grow simpler as the audience grows more senior.*

> *FlyerTalk (Singapore) — Posters mostly agreed that local men wear long trousers and short-sleeved shirts. One said jeans are too hot, another said shorts are fine for leisure but offices call for long pants and often a shirt and tie.*

> *Tales Magazine post reports (Kuala Lumpur) — Respondents describe offices as business casual to business attire depending on the employer. Older reports expect shirts and ties, while a 2025 respondent says men can wear untucked batik shirts with slacks and still look dressy.*

## Conclusion

Pack for the sector, not the stereotype. In Malaysia, bring modest, formal basics, a batik shirt or tunic for the designated days and a courteous way of addressing seniors. In Singapore, bring lightweight long trousers, a layer for the freezing offices and the discipline to ask about attire before the first morning. Employers in either country will tell you what is expected if you ask, and will remember if you do not.

The honest summary is that Malaysia dresses to honour the room and Singapore dresses to survive it. Both will judge your footwear.
