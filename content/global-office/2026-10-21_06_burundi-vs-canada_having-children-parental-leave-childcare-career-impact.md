**Burundi Gives New Mothers 98 Days and a Mandate to Rest. Canada Gives Them Up to 69 Weeks and a 34% Pay Cut That Never Fully Goes Away.**

🇧🇮 Burundi · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Burundian mother gets 49 days of fully paid leave before birth and 49 after — 98 days total, full salary, no negotiation — and then returns to a childcare market so expensive that a single daycare spot in a nice part of Bujumbura can cost more than she earns in a month. A Canadian mother can stretch parental leave to 69 weeks between both parents, heavily subsidized by the state, and still watch her long-run earnings settle 34 percent below where they'd have been without the child, a decade later, according to research controlling for education and occupation. Burundi compresses the crisis into fourteen weeks and lets the childcare market do the rest of the damage. Canada spreads the crisis out over sixteen months and the following decade.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇮 Burundi

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect maternity leave to be short (49 days pre-birth, 49 post) but paid in full — plan your finances around full salary, not partial | Expect 98 days to feel like enough; even mothers who receive it describe it as leaving "not much time to rest" |
| Budget for private childcare as a major expense if you're in a professional, urban job — a single spot in a good Bujumbura district can exceed a typical monthly salary | Assume state schools are a safe default; many professional parents choose costly private schools specifically because public education quality is considered poor |
| Arrange a live-in housekeeper or family support early if possible — colleagues without one or without nearby family describe genuinely struggling to manage | Expect your job to wait patiently through any employer disruption; one returning mother considered herself lucky simply not to have been fired after a company restructuring |
| Expect breastfeeding accommodation (one paid hour daily) only for the first six months, not longer | Assume single motherhood carries the same support structure as a two-parent household — it is explicitly described as the harder path here |
| Note the national fertility rate (still nearly 4.8 births per woman) when thinking about extended-family norms — bigger families and shared caregiving are the cultural default, not the exception | Expect a father's presence at the birth to be the norm; one mother called her husband attending all five of their children's births "rather an exception" |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the two leave options clearly before choosing: standard (up to 40 weeks shared, 55% of pay) or extended (up to 69 weeks shared, 33% of pay) — you cannot switch once started | Assume generous leave length means generous leave pay; the maximum weekly benefit tops out at $729, far below most professional salaries |
| Expect your earnings to drop sharply the year of birth (averaging 49% in research) and to recover only partially, long-term, especially with a second or third child | Assume the penalty is temporary; the long-run earnings penalty 5–10 years out averages 34% nationally, and nearly 47% for parents of three or more children |
| Ask whether your employer is in Quebec — fathers there now claim benefits at a 92.9% rate, versus 31.3% elsewhere in Canada, which changes real household dynamics | Assume subsidized daycare is universal; it is heavily concentrated in Quebec, where it has demonstrably cut the long-run motherhood penalty from 40% to 23% |
| Push hard on childcare access specifically if you're outside Quebec — it is the single policy lever shown to most reduce the long-term career cost of parenthood | Treat a post-leave layoff as necessarily unrelated to timing; workers who are let go shortly after returning from leave commonly, and reasonably, suspect the connection |
| Expect women's share of family income to keep climbing (now about 40% in dual-earner couples with children) even amid the penalty — progress and penalty are coexisting, not sequential | Assume unpaid domestic labour has equalized; women aged 25–44 still average roughly two more unpaid hours daily on children and household tasks than men |

---

Burundi's system is blunt and fast: a legally mandated 49-days-before, 49-days-after maternity leave at full pay, with no means-testing and no employer discretion, according to accounts from women who've used it. The brutality shows up afterward, in the childcare market rather than the leave policy itself — a spot at an upscale Bujumbura daycare can run to roughly 500,000 Burundian francs a month, more than many professional salaries, in a country where the fertility rate, while falling steadily from 5.48 in 2017 to 4.79 in 2024, still sits more than double the global average of 2.38. Burundi has no individually published Hofstede cultural-dimension score, so there's no tidy cultural-values shorthand here; what the on-the-ground accounts do describe is a system that leans hard on extended family and paid domestic help precisely because the formal childcare market and the formal leave period are both too thin to carry the load alone.

Canada's system is the inverse: slow, generous on paper, and quietly punishing in the data over the following decade. Standard parental leave runs up to 40 weeks shared between parents at 55 percent of earnings (capped at $729 weekly); extended parental leave stretches to 69 weeks at 33 percent. CIBC Economics found the average earnings penalty for mothers of young children sits at 18 percent in the child's early years, with a long-run penalty — controlling for education and occupation — averaging 34 percent nationally five to ten years out, rising to 47 percent for parents of three or more children. Canada's Hofstede individualism score of 80 pairs oddly with a system this state-subsidized; the leave itself is collectively funded through Employment Insurance, even as the long-term career cost is absorbed almost entirely by the individual parent, usually the mother.

### The Reckoning

The real divergence is where each country puts the pain. Burundi concentrates it at the very start — a short, intense leave window, followed immediately by an expensive, improvised childcare scramble that disproportionately punishes single mothers and anyone without family nearby. Canada spreads the pain across sixteen months of reduced income and then, invisibly, across the following decade of a shrinking paycheck that most new parents don't see coming until the research tells them it happened. Quebec is the natural experiment proving the point: its subsidized daycare expansion cut the province's long-run motherhood penalty nearly in half, from 40 percent to 23 percent, simply by attacking the actual bottleneck — childcare cost and availability — rather than extending leave length, which the rest of Canada has mostly done instead.

Neither country has solved what a child costs a parent's career. Burundi never pretended to try past the leave itself; Canada built a research literature proving its own fix is incomplete.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Burundi with children, the real question isn't the leave policy — it's genuinely fine — it's whether you have household help or family nearby to absorb what comes after it, since state support stops at week fourteen and the market picks up from there, expensively. If you're doing the same in Canada, the question is whether you're in Quebec, since that jurisdictional accident of policy is worth roughly 17 points of your future earnings. Ask less about how long the leave is, in either country, and more about who pays for the years after it — because in both places, disproportionately, it's still the mother.
