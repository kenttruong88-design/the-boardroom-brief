# No Boasting in Oslo, No Strangers in Jakarta: Two Theories of How Colleagues Become a Team

**🇳🇴 Norway · 🇮🇩 Indonesia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Norway builds workplace bonding by making sure nobody stands out; Indonesia builds it by making sure nobody stands alone. Both produce genuinely cohesive teams, and both will initially feel, to the wrong kind of newcomer, like something is missing — the Norwegian office for its lack of overt warmth, the Indonesian one for its lack of personal space. Neither read is fair, and both are common enough to be worth correcting before your first week.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Downplay individual wins and credit the team — Janteloven runs deep even in modern offices | Boast about a personal accomplishment in a meeting; it reads as a genuine social misstep, not confidence |
| Be the one to initiate friendships — Norwegians warm up slowly but sincerely | Wait for colleagues to invite you first; the initiative is expected to come from the newcomer |
| Expect a genuinely flat structure — leadership chatting with cleaning staff is normal, not performative | Assume flatness means the org chart doesn't matter; decisions still happen, just quietly |
| Use the 37.5-hour week and flexible hours as they're intended — leaving on time is not a lack of commitment | Schedule after-work bonding late; Norwegian colleagues protect personal time fiercely |
| Take outdoor team activities seriously as real bonding time, not a novelty offsite | Skip them assuming they're optional box-ticking — participation quietly signals buy-in |

#### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles — *Pak* (Mr.) or *Bu* (Mrs./Ms.) plus first name — from day one | Skip titles assuming first-name informality; it reads as disrespectful, even rudely so |
| Join the WhatsApp "WorkFam" group and actual birthday potlucks — they're the real social infrastructure | Treat group chats and office celebrations as optional extras separate from "real" work |
| Let the most senior person speak last in your first all-hands meeting | Jump in with your own opinion before hierarchy has had its say — it reads as impatience, not initiative |
| Read tone and context around a "yes" — it can mean agreement, acknowledgment, or polite deflection | Take every "yes" as a literal commitment; confirm important agreements a second, gentler way |
| Expect gotong royong — colleagues quietly covering for each other under deadline pressure | Assume that collective effort means no one is individually accountable — it's cooperation, not indifference |

### Norway: Bonding Through Equality, Not Exposure

Norwegian workplace bonding runs on Janteloven, the unwritten social code against standing out or boasting, which functions less as an insecurity and more as a deliberate leveling mechanism: team achievement gets celebrated more readily than individual credit, and flat hierarchies mean a company's top leader might genuinely stop to chat with the person cleaning the break room. Norway consistently ranks among the world's highest-trust societies, and that trust is the actual substrate bonding gets built on — not forced fun, but the quiet, accumulated confidence that colleagues will treat you fairly without needing to perform warmth to prove it.

The catch for newcomers, well documented across expat accounts, is that Norwegians take real time to warm up, and the burden of initiating typically falls on the new arrival rather than the existing team. Outdoor group activities — a hike, a ski trip, a shared cabin weekend — carry more genuine bonding weight here than a standard office happy hour would elsewhere, because they strip away hierarchy in a way an indoor meeting room can't. Skipping them isn't a neutral choice; it reads as opting out of the team's actual social contract.

### Indonesia: Bonding Through Family, Hierarchy, and Presence

Indonesian workplace culture runs on two intertwined values: *rukun* (harmony) and *gotong royong* (mutual, communal effort), which together produce what one first-person account memorably described as an org chart structured "like a family WhatsApp group with grandparents at the top, then parents, then cousins" — hierarchy and warmth operating simultaneously rather than in tension. Hofstede data backs the structural half of that picture: Indonesia scores 78 on power distance, among the more hierarchical profiles globally, paired with a collectivism score that puts group loyalty well ahead of individual goals.

The bonding half shows up in the details newcomers often mistake for informal extras: birthday potlucks, group selfies, and WhatsApp groups literally named things like "WorkFam" aren't peripheral to the job, they're load-bearing social infrastructure. *Gotong royong* means colleagues quietly absorb each other's overflow under deadline pressure without being asked, a form of mutual aid that can look, to someone from a more individually accountable culture, like blurred responsibility rather than what it actually is: insurance against anyone being left exposed alone.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures solve for the same underlying problem — making sure no one on the team feels isolated — but they solve it with opposite tools. Norway removes hierarchy to produce equality, and treats visible modesty as the social glue. Indonesia keeps hierarchy fully intact and treats visible warmth — potlucks, titles, group chats — as the social glue, with the hierarchy itself functioning as a source of care (the senior person speaks last partly so juniors aren't overshadowed) rather than pure control. An employee who reads "flat" as "informal" in Norway will misjudge how much unspoken structure remains; an employee who reads "hierarchical" as "cold" in Indonesia will miss how much genuine family-style warmth is doing the actual bonding work underneath the formality.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering a question about integrating into Norwegian society, one respondent wrote that foreigners often struggle at first not because Norwegians are unfriendly but because friendship there requires active pursuit — waiting to be invited, the standard approach in many other cultures, can mean waiting indefinitely.*

> *Quora — A separate thread on Norwegian work culture described the flat hierarchy as more than symbolic: a poster recalled a company's top executive stopping mid-hallway to ask a cleaner about her weekend, treating it as unremarkable rather than a gesture for visitors to notice.*

> *Blind — In a thread titled simply "team camaraderie," one tech worker described a team where "everything is just a transaction," with colleagues operating on an unspoken "you do this, then only I'll do that" basis and no one asking how anyone's day was going — team-building events, they noted, hadn't moved the needle after more than a year of trying, a useful reminder that bonding culture has to be structural, not scheduled.*

> *Medium (Daffa Ghiffary Kusuma, "Indonesian work culture and management styles") — Described a first all-hands meeting where letting the most senior person speak last, rather than first, earned instant credibility with the room, and warned that a flat "yes" in a meeting could mean genuine agreement, polite acknowledgment, or quiet disagreement depending entirely on tone and context a newcomer hasn't yet learned to read.*

### Conclusion

Pick Norway if what you actually want is a team that leaves you alone to do good work and trusts you to seek out connection on your own schedule — just know the seeking is on you. Pick Indonesia if you want a team that pulls you in from day one whether you asked for it or not, titles and potlucks included — just know the hierarchy that makes the warmth possible isn't going anywhere. The thing I'd tell a friend weighing the two: in Norway, silence usually means respect; in Indonesia, silence in a group chat usually means something's wrong. Learn to read the quiet correctly and you'll be fine either way.
