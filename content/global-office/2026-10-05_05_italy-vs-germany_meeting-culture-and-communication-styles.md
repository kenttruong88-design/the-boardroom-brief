**The Meeting Starts When It Starts: Communication Culture in Italy and Germany**

**🇮🇹 Italy · 🇩🇪 Germany**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Italy, a 10am meeting is a loose suggestion that becomes real somewhere between 10:15 and "after this phone call." In Germany, 10am means 10am, the agenda will be followed in order, and your idea will be taken apart in front of the room — not because anyone dislikes you, but because that's what a good idea is for. Both countries consider themselves direct. Only one of them means it about the clock.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build in buffer time — meetings routinely start late and run long, and treating that as a personal insult will exhaust you fast | Expect an agenda to be followed rigidly; ideas develop organically, and improvisation is normal, not sloppy |
| Use the formal "Lei" with superiors and new professional contacts until invited to switch | Default to casual physical greetings (hugs, cheek kisses) in a business setting — handshakes are the professional norm |
| Let heated, animated debate happen in meetings — it signals engagement with ideas, not a breakdown in the room | Mistake raised voices or passionate disagreement for a relationship actually souring |
| Invest time in meals with colleagues and clients — real business gets concluded over a long lunch as often as at a desk | Rush straight to the agenda item without any relationship-building small talk first |
| Dress sharply; appearance reads as a signal of competence and respect, not vanity | Assume southern and northern offices run identically — expect more structure and pace up north, more relationship focus down south |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive early and expect the agenda to run in order, on time, to the minute | Read direct criticism of your idea as a personal attack — Germans separate the critique from the person delivering or receiving it |
| Prepare thoroughly before offering an opinion — half-formed ideas invite exactly the scrutiny you're not ready for | Expect praise to soften the feedback; German feedback culture tends to foreground what's wrong before what's working |
| Say what you mean plainly; vague hedging reads as evasive, not polite | Take a quiet, process-heavy decision cycle as paralysis — it's deliberate, not broken |
| Respect the written rules, even the unwritten social ones (quiet hours, recycling norms) — enforcement of fairness runs through the whole culture, not just the office | Treat German bluntness as cruelty; reframe it as the system's version of respect — it assumes you can handle the truth |
| Separate your personal life from work socializing — the boundary is real and generally welcomed | Overshare at a work social event; warmth builds slowly and in its own designated spaces |

### Italy: The Clock Is a Suggestion, the Debate Is Not

Italian meeting culture runs on two things that look contradictory and aren't: a clear, centralized hierarchy and genuinely loose process around time. Commisceo Global's management guide notes that "decisions are centralised and taken by the persons positioned at the top" — employees expect direction from above and generally welcome it rather than resenting it — while the meetings themselves can be loud and improvisational: "heated debates and arguments often erupt in meetings; this is simply a function of the free flow of ideas," not a sign anything has gone wrong.

Punctuality is where the stereotype earns its keep. The Local's guide to Italian business etiquette is blunt about it: Italy has adopted what one etiquette consultant called a "slapdash attitude" toward meeting start times, and the polite form of address — "Lei" rather than the informal "Tu" — follows "very precise rules, according to hierarchical relationships" even as the schedule around those same hierarchies stays loose. Food does real business work here: "often in Italy the most important business is concluded at table over a glass of wine or good food," which functions less as a perk and more as an actual negotiating venue. One long-term foreign resident's account of Italian professional life adds a less flattering layer underneath the charm: certain sectors run on an "intellectual class system" where accent and formal titles ("Maestra," "professoressa") shape how seriously colleagues take you — formality and improvisation coexisting, again, without much friction.

### Germany: The Feedback Is the Respect

German communication culture inverts the Italian formula: rigid time, loose hierarchy of opinion. Meetings start when scheduled and proceed through a prepared agenda, and critical, specific feedback is treated as the normal cost of participating, not an aggressive outlier. One Australian expat's account of his early German team meetings described a "barrage of pointed questions and critical remarks" aimed squarely at his ideas — jarring at first, until his partner explained the local operating principle plainly: "Here, we don't sugarcoat our feedback." He eventually came to see directness as a form of collaborative problem-solving rather than hostility, "a new dance that took practice."

A longer-term resident's account pushes the same point further, with sharper humor: German feedback among Germans themselves can look "like gladiators beating each other senseless" — brutal, but evenly matched and expected. For a foreigner on the receiving end, though, the dynamic can feel lopsided, "like watching a naked person fighting a tank," because the directness arrives without the shared cultural context that makes it feel fair among locals. The same account describes a native-English-speaking friend who taught an entire well-received lesson and received exactly one piece of feedback afterward: criticism of her accent — evidence, in the author's view, of a genuine cultural pull toward finding the flaw first, regardless of overall performance. None of this reads, to Germans, as negativity for its own sake; it's consistent with a broader social expectation — enforced down to Sunday laundry-drying rules in some neighborhoods — that fairness and correctness matter more than anyone's immediate comfort.

### The Reckoning

The real inversion: Italy is loose about structure (time, agenda, process) and tight about hierarchy (who ultimately decides); Germany is tight about structure and comparatively loose about hierarchy — anyone's idea, including a junior's, can get torn apart in a meeting precisely because rank doesn't exempt you from scrutiny. A newcomer who expects "Northern European" to mean one coherent style will be surprised that Germany's rigor is about process and honesty, not about softening the blow for people lower on the org chart.

The other divergence worth noting: both cultures consider themselves plainspoken, but about different things. Italians will tell you exactly how they feel about an idea in the room, loudly, while treating the clock as a flexible courtesy. Germans will tell you exactly what's wrong with your work, specifically, while treating the schedule as close to sacred. If you need brutal honesty about your output, book the German meeting. If you need the meeting to actually start near the time it says on the invite, don't book the Italian one.

### Conclusion

If you want your meetings to start on time and your work critiqued with surgical precision, move to Germany and build a thicker skin before you land — the feedback isn't personal, but it will feel personal for a while. If you want your ideas debated passionately and your schedule treated as a rough draft, Italy will deliver both, generously, often over lunch. Pick based on what actually unsettles you more: hearing exactly what's wrong with your work, or never quite knowing when the meeting will actually begin.
