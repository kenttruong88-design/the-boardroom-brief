# Nepal Hands You a Letter and Wishes You Luck; Japan Hands You a Manual and a Senpai

🇳🇵 Nepal · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On the first of April, tens of thousands of Japanese graduates walk into a ceremony, receive weeks of group training, and are assigned a more senior colleague to worry about them. In Nepal, according to a local human-resources magazine, new hires often receive an appointment letter with no terms of reference and are left to discover the job by trial, error and watching whoever seems busiest. Both systems claim to be teaching newcomers how things are done. Only one has written it down.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for your terms of reference and a written contract in your first week. | Assume a missing job description means the employer has nothing in mind. |
| Identify, early, one senior colleague who will actually answer questions, and ask politely for their time. | Wait for a formal mentor to be assigned. |
| Learn the unwritten hierarchy by watching who is deferred to in meetings. | Challenge a senior in public during your first month. |
| Keep your own record of tasks given and hours worked. | Rely on the Labour Act's eight-hour day being enforced as written. |
| Ask what success looks like at three and six months. | Wait for feedback to arrive unprompted. |
| Offer something in return, such as digital skills, since younger staff are often valued for them. | Treat seniority as a dead letter because the office looks modern. |

### In Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend the group induction fully, including the sessions on greetings, business cards and reporting. | Treat generic manners training as beneath a specialist. |
| Follow the manual first, then propose changes through your senpai. | Rewrite a procedure in your first month because it seems inefficient. |
| Report progress early and often to your supervisor. | Wait until a task is finished to mention it. |
| Use the mentor system for career worries, since the mentor is usually from another division. | Bring daily task questions to the mentor when your own team can answer them. |
| Learn to read indirect feedback, because a gentle "we will consider it" can mean no. | Expect blunt correction, which seniors increasingly avoid. |
| Ask whether training time is paid before you sign. | Assume it is, because one foreign employee reports it was not. |

## Nepal

Nepal's onboarding problem is mainly a structural one. According to the ILO, as cited in the Kathmandu Post in October 2026, more than 86 per cent of Nepal's workers were in informal employment as of December 2025, and the 2017/18 Labour Force Survey put the figure at 84.6 per cent. About 78 per cent of workers in registered firms held informal jobs in 2018. A formal induction programme is a luxury for an employer who has not issued a contract. The National Statistics Office is currently fielding its fourth Labour Force Survey, with results expected in August 2027; the last completed one found unemployment of 11.4 per cent.

What practitioners describe is consistent with this. The HRM Nepal magazine says leaders often undermine the need for induction, handing over appointment letters without clear terms of reference. It describes a teacher who received no performance feedback all year and was dismissed at the end of it. The Kathmandu Post reported in 2019 that a Nepal Economic Forum study on the startup ecosystem listed a lack of mentors as the biggest constraint, above financing, though the column gives no sample size. A banking-sector writer in HRM Nepal describes workplaces in which influence follows rank and long tenure signals trust, and in which younger staff leave for employers who respect their purpose and autonomy.

The mentor, then, is a person one finds, not a programme one joins. This can work well where a senior colleague is generous, and it can work badly in the other cases. The Record, a Nepali investigative magazine, reported in 2021 on young workers who went months without contracts, worked beyond a nine-hour day and were told that exposure would pay the bills. It added that the Labour Act's limit of eight hours a day and 48 hours a week, with overtime at 150 per cent, is followed by very few organisations. The cultural mechanism is deference. The Record's reporting notes that norms of deference discourage young workers from challenging employers, and that many do not know their rights. Where nobody has told a new hire what the job is, the only available instruction is imitation, and imitation transmits habits along with skills. None of this describes every Nepali employer, and the larger banks and international organisations in Kathmandu run more orderly induction. It does describe the default.

## Japan

Japan's system is the reverse: a great deal of structure, applied to everyone at once. Reporting by AFP in 2015 put the share of new university and high school graduates starting work at the April intake at about 90 per cent. The Japan Times has described large companies running broad training for the whole cohort, covering conduct and polite business language, with role-specific skills picked up afterwards through on-the-job training. A foreign employee interviewed by the paper, expecting the week of mentorship familiar from a Chicago consulting firm, was instead introduced to colleagues and left to work out the duties.

Mentorship, where it exists, is formalised. Robert Walters Japan notes that the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare publishes a manual on adopting a mentor system, that mentors are usually from a different division to avoid conflicts of interest, and that they are ideally in their third year or later. The attraction is plain in the numbers. According to the ministry data reproduced by nippon.com, 32.0 per cent of university graduates in the March 2016 cohort left their first job within three years, and the figure was 57.7 per cent at companies of five or fewer employees against 25.0 per cent at firms of more than 1,000. A mentor is a cheap response to a costly resignation. Larger employers invest heavily in the start. One Japan-based writer describes a friend at a major bank spending about two months in training, then several months at a branch greeting customers, a sequence the writer reads as instruction in composure. Newcomers interviewed by Unseen Japan found the detail thorough, from business card etiquette to progress reporting, and sometimes oppressive, since manuals and repeated confirmations feel like constant documentation to those used to individual discretion. Pebl, an international employment firm, adds that onboarding in Japan front-loads relationships, through shared meals and project work, and advises against rushed introductions.

The system is also fraying. A Recruit Management Solutions survey, reported by HRM Asia in April 2024, found about 70 per cent of senior staff uneasy about guiding new hires, and 16.3 per cent admitting they pretended not to notice problems. Writing in East Asia Forum in July 2026, Miyo Tanaka observed that firms keep the April intake and its induction while dropping the job security that once justified it, and noted that Japan ranked 28th of 38 OECD economies on productivity in 2024.

## The Reckoning

The contrast is almost too neat. Nepal asks a newcomer to find their own mentor in an economy where the employer's obligations are frequently unwritten. Japan assigns one and hopes the senior is still willing to criticise anything. For a Nepali professional heading to Japan, the shock is not the hierarchy, which is familiar, but the formality: the manual, the reporting cadence and the expectation that every question has been anticipated by someone.

Hofstede's published scores for Japan are 95 on masculinity and 88 on long-term orientation, which fits a culture that invests for years in people it expects to stay. Hofstede's well-known country tables do not, as far as I could find, include a score for Nepal, so any comparison on that basis would be invented, and this newspaper declines to invent.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TokyoDev forum — Entry-level training tends to cover a company's own product, not general skills, and a firm that hires people with no programming background may be demanding for a reason. Career changers without Japanese or experience find few roles and heavy competition.*

> *SoraNews24 reader survey — Of seven foreign employees surveyed, three praised welcoming colleagues who explained everything despite weak Japanese, and four felt treated as guests, with lower pay or less valued opinions. One said new hires were not paid during training.*

> *The Japan Times — A foreign banker who had a week of mentorship at a Chicago firm was simply introduced around a Tokyo office and left to work out duties alone, while the real training happened later, on the job.*

> *The Record — A 23-year-old Nepali programme coordinator went seven months without a contract, was dismissed after declining duties outside her job description, and was paid less than a male colleague in the same role.*

> *eJable interview — A Nepali engineer at a Japanese company praises colleagues as generous with time and committed to quality, notes that visa paperwork was handled for him, and finds everyday life harder than work because of the language.*

## Conclusion

Anyone moving from Nepal to Japan should treat the first ninety days as an examination in procedure. Attend every session, learn the reporting rhythm, and use the mentor for career questions, not for rescuing a deadline. Anyone moving in the other direction should ask three things at the offer stage: whether there is a written contract, who will answer questions, and what feedback looks like in month three. In Nepal, the answers will tell you more about the employer than any careers page.

Neither country offers a perfect start. Japan will tell you everything, repeatedly, and ask you to confirm. Nepal will tell you almost nothing and call it trust.
