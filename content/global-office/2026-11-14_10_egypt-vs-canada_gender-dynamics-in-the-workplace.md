# Egypt Has the Graduates, Canada Has the Spreadsheet: Women at Work, Two Ways

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Women make up about 53 percent of Egypt's university graduates and 29 percent of its workforce, according to an analysis of 2025 WUZZUF platform data. In Canada, Statistics Canada can tell you that women's average hourly wage was $32.77 in 2024 against $37.50 for men, a gap of 12.6 percent, measured to a decimal place and published with a catalogue number. Egypt wastes talent the way a warehouse wastes space: quietly, and at scale. Canada measures its waste to the cent, which is not the same as having stopped.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect questions about marriage or children in some interviews, and decide in advance how to answer | Assume Canadian-style norms on interview questions apply everywhere |
| Dress to the neighbourhood and the office; conservative cover is the safer default | Treat a Cairo business district like a resort |
| Learn your rights under the 2014 harassment law and the new Labor Law, in force since September 2025 | Rely on slow courts as your first line of defence |
| Ask about maternity terms; the law now provides 120 days, up from 90 | Assume the private-sector contract matches the statute without reading it |
| Build allies among colleagues, including senior women, with realistic expectations | Take for granted that women managers will mentor women staff |
| Ask whether the employer holds the Egyptian Gender Equity Seal | Accept a vague reference to "company image" as a reason without noting it |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether the employer is federally regulated and subject to the Pay Equity Act | Assume a legal duty to review pay means pay is already equal |
| Document unclear incidents, such as being passed over or left out of meetings | Dismiss ambiguous slights as oversensitivity |
| Use HR and written channels; Canadian workplaces expect process | Rely on informal goodwill to fix a pay discrepancy |
| Compare pay for your education level; gaps persist at every level | Take a higher education as a guarantee of parity |
| Ask directly about parental leave and return-to-work policies | Assume Canadian experience will be waived for a foreign résumé |
| Ask about mentorship and sponsorship, not just policy statements | Mistake a diversity page for a promotion pipeline |

## Egypt

The headline figure depends on who is counting. The World Bank, in a March 2025 feature, put female labor force participation in Egypt at about 18 percent, against 73 percent for men, and estimated that closing the employment gap could lift the economy by around 56 percent. CAPMAS, in preliminary 2025 Labour Force Survey figures reported by Business Today Egypt, put women's participation at 20.7 percent, up from 16.9 percent in 2024, with women's unemployment at 15.3 percent against 3.7 percent for men. The two sources differ in year and method. They agree on the direction of the problem and are less in agreement on its size.

The barriers are partly legal and mostly practical. The World Bank lists unsafe transport, weak private-sector protections, childcare costs, school hours that clash with work hours, and social norms, and notes that 24 percent of men do not support women working at all. A 2014 law criminalises sexual harassment, yet the same feature quotes an advocate saying slow judicial processes leave women feeling unprotected. The new Labor Law, which took effect on 1 September 2025, raises maternity leave to 120 days from 90 and lets women take it three times in a career, according to Jurist. It also targets harassment and bullying at work.

There is a hiring problem in the data too. WUZZUF's 2025 analysis, reported by Business Today Egypt, found women are 41 percent of job seekers but submit 29 percent of applications, apply to an average of 10.6 jobs against 14 for men, and see female-targeted advertised roles averaging EGP 13,000 against EGP 18,000 for male-targeted roles and EGP 19,000 for gender-neutral ones. Employers, notably, were slightly more responsive to women who did apply. Only 7 percent of postings offered remote work, while 18 percent of women said they preferred it fully. The talent is present. The invitation is selective. Women who do secure work fare reasonably on formality: the same CAPMAS figures show 85.0 percent of employed women in permanent jobs and 53.3 percent covered by social insurance, against 34.3 percent of employed men. The World Bank also points to the Egyptian Gender Equity Seal, which certifies private firms with sound recruitment, work-family balance and harassment policies. Such a seal is a useful signal for a job seeker, though a voluntary one.

## Canada

Canada's version of the problem is smaller, better documented and more resistant to a slogan. Statistics Canada reports that the hourly gap narrowed to 12.6 percent in 2024 from 16.5 percent in 2006, as women's wages grew 21.3 percent against 15.9 percent for men. The gap is not a function of education: women with a bachelor's degree or higher earned $41.55 against $48.26 for men, a 13.9 percent gap. A separate Statistics Canada release puts women in management at 39.0 percent in 2023, up from 27.1 percent in 1987, and women's labour force participation at 61.2 percent in 2024. The same release records an executive pay gap of 32.1 percent in total compensation.

The law has more machinery than Egypt's, and the machinery is still warming up. The federal Pay Equity Act covers about 5,000 federally regulated employers and 1.4 million employees, according to the Pay Equity Commissioner's 2025-2026 report. That report records 643 annual statements received, about 12 percent of covered employers, of which 129 reported owing pay increases to women averaging $2.37 an hour. It is a rare case of a statistic being both reassuring and mildly alarming. Outside the payroll, Statistics Canada reports that 20.9 percent of private-sector businesses were majority-owned by women in the third quarter of 2025, which suggests that some women have concluded the quicker route to equal pay is to sign the cheque.

The subtler difficulty is the unprovable slight. Reporting in the Globe and Mail on a 2023 study in Sociological Science, based on interviews with 31 professional women and a survey of 600, found ambiguous incidents are more common than overt discrimination, and that women who cannot tell whether gender was a factor tend to turn inward and alter their own behaviour. For immigrants the picture compounds. CIC News, citing the Labour Force Survey, reported unemployment of 15.2 percent for recent immigrant women against 8.0 percent for Canadian-born women in the first half of 2021.

## The Reckoning

The two countries pose different questions. Egypt asks whether women will be admitted to the labour market in the first place, and the answer, per CAPMAS and the World Bank, is improving from a low base. Canada asks why women admitted in large numbers, and with the degrees, continue to earn about 12 to 14 percent less per hour. In Egypt the obstacles are overt: transport, childcare, an interviewer's question about marriage, an advertisement that prefers a particular look. In Canada they are procedural and quiet: a promotion that did not happen, a meeting that did not include you, a pay review that is under way.

Hofstede scores, as tabulated by secondary sources and dating from decades of survey work, add a rough frame. Egypt scores 80 on power distance and 52 on masculinity, with 68 on uncertainty avoidance, while Canada scores 39, 52 and 48 respectively. The masculinity score is identical which is a useful reminder that a single number cannot describe a workplace. What the power distance gap does suggest is that the practical route to redress differs: in Canada you file, document and escalate, and in Egypt you depend more on the person above you, on the law's slow courts, and on the employer's own seal of good conduct.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Egyptian Streets — A woman in her first job at a multinational's Cairo office wrote that her interview included questions about marriage and children, and that, to her disappointment, some female colleagues said they preferred male bosses and criticised women leaders more harshly than men.*

> *Egyptian Streets — Reporting on hijabi women described job advertisements asking for non-veiled candidates, particularly in customer-facing roles, and one woman who said a bank interviewer suggested she could be considered if she removed her scarf, after which HR barred that interviewer from hiring decisions.*

> *Expat.com Egypt forum — In an old thread, one poster described a large factory with no women among its workers, while another saw hiring preferences, such as secretarial work going to women, but no pay difference. Both stressed they were describing single workplaces.*

> *Reddit r/ImmigrationCanada — In a March 2024 thread, none of the commenters discussed gender, but a former UK government worker said Canadian workplaces expect employees to visibly show they are working hard, and others warned that foreign experience counts for little with some employers.*

> *Rank and File — One Canadian woman said that after being promoted to weekend manager at a restaurant she later learned a male co-worker in a non-management role earned two dollars an hour more than she did; another recalled being the only woman in an oil and gas boardroom in the 1990s, where colleagues argued against hiring a female engineer.*

## Conclusion

For a woman weighing the two, the choice is between visible obstacles and invisible ones. In Egypt, research the specific employer, ask about the Gender Equity Seal, read the contract against the new Labor Law, understand local dress and transport norms, and treat a multinational or a firm with explicit policies as meaningfully different from an average workplace. In Canada, assume the law is a floor, document everything that feels off, ask about pay equity and parental leave before accepting, and expect to negotiate for your own pay.

Neither country will hand you a level field on arrival. Egypt is more candid about the slope, and Canada has better surveying equipment.
