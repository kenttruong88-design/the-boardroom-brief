# Blunt in Amsterdam, Warm in Buenos Aires: Two Ways to Hear You're Underperforming

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇦🇷 Argentina**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Amsterdam, a manager will tell you your project missed the mark in the same tone she'd use to tell you it's raining, and she will consider this a courtesy. In Buenos Aires, a manager may spend the first ten minutes of your review asking about your family before mentioning, gently, that a few things need work — and he, too, will consider this a courtesy. Both are being honest. Only one of them will make a newly arrived employee cry in the bathroom, and it's rarely the one you'd guess.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat blunt feedback as respect, not hostility — Dutch colleagues give it because they take you seriously | Read a critical comment as personal; it's about the work, delivered flat on purpose |
| Push back on your manager's idea directly if you disagree — flat hierarchy expects it | Soften your own feedback so much that it reads as evasive or insincere |
| Ask for feedback often — Dutch teams increasingly use structured tools and expect regular check-ins | Expect a warm preamble before the point; "doe normaal" (just act normal) means getting to it |
| Say what you actually think in a review, even about your manager | Mistake first-name informality for a lack of real performance standards |
| Give yourself a few weeks to stop flinching at directness — most newcomers adjust fast | Assume tight-knit Dutch social circles at work equal an easy path to close friendships outside it |

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real relationship-building — *personalismo* — before and during any feedback conversation | Deliver criticism cold or in writing only; it can land as a serious breach of respect |
| Read between the lines for what's not being said directly | Assume indirectness means the feedback isn't real or won't affect your standing |
| Expect passionate, opinionated debate in meetings — it's engagement, not conflict | Publicly question your manager's competence or decision in front of the team |
| Pair any criticism you give with genuine recognition of what's working | Treat your manager's personal warmth as informal — loyalty here often runs to the person, not the org chart |
| Prepare for reviews to take longer and cover more relationship ground than a Dutch equivalent | Rush a review meeting the way you might in a more transactional culture — it can read as dismissive |

### Netherlands: Directness as a Duty of Care

Dutch feedback culture rests on a phrase every newcomer eventually hears: *doe normaal*, roughly "just act normal," which in practice means dropping performance and getting to the point. The cultural logic, well documented across expat guides and confirmed by Quora threads full of newly arrived professionals describing being "shocked time after time," is that hiding your real assessment is the actual rudeness — patronizing the other person by assuming they can't handle the truth. Statista's 2019 survey of Dutch HR professionals found close to 65 percent reporting that structured feedback tools improved employee motivation, suggesting this directness isn't ad hoc bluntness but something organizations have deliberately built process around.

The flat hierarchy that makes this possible is real, not decorative. Managers introduce themselves by first name, and a junior employee pushing back on a senior's plan in a meeting isn't a career risk — it's the system functioning as intended. The adjustment curve for most newcomers, per the expat accounts on record, runs a few weeks: long enough to stop mistaking flat delivery for anger, short enough that most people land on the other side describing it as a relief rather than a wound.

### Argentina: The Relationship Carries the Review

Argentina's Hofstede uncertainty avoidance score of 86 — among the highest in the comparison set — helps explain a workplace that prefers structure and established relationship norms over improvised bluntness. *Personalismo*, the premium placed on personal connection before professional business, isn't a soft add-on to Argentine management; multiple guides describe it as the actual mechanism through which trust, and therefore honest feedback, becomes possible at all. A manager who hasn't built that relationship first has, in a real sense, not yet earned the standing to critique your work without it landing as an attack.

This doesn't mean Argentine workplaces avoid honesty — meetings run dynamic and opinionated, and management guides note real ability to give open feedback in both directions once relationship exists. What it does mean is sequencing: skip the relationship-building and go straight to the critique, Dutch-style, and the same words that would register as respectful bluntness in Amsterdam can register as a personal affront in Buenos Aires, because the social contract that would have made them safe was never built.

### The Reckoning

The instructive contrast isn't "honest vs. dishonest" — both cultures value truthful feedback and would bristle at the suggestion they don't. It's that the Netherlands treats directness itself as the trust-building act, while Argentina treats relationship-building as the prerequisite that makes directness safe to deliver. A Dutch manager skips the preamble because skipping it is the respect. An Argentine manager includes the preamble because including it is what earns the right to be direct at all. Transplant one style into the other country without translation, and you get exactly the failure mode each culture already fears: the Dutch manager in Buenos Aires reads as cold and untrustworthy; the Argentine manager in Amsterdam reads as evasive and unclear.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering a question about culture shock in the Netherlands, one respondent wrote plainly that the directness "will truly shock you... and you will be shocked time after time and when you don't expect it," describing it less as a one-time adjustment than a recurring recalibration that kept resurfacing months into the job.*

> *Quora — A longer thread on Dutch workplace culture noted that colleagues genuinely do what they individually think is best rather than defer to a supervisor by default, and that new arrivals often mistake this for disorganization before realizing it's a deliberate trade of top-down control for individual accountability.*

> *Substack (Emanuela B, "Direct vs. Indirect Feedback: The Cultural Lens Everyone Should Have") — Writing from cross-cultural workplace experience, the author described watching a Latin American colleague visibly brace before a review with a Northern European manager, and noted that what the manager intended as efficient respect landed, without translation, as coldness — the mismatch wasn't in the content of the feedback but in the missing social ritual around it.*

> *IamExpat.nl (reader-sourced expat reporting, "The culture shock in the Netherlands that nobody really explains") — Described the specific frustration of Dutch social circles at work: colleagues will chat warmly in English all day but rarely extend an invitation home, leaving newcomers with plenty of honest feedback about their performance and comparatively little insight into where they actually stand socially.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving into a Dutch team, the practical skill to build fast is not taking feedback personally — assume the bluntness is the compliment, not the insult, and give it back in kind once you've found your footing. If you're moving into an Argentine team, the skill is patience: invest in the relationship before you need it, because the day you need honest feedback delivered kindly is not the day to start building the trust that makes it possible. The line I'd give a friend heading either direction: in Amsterdam, worry if the feedback stops; in Buenos Aires, worry if the coffee invitations do.
