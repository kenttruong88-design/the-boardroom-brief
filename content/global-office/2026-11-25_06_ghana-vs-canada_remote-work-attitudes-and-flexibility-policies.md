# Ghana Waits for the Lights, Canada Waits for the Memo: A Guide to Working From Home

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Canada, the working-from-home debate is about arithmetic: how many days in the office, and who gets to decide. In Ghana, it begins a step earlier, with whether the power is on, whether the data bundle will last, and whether a manager believes a person at home is working at all. Statistics Canada found that about 20 per cent of Canadians worked mostly from home in late 2023, up from about 7 per cent in 2016. A paper in Information Systems Frontiers describes remote working as "not popular" in Ghana's corporate environment. A candidate comparing offers from Accra and Toronto will find that the word "flexible" is doing different work in each.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask any employer what backup power and data they provide before agreeing to work from home | Assume the grid and the home connection will behave on the day of a deadline |
| Keep a mobile data plan as a fallback to your fixed connection | Rely on a single internet source for client calls |
| Show up in person for key meetings and relationship-building | Propose a fully remote arrangement in your first month |
| Frame remote days around deliverables and availability, not hours seen | Expect home working to be read as neutral, since some employers see it as laziness |
| Plan for the heat and for disrupted sleep during outages, and schedule demanding work accordingly | Promise overnight turnarounds unless you have tested your power setup |
| Propose hybrid, with specific days at home for specific tasks | Pitch it as a lifestyle preference |

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract for any written remote or hybrid term before you sign | Rely on a verbal promise of flexibility |
| Ask how many office days are required now and how likely that number is to change | Assume the pandemic-era arrangement is permanent |
| If you work for a federally regulated employer, put a flexible work request in writing after six months | Expect the same statutory process at a provincially regulated employer |
| Expect a written reply within 30 days, and know that denial needs a stated reason | Treat a refusal as the end of the discussion without asking about alternatives |
| Ask about the employer's right-to-disconnect policy if federally regulated | Assume that working from home means being available at all hours |
| Check commute time and transit before accepting a four or five day office role | Accept a downtown role on the assumption that hybrid will return |

## Ghana: The Grid Has Opinions

Ghana's remote work story begins with infrastructure. According to DataReportal, 69.9 per cent of Ghanaians, or 24.3 million people, were online in early 2025, while 10.5 million, about 30.1 per cent, were not. The median fixed download speed was 46.16 Mbps, up 37.4 per cent in a year. For a salaried professional in Accra, that is adequate on a good day. The harder problem is power. OkayAfrica reported on four creative professionals whose work was disrupted by the outages known as dumsor in spring 2024. One said projects that should take a week stretched into months. Another, who works with clients in the United States, the United Kingdom and Ghana, said broadband disruptions cut her off mid-project and forced her to pay for places with power. Generators, one said, were too expensive given fuel costs.

Attitudes are the second obstacle. The Information Systems Frontiers paper, based on a survey of 310 Ghanaian and 333 British employees and 22 interviews, states that remote working is not popular in Ghana's corporate environment, and notes that the government's Smart Workplace Project scheduled 300,000 public workers to work from home during the pandemic. A commentary on TechLabari argues that some employers, clients and relatives view working from home as laziness or a lack of respect for work, and that many Ghanaians prefer face-to-face meetings and place a high value on personal relationships. That article cites figures attributed to the World Bank and an ILO-linked survey without linking them, so they are best treated as indicative only.

Hofstede's comparison tool, cited once here, offers a cultural explanation. Ghana scores 80 for power distance and 9 for individualism, against Canada's 39 and 72. A hierarchical, collectivist office tends to value visible presence and group contact, and a manager who cannot see you has fewer ways to know you are there. This is a generalisation, and a GhanaWeb column notes that employers increasingly prize time management and digital literacy and that hybrid is the likelier outcome than either extreme. The column is opinion, written partly around one remote graphic designer in the Western Region who says he can keep an international job without leaving his family to find work in Accra. It is one account, not a trend, but it shows where the argument for remote work in Ghana is made: geography, not convenience.

## Canada: Flexible, Within Limits

Canada made the transition early and is now arguing about the retreat. Statistics Canada reports that the share of Canadians working mostly from home was 7.1 per cent in May 2016, 24.3 per cent in May 2021 and 20.1 per cent in May 2023. Hybrid work rose from 3.6 per cent in January 2022 to 11.7 per cent in November 2023, while fully remote work fell from 24.3 per cent to 12.6 per cent. About 39 per cent of Canadian jobs could in principle be done from home. The agency estimates that potential teleworkers save about 55 minutes a day in commuting, and about 90 per cent of new teleworkers said in February 2021 that they were at least as productive at home.

Employers have taken a different view of what to do with that. Ontario ordered its public servants to the office five days a week from 5 January 2026, Alberta's public service was slated to follow in February, and BNN Bloomberg reported that many major banks had asked for at least four days. The Angus Reid Institute found Canadians divided on ending hybrid work for federal public servants, with 45 per cent in support and 43 per cent opposed. Among public-sector workers, 36 per cent supported it and 53 per cent opposed, and under-35s opposed it 56 to 28 per cent. A separate Angus Reid survey, reported by CBC, found that three in five Canadians would prefer to work mostly from home and that 51 per cent of employees ordered into the office more often were upset or very upset.

Remote work in Canada is also unevenly distributed. Global News, summarising Statistics Canada research, reports that highly paid and highly educated workers worked from home far more than others, and that in Ottawa 47 per cent of workers did so during the pandemic. In August 2023, about 24 per cent of usual home workers wanted more hours at home, about 65 per cent wanted the same and about 12 per cent wanted fewer. A mismatch between preferred and actual hours, the research suggests, may hurt retention.

What Canada has that Ghana lacks, on the evidence reviewed, is process. Employment and Social Development Canada says federally regulated employees with six continuous months of service can make a written request to change their work location, schedule or hours. The employer must respond in writing within 30 days, stating reasons if it refuses, and cannot retaliate. Federally regulated employers must also adopt a right-to-disconnect policy. The source page covers federally regulated workplaces only, so a candidate at a provincially regulated employer should not assume the same procedure applies.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries are arguing from different starting points. Canada has the infrastructure and a legal procedure, and disputes the number of days. None of the Ghanaian sources reviewed here describe an equivalent statutory request process, power is intermittent, and and there is a prior question of whether remote work is respected. Employees abroad sometimes find the Ghanaian version more personal than they expected. One American who works remotely from Ghana for a US company, as reported by Yahoo News, said she feels free to be herself there, though she also warned that tourism has pushed prices up faster than wages. A Canadian worker negotiating flexibility asks for a policy to be applied. A Ghanaian worker negotiating it asks the employer to extend trust, and often to share the cost of making it possible.

Both countries are converging on hybrid. The TechLabari and GhanaWeb pieces predict it, and Statistics Canada's figures show it. The difference is who bears the risk. In Canada the risk is a mandate that rolls back a norm many employees assumed was permanent. In Ghana the risk is a power cut during the video call the arrangement was meant to make possible. Employers in Toronto cite collaboration and mentorship. Commentary from Accra points instead to presence and personal relationships. Both are speaking about trust, in different units.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, ask different questions. For a Canadian role, ask how many days are required, whether that is written into the contract and whether the employer is federally regulated. For a Ghanaian role, ask what happens when the power fails, who pays for backup, and how remote days are evaluated. In both cases, get the arrangement in writing, because flexibility discussed in an interview has a short shelf life.

Canada offers a procedure and a mandate. Ghana offers a negotiation and a generator. Neither can promise the Wi-Fi, though one can at least promise a form.
