# Ecuador Will Hug You on Day One. Canada Will Add You to a Spreadsheet by Week Three.

🇪🇨 Ecuador · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ecuador's Hofstede individualism score is 8 out of 100, one of the lowest ever recorded, the kind of number that all but guarantees an invitation to a stranger's niece's quinceañera within your first month. Canada's score is 80, practically a monument to going it alone. And yet, per Gallup's 2023 Global State of Social Connections survey, 79 percent of Canadians report feeling connected to other people, against 59 percent of Ecuadorians. If you arrived at this comparison expecting the collectivist country to win on warmth and the individualist one to lose on belonging, pack that assumption away with your travel adapter. You will not need it where you are going, but you will keep carrying it anyway.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇨 Ecuador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every person in a room individually, by name, even in a meeting of ten | Walk in and address the group collectively, then launch into the agenda |
| Accept the invitation to a colleague's family asado, even in your first week | RSVP "maybe" and then not show; decline clearly if you truly can't come |
| Treat the lunch after the meeting as the real meeting | Assume an emailed "yes" is final until a senior person confirms it in person |
| Learn the names of a colleague's spouse and kids early on | Decline weekend invitations repeatedly; it reads as personal rejection, not busyness |
| Bring a small gift when invited into someone's home | Arrive exactly on time to a social gathering; 15 to 30 minutes late is normal |
| Use "usted" and titles until someone explicitly invites informality | Joke publicly about rank, seniority, or who outranks whom |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| RSVP promptly and precisely to the team lunch calendar invite | Treat a single after-work drink as the start of an ongoing friendship obligation |
| Join the office Slack channel, charity run, or hockey pool if asked — that's the actual bonding ritual | Raise salary, politics, or religion at a workplace social event |
| Apologize reflexively when something minor goes sideways; it's lubricant, not an admission | Interpret workplace friendliness as an invitation into someone's home |
| Expect "let's grab a coffee sometime" to possibly never materialize, and let it go | Chase it, or take the non-materializing personally |
| Give new colleagues roughly an arm's length of physical space | Hug, kiss cheeks, or touch on a first meeting |
| Show up two minutes early to meetings | Expect spontaneous same-day plans; they tend to get scheduled weeks out |

Ecuador's social architecture, per Hofstede Insights, pairs that rock-bottom individualism score of 8 with a Power Distance of 78 and an Uncertainty Avoidance of 67 — a profile the firm describes as strongly hierarchical, strongly collectivist, and loyal above all to family and close personal networks. The catch is in that last clause: collectivism in Ecuador operates ferociously within the in-group and is largely indifferent outside it. A newcomer isn't dropped into "Ecuadorian society" so much as left standing outside a specific family's circle, waiting to be let in.

That gap shows up in the numbers. Gallup's connectedness figure of 59 percent, alongside a loneliness rate of 23 percent, sits below Canada's and below regional peers like Uruguay. One long-settled resident on the Expat.com Ecuador forum put a finer point on the mechanism: with less commercial competition in smaller towns, businesses have little incentive to court a returning customer, so service that feels warm in a personal encounter can feel strangely cold in a commercial one — connection, it turns out, is a relationship product, not a default setting. Workplace bonding follows the same logic: the real decisions, and the real relationship-building, happen over the two-hour lunch, not the scheduled meeting, and you are only as "in" as the last family event you were invited to.

Canada's profile runs almost the mirror opposite: Individualism at 80, Power Distance at just 39, and an Indulgence score of 68, according to Hofstede Insights. On paper this should produce a nation of pleasant strangers. Instead, the OECD's Better Life Index finds that 93 percent of people in Canada say they have someone to count on in a time of need, against an OECD average of 91 percent — a civic safety net that functions less like friendship and more like infrastructure. You may not be invited to dinner, but a stranger will still stop to help you change a tire.

The friction appears at the personal layer rather than the institutional one. An HR Reporter analysis of Angus Reid polling found 46 percent of Canadians working from home cite social isolation as a top struggle, and Statistics Canada research shows adult immigrants carry roughly 1.6 times the loneliness risk of the Canadian-born — not because anyone is unkind, but because, as one Expat.com forum contributor in Montreal observed after a year and a half of trying, many Canadians locked in their closest friendships in childhood and simply have no remaining bandwidth for new people, a pattern she found sharper outside the big cities than within them. InterNations' Expat Insider survey backs this up with a number attached: Canada ranks only 12th worldwide for ease of settling in, yet 73 percent of arrivals eventually report feeling at home in the culture, against 63 percent globally. The slow start is real. So is the eventual landing.

Here is the part that trips up relocators in both directions: Ecuador's collectivism and Canada's individualism both predicted the opposite of what they delivered. Ecuador, built on tight family loyalty, offers instant warmth to acquaintances but makes genuine membership conditional on being treated as kin — which can take years, or never happen. Canada, built on self-reliance, offers a documented, almost bureaucratic baseline of trust and support to total strangers, while making the actual friendship layer something you have to schedule, repeat, and wait out.

Team bonding inherits the same split. In Ecuador, work socializing is simply folded into family life — the asado invites the spouse and the kids, and skipping it reads as a rejection of the relationship, not a scheduling conflict. In Canada, work socializing is deliberately separated from private life and formalized instead: the Slack channel, the charity 5K, the fantasy hockey pool, the HR-budgeted happy hour. One culture bonds by absorption into something that already exists; the other bonds by enrollment into something built for the occasion. Neither is faster. They are just front-loaded differently, and knowing which one you're in changes what counts as a success in your first ninety days.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Ecuador forum) — a resident of several months in a small highland town describes genuinely warm one-on-one encounters alongside oddly indifferent service in shops and restaurants, attributing it to how little competition there is for customers in a smaller market.*

> *Expat.com (Canada forum) — a lifelong Canadian resident writes that it is nearly impossible to make truly new local friends past a certain age, because people tend to fill their social circle in childhood and simply run out of room, a pattern he says is worse in suburban and rural areas than in city centres.*

> *Expat.com (Ecuador forum) — two longtime expats advise that the fastest route into the social fabric is learning Spanish and building relationships with neighbors directly, ideally through volunteering, though one cautions that newcomers should expect some new "friends" to be more interested in financial help than friendship, and to calibrate accordingly rather than cynically.*

> *Statistics Canada-cited pattern, not a single respondent — immigrants to Canada carry roughly 1.6 times the loneliness risk of the Canadian-born, a gap researchers attribute less to unfriendliness than to the time it takes to be absorbed into networks that largely closed in grade school.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey — in the same breath that Canada ranks a modest 12th worldwide for ease of settling in, 73 percent of arrivals eventually report feeling at home in the culture, comfortably above the 63 percent global average, suggesting the slow Canadian thaw is a real cost, but not a permanent one.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is to feel human contact immediately, move to Ecuador: you will be fed, teased, and included within weeks, and the cost is that true belonging stays conditional on proximity to someone's family for longer than you might expect. If your priority is a durable, almost institutional floor under you, move to Canada: strangers will show up when it matters, per the OECD's own numbers, and the cost is a social on-ramp measured in months of scheduled coffees rather than days of spontaneous lunches. Neither country is colder or warmer than the other so much as differently timed, and the Hofstede scores that seem to predict the opposite outcome are a useful reminder that national culture indices describe averages, not your actual Tuesday.

Pick based on what kind of lonely you can tolerate on the way to not being lonely at all. If you want to be hugged on day one, Quito and Cuenca are waiting. If you want someone to actually show up when your pipes burst in year three, keep your calendar open in Toronto or Vancouver — and either way, bring something to share to the first gathering, because that part, mercifully, translates everywhere, no matter which hemisphere's version of belonging you end up buying into.
