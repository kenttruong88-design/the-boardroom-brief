# The Philippines Sells You Cheap Help and a Patchy Safety Net; Portugal Offers a Safety Net and a Lisbon Rent

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Portugal's minimum wage for 2026 is 920 euros a month, according to the law firm Garrigues. A typical 80-square-metre flat in Portugal rents for about 1,312 euros, according to Idealista. The Philippines pays its Metro Manila minimum earners 695 pesos a day and charges, according to Numbeo's crowd-sourced users, 38,874 pesos a month for private full-day preschool. Both countries advertise themselves as affordable, which is true in the sense that every country is affordable to someone with a different salary.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In the Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget from the posted evidence: long-resident posters on Expat.com put a comfortable Metro Manila condo life at roughly 60,000 pesos a month | Believe the figure of 200 to 300 dollars a month; residents call it unrealistic in Manila |
| Enrol in PhilHealth, which direct contributors fund at 5 percent of income, split between employer and employee | Treat PhilHealth as complete cover; out-of-pocket spending is 44.4 percent of total health spending |
| Compare private schools carefully, since expats report fees from about 30,000 pesos a year to 250,000 or more | Assume a high fee means a good school; one poster called quality hit and miss |
| Check eligibility for the 105-day paid maternity leave, with 15 extra days for solo parents | Assume leave is automatic for the self-employed without registering with the social security system |
| Hire household help if childcare is the constraint; a diplomatic family reported nannies from about 400 dollars a month | Rely on one nanny as a complete childcare plan |

### In Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a health centre early; about 15 percent of residents had no assigned GP in early 2025 | Assume a family doctor is waiting for you in Lisbon, where the unassigned share was 31 percent |
| Take private insurance seriously; a third of Portuguese hold voluntary cover | Wait until old age for it; insurance becomes very hard to get after 75, according to a forum expert |
| Apply early for a free creche place if your child was born on or after 1 September 2021 | Plan around a place in Lisbon's tech corridor without a backup |
| Use the parental leave arithmetic: 120 days at 100 percent, or 150 days if shared | Assume the 180-day option pays in full; it pays 83 percent, or 90 percent with extra paternity time |
| Budget for heating; a recent American arrival was surprised by the cost | Plan on air conditioning and central heating being standard |

## The Philippines

The Philippines has built an inexpensive way of life and a thin way of paying for it. According to Numbeo, a one-bedroom flat in central Manila costs about 33,775 pesos a month against an average net salary of 31,515, and a long-resident Frenchman on the Expat.com forum puts a comfortable small condo within a budget of 60,000 pesos. The Daily Tribune cites the Philippine Statistics Authority's poverty threshold for a family of five at 13,873 pesos a month in 2023 and adds that many consider it too low for cities. Those with foreign incomes find the country inexpensive; those with Philippine incomes find it merely Philippine.

Insurance is the plainer weakness. The Commonwealth Fund reports that about 83 percent of the population was enrolled in PhilHealth by June 2024 under the Universal Health Care Act of 2019, but that out-of-pocket spending accounted for 44.4 percent of total health spending in 2023, against 35.5 percent for lower-middle-income countries. Direct contributors pay 5 percent of income, shared with employers. A remote consultant profiled by DEV Community carried a private Intellicare plan at about 1,500 dollars a year and still budgeted around 2,200 dollars for care beyond insurance. Enrolment, in short, is not the same as protection.

Families get more help from people than from policy. Employees may claim 105 days of paid maternity leave, with 15 more for solo parents, according to the Philippine Commission on Women, and a diplomatic family in Manila reported nannies from about 400 dollars a month and affordable preschools. A mother on Expat.com paid about 120,000 pesos for a year of Montessori tuition. InterNations nonetheless ranked the Philippines seventh in its 2025 Personal Finance Index, which says something about what expats expect to pay.

The bill rises where the expat bubble does. Numbeo's contributors put international primary school tuition at about 492,470 pesos a year, and the DEV Community profile of a remote consultant in Makati reported 800 dollars a month for a one-bedroom flat, 600 dollars for food and a total of about 3,400 dollars a month, some 41 percent below his American baseline. The same account called the widely advertised figure of 2,000 dollars a month unrealistic for anyone wanting American-grade internet, healthcare and groceries. The savings are genuine; they are simply smaller than the people who sell them.

## Portugal

Portugal has the opposite arrangement: a safety net that exists on paper and a cost of living that arrives in full. The 2025 State of Health in the EU profile, from the OECD and the European Observatory, reports out-of-pocket payments at 29 percent of health spending in 2023, nearly twice the EU average of 16 percent, with unmet dental needs above 15 percent. About 15 percent of residents, 1.6 million people, had no assigned family doctor in early 2025. In the Lisbon-Tagus Valley the share was 31 percent; in the North it was 3 percent. Waits averaged close to four months for cataract surgery and nearly six for knee replacement. An estimated third of the population carries private cover.

On the family side, the state does more than it is given credit for. The government's parental leave guide describes 120 days at full pay, or 150 days when shared, plus paternity leave of 28 days at 100 percent. The Portugal Post reported in August 2025 that the free creche programme covered 126,105 toddlers, paid at 473.80 euros per child per month, against typical private fees of 350 to 450 euros, with places scarce around Lisbon. The same outlet noted that children lose the benefit at three, which is when the next shortage begins.

Wages, however, are another matter. Against a minimum wage of 920 euros, Idealista's estimate of about 1,312 euros a month for an 80-square-metre flat makes arithmetic of the unkind sort, and the same article puts utilities at around 114 euros and a single person's everyday costs excluding rent at about 666 euros. Portugal is generous to families who arrive with money and polite to those who arrive without it.

Spending tells the same story. The 2025 health profile puts Portugal's health expenditure at 3,001 euros per head in 2023, about 22 percent below the EU average, although as a share of GDP, at 10 percent, it matches the Union's. The Commonwealth Fund gives the Philippines 5.1 percent of GDP and 12,751 pesos per person for 2024, with government sources funding 42 percent. Portugal spends a European share of a smaller economy; the Philippines spends a smaller share of a poorer one, and the patient fills the remainder in both cases.

## The Reckoning

On health, Portugal's safety net is broader and its waiting lists longer; the Philippines enrols more people than it protects. Private cover is a necessity in both, but the gap in price is real: an American on ExpatExchange reported private cover through a bank at about 67 euros a month and described the CUF network as good, while the Manila consultant paid 1,500 dollars a year for a plan that did not end his bills. Childcare favours the Philippines for those who can hire it and Portugal for those who can win a creche lottery. Housing favours neither unless the salary comes from abroad.

Hofstede's framework, cited once, is not unhelpful. Portugal scores 104 on uncertainty avoidance and the Philippines 44, while power distance runs 63 in Portugal and 94 in the Philippines, and individualism 27 against 32. Portugal prefers rules and the Philippines prefers relationships. Both prefer that you not discuss the waiting list. A third of Portuguese hold voluntary insurance, which suggests that the preference is not universally shared.

The practical difference is where the risk lands. In Portugal it lands in time: the specialist queue, the missing GP, the creche place that vanishes. In the Philippines it lands in money: the premium, the copayment, the tuition. An expat with income and flexibility handles the second better than the first. An expat on a local salary and a young child may find the arithmetic reversed.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, Philippines forum — A French resident of fifteen years in Metro Manila wrote that the advertised 200 to 300 dollars a month does not work there, that 20,000 pesos covers a simple provincial life, and that regular dining out can push a budget towards 50,000.*

> *Expat.com, Philippines forum — A mother in the Manila area said Montessori tuition ran about 120,000 pesos a child for the year, with uniforms, supplies, shoes and music lessons extra. Another poster advised against public schools unless the family cannot afford otherwise.*

> *Expat.com, Portugal forum — An American who arrived in 2024 found insurance costly, switched plans, and relies on the public system and a nearby district hospital. She was also surprised by heating costs, having run electric heaters all winter in a house without central heating.*

> *Expat.com, Portugal forum — A decade-long resident was moving back to Spain, citing the better health care system and security there. A forum expert added that insurance becomes very hard to get after 75.*

> *ExpatExchange, Portugal forum — A former Texan, delighted with private care at about 67 euros a month through a bank, noted that Portugal has fewer professionals and that complex procedures may be better done abroad.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving with a foreign salary, the Philippines is cheaper, warmer and better supplied with help, and you should price a private plan on day one because PhilHealth will not cover the difference. If you are moving with a local salary, Portugal gives you better statutory leave and a better safety net, and rents that will test both. Register with a GP, ask about creche places before the baby arrives, and read any insurance contract the way the insurer did.

Neither country hides its numbers. Both simply publish them in places where the brochure is unlikely to go.
