**Job-Hopping Is a Sport in Nigeria. In Niger, There's Barely a League.**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇳🇪 Niger**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Nigeria, 93% of the workforce operates informally and only 7.8% holds a formal job, according to a 2025 Nigerian Economic Summit Group report cited by BusinessDay — which means "job loyalty" is a concept most Nigerians experience the way most people experience Latin: present in the culture, absent from daily use. In Niger, the International Labour Organization's modeled estimates put roughly 72.5% of all employment in agriculture, much of it subsistence farming that has never had a human resources department to be loyal to in the first place. One country has too many jobs changing hands to count. The other has too few jobs for the question to come up. Anyone weighing a move for work should understand which problem they are walking into before they pack.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your resumption date, title, and notice period in writing before you resign your current job | Don't treat an offer email as a contract — wait for the signed, stamped letter |
| Keep a second income stream running (consulting, trading, content) — most of your colleagues already do | Don't announce you're job-hunting internally; "options" read as a flight risk, not candor |
| Ask interviewers what the last three people in the role did next — it tells you more than the job description | Don't assume a big title means big pay; Nigerian employers often offer rank instead of raises |
| Split your pay across a local account and a hard-currency option where legal | Don't keep all your savings in naira given ongoing exchange-rate volatility |
| Expect recruiter DMs within weeks of your LinkedIn profile going live | Don't ignore them reflexively — in this market, the outside offer is the raise mechanism |
| Budget actual negotiating time for your notice period | Don't expect two weeks' notice to be the norm — a month or two is standard |

#### 🇳🇪 Niger
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify whether your employer is the state, an NGO, or a donor project — the funding source matters more than the org chart | Don't expect a private-sector job market you can "hop" around in outside government and aid work |
| Learn French, even if your own role runs in English or Hausa | Don't assume English will carry you through contracts, ministries, or payroll paperwork |
| Build relationships with the handful of people who run procurement and logistics in your sector | Don't mistake a quiet CV market for a lack of competition — the pool of formal roles is just that small |
| Confirm your organization's operating status post-2023 before relocating | Don't assume the aid and diplomatic presence that existed five years ago still does |
| Plan your social and professional life around the same small expatriate and NGO circle | Don't expect to simply walk out and find another formal employer in weeks |
| Treat long tenure in a role as a reasonable, even rational, choice | Don't read long tenure as proof of loyalty — it's often proof there was nowhere else to go |

Nigeria's labour market runs on a kind of permanent restlessness that looks chaotic from outside and is, on closer inspection, a rational response to incentives. The National Bureau of Statistics data behind the NESG's 2025 report shows only 15% of employed Nigerians are wage earners; the other 85% are self-employed, and 81% of all workers sit in subsistence agriculture or retail trade — low-productivity work by definition. Against that backdrop, a 2024 Techpoint Africa/Intelpoint survey of 2,600 workers found that just 28.18% had stayed with their current employer more than three years, even though 74.6% said they had a visible career path where they were. People leave anyway, mostly for money: Techpoint's reporting on Nigerian "job-hopping" quotes M-KOPA country manager Babajide Duroshola doing the arithmetic directly — climbing a typical twelve-layer organisation through internal promotion alone takes at least 24 years, while moving between employers can cut that to 15 or 16. Loyalty, in other words, is a tax most Nigerian professionals have calculated they cannot afford.

Niger's picture is less a different answer to the same question than a different question altogether. A World Bank-affiliated PACT workforce assessment, reported by Ecofin Agency, puts the working-age share of Niger's roughly 28 million people at 47%, and describes an economy that is "largely informal," where even the employed are frequently underemployed in low-productivity work. ILOSTAT's modeled figures show agriculture absorbing about 72.5% of total employment as of 2019 — down only slightly from 76.65% in 2000, a two-decade flatline that tells its own story about structural change. Formal employers cluster around government, a thin layer of donor-funded projects and NGOs, and a handful of trading families; the 2023 coup further narrowed that layer as several international missions suspended or scaled back operations. There is comparatively little to defect from, which is not the same thing as job satisfaction — it is closer to a captive market with one buyer.

**The Reckoning**

The irony sitting underneath both countries is that neither has solved the actual problem, loyalty and mobility have just failed in opposite directions. Nigeria's workers have mobility without security — constant movement that rarely compounds into pensions, benefits, or protected tenure, because the formal safety net barely exists for 93% of them. Niger's workers, where formal jobs exist at all, often have a kind of security without mobility: a government or donor-funded post that is stable precisely because there is nowhere else to go, which breeds a loyalty that looks admirable on a CV and feels, from the inside, more like inertia. Hofstede Insights' dimension scores add some structural texture here — Nigeria registers high power distance and low individualism, consistent with hierarchical workplaces that job-hoppers nonetheless route around constantly, while Niger isn't profiled separately at all, folded instead into a generic West Africa regional score, which is its own quiet commentary on how little standardized workplace data exists for it.

Nigeria's mobility problem has an exit ramp Niger's doesn't: leaving the country entirely. Nigeria's health minister told reporters in 2024 that the country now has roughly 55,000 registered doctors left after some 16,000 emigrated in the preceding five years, according to Premium Times — a pace of medical "Japa" that treats a UK or Gulf-state job offer the way other labour markets treat an internal transfer. The same logic runs through tech and nursing. That outlet, not inward mobility, is what keeps Nigerian job loyalty structurally low: staying put for decades was never really the alternative to job-hopping, emigrating was. Niger has no comparable brain-drain valve of its own scale, partly because its formal workforce is smaller to begin with and partly because the visas, language requirements and recruiter pipelines that pull Nigerian professionals abroad were mostly never built pointing at Niamey.

The practical upshot: in Nigeria, two years at a company is already being read, informally, as evidence of either stagnation or unusual contentment. In Niger, two years barely counts as arrival.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a contributor working in Nigeria on a construction project recommended splitting pay between a local account and an offshore one before signing anything, after discovering notice-period terms were looser and less standardized than expected.*

> *Talesmag (U.S. Foreign Service community archive, Niamey posting) — a diplomatic household described expatriate morale as "survival mode together," noting they knew of no one working in Niger's local economy at all; nearly all foreign spouses' employment ran through the embassy itself.*

> *Zikoko (named Nigerian outlet, first-person pseudonymous interviews) — several Lagos professionals described staying in jobs they disliked specifically because they feared an outwardly "shinier" employer would turn out worse, treating loyalty as risk management rather than sentiment.*

> *TechCabal (on-record Nigerian tech workers) — one fintech employee who resigned over a toxic culture and job-security fears ended up tripling his pay after moving to a capital markets firm, an outcome he had not expected going in.*

> *Techpoint Africa (on-record interview) — a Lagos country manager advised future arrivals to ask, before accepting any role, what happened to the last three people who held it, calling that question more revealing than any job description.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison is not "which country treats its workers better," because neither labour market was built with that goal in mind. It is "which kind of professional risk can you tolerate." Nigeria will hand you a lot of mobility and almost no safety net underneath it; you will need to build your own stability — savings, a second income stream, a thick skin about short tenures — because the system will not build it for you. Niger will hand you something closer to stability, but mostly because the formal economy is too thin to offer alternatives, and your career progress will depend less on performance reviews than on who you know among a very small circle of government, NGO, and trading contacts. If you need to feel your career moving to feel sane, Nigeria will oblige you constantly, sometimes uncomfortably so. If you need an employer to actually need you for longer than eighteen months, Niger can offer that, largely by default. Weigh the emigration option honestly too: if the realistic end state of a Nigerian role is eventually using it as a launchpad abroad, say so to yourself on arrival, rather than discovering it the way most of your future colleagues already have. Either way: in Lagos, blink and someone has already taken your desk and improved on your job title; in Niamey, blink and you've simply watched a fiscal quarter go by.
