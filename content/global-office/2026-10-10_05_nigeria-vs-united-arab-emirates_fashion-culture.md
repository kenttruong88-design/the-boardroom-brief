**Nigeria vs. the UAE: One Country Dresses to Be Seen, the Other Dresses to Not Be Misjudged**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Lagos office on a Friday can look like a runway: tailored Aso Oke ties, Adire blazers, headwraps coordinated down to the earrings. A Dubai office on any day can look like a dress code audit: dark suits, covered shoulders, nothing sheer, nothing loud. Nigeria dresses up because it can. The UAE dresses carefully because, for a lot of people in the room, it has to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect Friday (and often any day) to be an occasion for sharp traditional wear — Aso Oke, Adire, agbada | Assume "business casual" means dressing down; it more often means dressing up |
| Keep facial hair neat and trimmed if you have it — it reads as more professional | Show up with visible tattoos or piercings expecting no reaction from HR |
| Invest in well-fitted trousers worn at the waist, not below it | Wear sagging or overly tight trousers and assume a tucked-in shirt fixes it |
| Treat a wedding or major event's aso ebi (coordinated fabric) request as a real social obligation | Skip buying the aso ebi fabric and expect no social cost |
| Let tailoring do the work — fit matters more than brand name in most offices | Assume expensive, loud, or flashy automatically reads as more professional |
| Match your formality to the room; a client pitch and an internal standup are not the same outfit | Wear the same casual outfit to a boardroom meeting as you would to a desk day |

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to conservative: dark suits for men, knee-length or longer and covered shoulders for women | Wear anything sheer, low-cut, or above the knee as a starting assumption |
| Keep a modest, lightweight layer on hand for mall visits, mosque visits, or client meetings | Assume beachwear norms extend anywhere beyond the beach or pool |
| Recognise the national dress (kandura, abaya) as formalwear, not costume | Comment on or stare at traditional dress as though it's unusual in its own country |
| Treat Dubai as somewhat more fashion-forward than Abu Dhabi or the Northern Emirates | Pack one wardrobe and assume it works identically across every Emirate |
| Dress a notch more formal than you think you need to until you learn the room | Copy a colleague's casual Friday look before confirming that's actually the norm there |
| Expect to be visually assessed before you've said a word, in either direction | Assume national dress signals less authority, or assume Western dress signals more |

Nigeria's fashion culture treats clothing as a public, participatory language rather than a private choice. Aso Oke and Adire — fabrics once reserved for weddings, coronations, and festive occasions — have moved into Lagos offices on Victoria Island and Lekki as everyday corporate wear, per reporting from BusinessDay Nigeria: tailored Adire blazers, Aso Oke ties over white shirts, two-piece sets cut for a Monday meeting rather than a wedding. Oyo State has gone further and made traditional attire, including Aso Oke, mandatory for civil servants every Thursday. None of this is driven by casualness — if anything, Nairametrics' own workplace style guidance runs in the opposite direction, warning that visible tattoos, piercings, sagging trousers, and unkempt facial hair are still read as unprofessional in many Nigerian offices, traditional or Western dress alike.

The UAE's fashion culture treats clothing as a line that gets drawn for you, in both directions at once. Business dress is conservative by design: dark, well-fitted suits in breathable fabric for men, knee-length hemlines and covered shoulders for women, nothing sheer or figure-hugging, per guidance compiled by UAE-focused business resources — with Dubai allowing marginally more contemporary styling than more conservative Abu Dhabi or the Northern Emirates. The kandura and abaya aren't a dress-up option; they're standard formalwear for Emirati nationals, worn alongside Western business attire in the same meeting rooms, and official UAE guidance frames modesty expectations as a matter of public respect rather than religious imposition on visitors specifically.

**The Reckoning.** Neither Nigeria nor the UAE has an individually published Hofstede dimension score on its own — Nigeria is typically discussed only within a broader West African cluster, and the UAE within the Arab World cluster — so the sharpest contrast here is observational rather than numerical. Nigeria's fashion signals ascent: a well-executed Aso Oke outfit says you've arrived, you belong, you understand the occasion. The UAE's fashion signals restraint: a correctly modest outfit says you understand the room, full stop, whether you're an executive or a new hire. Both are forms of fluency. Only one of them photographs well on Instagram.

The sharpest irony is who gets misread under each system. In Lagos, dressing down can cost you credibility in rooms where sharp dressing is the baseline expectation. In Dubai, dressing in national dress can get an Emirati professional treated as a token representative rather than a colleague — praised for fluent English as though that were the achievement, assessed "head to toe" before anyone hears them speak, as one Emirati professional put it in a widely shared essay. Clothing solves one country's credibility problem and creates the other's.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The National — Khalid Al Ameri, an Emirati professional, wrote about wearing the kandura to international business meetings: it's "super-comfortable" and a source of pride representing his country, but being the only kandura-wearer in a room often leaves colleagues unsure how to engage with him. He's been told his English is "excellent" in a tone that implied speaking it well was itself an achievement, and recalled Emiratis in Western clothes socially being told, "sorry I didn't recognise you without your local stuff on."*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Lagos — One diplomatic-community respondent called Nigerian fashion "a highlight" of living there, while another admitted giving up on wearing Nigerian outfits at home after a spouse's teasing — favouring light cotton instead for the heat — and a third described the office as casual day-to-day (sundresses, sandals) but requiring a proper suit the moment an actual meeting was called.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Abuja — One respondent was blunt about the climate's effect on expat style: "I find westerners tend to dress poorly here," citing heat, dust, and dirt as reasons people stop trying — concluding flatly that "it's not a place to bring nice clothing," while noting that many Nigerian colleagues remained conservatively and properly dressed throughout.*

> *Nairametrics workplace style column — The piece lists specific, practical red flags for men in Nigerian offices: visible tattoos and piercings still read poorly to HR, trousers worn below the waist look unprofessional regardless of a tucked-in shirt, and an unkempt beard can read as less responsible than a trimmed one — summed up as "trendy does not necessarily mean good."*

### Conclusion

If you're moving between these two, the practical skill isn't picking the "right" outfit — it's learning what your clothing is doing for you in each room. In Nigeria, dressing well is how you earn the benefit of the doubt before you've opened your mouth. In the UAE, dressing modestly is how you avoid becoming the day's cautionary tale, while dressing in national attire, if it's yours to wear, can mark you as a token rather than a peer in the wrong room.

Here's what I'd tell a friend: in Lagos, spend the money on the tailor. In Dubai, spend the money on the plainer suit. Either way, you're paying for the privilege of being judged on something other than your clothes — which, in both places, takes longer than you'd think.
