**Your Rent in Toronto Could Fund a Nanny, a Driver, and Your Groceries in Baku**

🇦🇿 Azerbaijan · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A one-person monthly budget in Baku runs about $856; the same budget in Toronto runs about $2,415 — Baku is, by that measure, roughly two-thirds cheaper across rent, groceries, and daily expenses (livingcost.org cost-of-living comparison). A family's monthly cost follows the same ratio: $2,114 versus $5,363. None of that tells you which country is actually easier to build a life in, because the two run on completely different welfare logic — one where the state is mid-rollout on compulsory health insurance and households routinely hire private help because it's affordable to, and one where healthcare is free at the point of use but a Toronto one-bedroom costs more than an entire Baku household's monthly budget.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇿 Azerbaijan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for a nanny or housekeeper — a local hire runs roughly 600 manat/month, expats often pay 50-100% more for English-speaking help | Assume household help is a luxury reserved for the wealthy — it's a normal, affordable line item here |
| Cook with local produce and meat, genuinely cheaper than imported goods | Stock your kitchen with imported specialty items and wonder why the bill is high |
| Bring cash or a Visa card — it's the most widely accepted | Rely on American Express — many places simply don't take it |
| Check whether your employer's private health plan covers you before assuming state coverage does | Assume Azerbaijan's healthcare system works like a Western universal system — mandatory insurance is still being phased in |
| Pick housing based on commute first — gated suburban clusters can mean 30-60 minutes to the embassy district | Choose a home purely on size without checking the actual commute |
| Treat gym membership as the upper-income activity it currently is | Expect a cheap, ubiquitous gym culture the way you might in North America |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your province's childcare rates before assuming the "$10-a-day" promise applies to you — Quebec and some B.C. programs are close, Ontario and others aren't yet | Assume national childcare pricing is uniform — it varies enormously by province |
| Remember that every price tag you see excludes sales tax | Get surprised at checkout — budget an extra 5-15% depending on province |
| Budget for furnishing an empty rental from scratch — Canadian apartments rarely come furnished | Assume appliances or furniture are included the way they might be elsewhere |
| Use public healthcare for ordinary needs — it's free at the point of use | Expect short wait times for non-urgent specialist care — that's the well-known trade-off |
| Shop around for car insurance and registration costs before buying a vehicle | Assume a used car's sticker price is close to its final, taxed cost |
| Expect your first month's real spending to run well above any online estimate | Plan your arrival budget on a single "average cost of living" number alone |

### Azerbaijan

Baku's affordability is real and structural, not a mirage. Imported specialty goods cost more, but staples are dramatically cheaper than in a North American city — a dozen eggs runs about $1.79 against Toronto's roughly $3.50, a kilo of chicken breast about $5.11 against $13.20 (livingcost.org). That price gap is exactly why hiring household help is so normalized: a diplomat's own post report on Baku notes paying a nanny/maid 600 manat a month, with expats typically paying 50-100% more if they need English-speaking help specifically (Talesmag Real Post Report, Baku).

What's less settled is the welfare infrastructure underneath that affordability. Azerbaijan has been phasing in a system of compulsory medical insurance for years rather than operating a single universal system from the start, with officials periodically announcing coverage expansion to more services and lower mandatory fees (reporting via Azer News/PressReader and the World Bank's "Challenges on the Path to Universal Health Coverage" publication on Azerbaijan). For a relocating employee, this means checking what your specific employer's insurance actually covers matters more than it would somewhere with one unified public system.

### Canada

Canada's welfare state is comprehensive on paper — public healthcare, a federal push toward $10-a-day regulated child care — and wildly uneven in practice depending on where you land. Quebec reached roughly $9.65 a day for regulated child care in 2026; Ontario's provincial average sits closer to $19 a day even with federal subsidies; British Columbia runs a mixed system with some $10-a-day designated centres and others well above that (WealthNorth.ca childcare cost breakdown, 2026). The 2021 federal budget set a national goal of an average $10-a-day fee "by 2025-26" — a target some provinces have hit and others clearly haven't.

Housing is the number that actually breaks a newcomer's budget. Rent for a single person in Toronto averages around $1,430 a month — more than the entire monthly cost-of-living budget for a person in Baku (livingcost.org). One newcomer family's own tally of their first month in Canada included $539 for a week of stopgap rental housing, over $4,000 upfront for a long-term lease's first/last month, and more than $5,500 furnishing an empty apartment from scratch — on top of discovering, as they put it, that "all price tags in Canada are written before taxes" (immigrant.today, first-person account).

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't rich-country-versus-poor-country — it's "pay for help, build your own safety net" versus "pay for housing, rely on the state's safety net." In Baku, the cost of living is low enough that hiring a nanny, a driver, or a cleaner is a normal middle-class decision, while the welfare system you're relying on is still being built out underneath you. In Toronto, you're taxed and priced as though the safety net is comprehensive — because for healthcare, it largely is — but you'll furnish your own apartment, pay market rent with no subsidy, and discover your childcare bill depends entirely on which province you happened to land in.

Neither system is "better" in the abstract; they allocate the burden differently. Baku shifts cost onto the household and lets cheap labor absorb it. Canada shifts cost onto the state for healthcare and early education in theory, while housing — the actual line item that bankrupts newcomers — sits almost entirely outside that safety net.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/Baku) — Someone with 15 years in Dubai, planning a move to Baku with family, opened their post by noting that the "cost of living in Dubai is too high" and went hunting for a rental site for a 3-bedroom apartment in the $1,000-$1,500 range — a budget that, per livingcost.org's own figures, would comfortably cover a Baku family's entire monthly cost of living and then some.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report (Baku, diplomatic community) — One report-writer settled the common new-arrival housing dilemma bluntly: high-rise apartments near the embassy district are "definitely the way to go if you don't have kids," while families cluster instead in gated suburban communities 30 to 60 minutes out — a tradeoff between commute and space that every newcomer ends up re-litigating in their first month.*

> *Immigrant.today (first-person account, Canada) — A family who moved to the Montreal area tallied more than $11,600 CAD in spending during their first month alone — furniture, a used car, groceries, deposits — and flagged, almost as an aside, that "all price tags in Canada are written before taxes," a detail that kept throwing off their running budget at checkout.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report (Baku, diplomatic community) — On groceries, the same writer noted that local produce and meat run "much cheaper than in the US," while specific American staples were simply unavailable — their specific advice to future arrivals: bring your own maple syrup.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is stretching a modest salary into a comfortable household with help you couldn't otherwise afford, Baku delivers that today, with the caveat that you should personally verify what your health coverage actually includes. If your priority is predictable, state-backed healthcare and (eventually, depending on your province) genuinely cheap childcare, Canada delivers that — just budget for Toronto rent like it's a second mortgage, and remember the price tag is an opening offer, not the total. Pick your welfare state. Just don't assume either one is finished building itself.
