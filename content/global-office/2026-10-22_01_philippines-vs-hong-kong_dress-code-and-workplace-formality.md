**Sweating It Out: How Manila Loosened Its Collar While Hong Kong Still Irons Its Shirt**

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Manila, where the average daily high rarely drops below 30°C, most corporate offices still expect long sleeves Monday through Thursday, according to expat workplace guides. In Hong Kong, where the office air conditioning is cold enough that colleagues reportedly wear tights and jumpers in July, nearly half of professionals surveyed say a tracksuit is acceptable office wear — more than double the global average, per a Regus survey covered by Human Resources Online. Humidity, it turns out, correlates poorly with formality. Hierarchy correlates rather well.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a barong tagalog to office formal events — it is treated as the equivalent of a Western suit, not a novelty costume. | Assume "casual Friday" means jeans and a graphic tee everywhere; many employers still expect a collared polo. |
| Keep a light cardigan or blazer on hand for aggressively air-conditioned meeting rooms. | Call a superior by their first name without "sir" or "ma'am" attached — titles are near-universal regardless of age gap. |
| Ask HR for the written dress code before day one; it swings hard between BPO business-casual and law-firm formality. | Wear rubber flip-flops or sandals to a client-facing role — HR guides flag this as a reliable way to get sent home. |
| Default to long-sleeved shirts, conservative dresses, or tailored pantsuits for client meetings. | Show up visibly underdressed to a team lunch; effort is noticed even when the dress code is relaxed. |
| Choose breathable cotton or linen, tailored to fit, over anything heavy or synthetic. | Dress down to "match" a low-income customer base without checking first — some firms do this deliberately, and getting it wrong reads as careless rather than relatable. |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a scarf or jumper everywhere, even in August — the air conditioning is a known hazard, not a rumor. | Assume finance has gone casual just because tech has; Goldman Sachs' widely reported 2017 dress-down policy covered roughly a quarter of its Hong Kong staff. |
| Default to a full suit, or at minimum a jacket, for client-facing finance, legal, or banking meetings. | Wear flip-flops or sandals without checking first; acceptance is rising but still well short of a norm outside casual sectors. |
| Watch what people at your exact level wear before copying a "casual" policy — norms vary floor to floor. | Treat "business casual" as license for a tracksuit; roughly half of professionals say it's fine, which means roughly half disagree. |
| Dress visibly smarter for video calls than you might expect — the vast majority of professionals say they do. | Wear all-white to a celebration; the color carries funereal associations. |
| Stick to dark, tailored basics, especially in banking and law. | Expect the written dress code and daily office reality to match; they frequently don't. |

### The Philippines: Formal Until Proven Otherwise

The Philippines' Hofstede power-distance score is 94, among the highest recorded anywhere, per Hofstede Insights, and office dress behaves accordingly. Nearly every expat guide, including Expat.com's Manila briefing, repeats the same line: Filipinos "mostly dress formally" Monday to Thursday, with the suit itself optional because of the heat but the long-sleeved shirt or conservative dress treated as non-negotiable. The barong tagalog, the national embroidered shirt worn untucked, functions as a sanctioned loophole — it satisfies formality while being, structurally, tropical clothing. Hofstede Insights also lists the Philippines' individualism score at a low 32, which tracks with the honorific culture new hires are warned about everywhere: first-naming a superior is a fast way to cause quiet offense.

Underneath that uniformity, the dress code is doing economic work too. A Philippine Daily Inquirer business column notes that companies serving lower-income customers deliberately dress staff down, reasoning — backed by the columnist's own reporting on small entrepreneurs — that overly polished clothing can make customers feel overcharged; the same column notes lawyers keep full suits even in crowded, poorly ventilated courtrooms, partly to justify the bill. Worth remembering: only about 58 percent of Filipino employment sits in services at all, per ILO modeled estimates, so this entire question concerns a narrow, higher-status slice of the workforce, not the jeepney driver or the market stall.

One more wrinkle: Gallup's State of the Global Workplace puts Filipino employee engagement at 39 percent, well above regional and global averages, even as 50 percent report significant daily stress. The formal dress isn't concealing misery exactly — more like exhausted enthusiasm.

### Hong Kong: Cold Rooms, Hot Debate

Hong Kong presents the opposite paradox: a Hofstede uncertainty-avoidance score of just 29, one of the lowest in the region, paired with a dress culture that the South China Morning Post itself describes as "entrenched" enough that even bank tellers answer to formal codes. The same SCMP piece calls Hong Kong's business-attire norms "ridiculous" for a subtropical climate and notes that when Goldman Sachs loosened its rules in 2017, the change touched only about a quarter of its local staff — hardly a revolution, more a controlled experiment.

The data on where informality has landed is almost comic. The Regus survey cited by Human Resources Online found 28 percent of Hong Kong professionals consider flip-flops acceptable at work, double the global rate, 45 percent are fine with sandals, and 45 percent would wear a tracksuit — yet 91 percent dress up specifically for video calls, above the global average of 82 percent. The office itself is cold enough that over 60 percent of the same respondents say they're likely to wear pyjamas at home, purely by contrast. CIPD's coverage of Asia's slow drift toward casual dress quotes Monash University's Dr Fang Lee Cooke observing that for client-facing staff, especially the Big Four accounting firms, "being well-dressed is an integral part of their impression management." Gallup adds a bleak coda: Hong Kong employee engagement sits at just 5 percent, with 28 percent actively disengaged — one of the least engaged workforces Gallup tracks. The suit, evidently, survives the morale.

### The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: the country more statistically comfortable with ambiguity enforces the stricter dress code. Hong Kong's uncertainty-avoidance score (29) is lower than the Philippines' (44), meaning Hong Kong workers are, by Hofstede's own framework, less bothered by unclear rules — and yet it's Hong Kong where companies police "appropriate" attire down to the bank teller, while Philippine offices, operating with more discomfort around ambiguity, have quietly settled on the barong as a workaround that satisfies formality and physiology at once. Rule tolerance and rule enforcement are not the same variable.

The second irony is economic. Hong Kong's labor market is nearly saturated with the office jobs this debate concerns — 85.8 percent of employment sits in services, per Hong Kong government data tracked by Statbase — so almost everyone is subject to some dress expectation, and disengagement (5 percent, per Gallup) and formality have calcified together. The Philippines, with just 58 percent of employment in services, has a narrower, more self-selected office class that is simultaneously more engaged (39 percent) and more willing to relax dress on a Friday. More exposure to office culture does not reliably produce more comfort with it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag "Real Post Report" Q&A, Manila — A long-serving diplomatic spouse described workplace dress as having drifted toward business casual since the pandemic, with suits reserved mostly for embassy functions and the Marine Corps Ball; one respondent's practical warning was that formal-wear rental in larger sizes is hard to find locally, so anyone tall or broad should pack a backup.*

> *Mumsnet forum, Hong Kong thread — Posters warned a UK worker heading to a Hong Kong client site that the dress code itself would feel barely different from London, "slightly smarter" if anything, but that the office air conditioning was the real adversary: one called it "savage" and recommended a scarf, another said local colleagues wore tights and jumpers through summer, a third solved it with a blazer over a dress indoors and nothing extra outside.*

> *Fodor's Travel Forum, Hong Kong — One regular pushed back on the idea that shorts alone signal disrespect, arguing neatness matters more than coverage; another noted that in Central and Tsim Sha Tsui, locals default to dress shirts, trousers, and leather shoes, sometimes full suits, so casually dressed visitors stand out there regardless.*

> *CIPD interview, on Asia's dress-code shift — Monash University's Dr Fang Lee Cooke told the HR body that flexible dress policies suit client-facing staff poorly, singling out the Big Four accounting firms as places where looking the part is inseparable from doing the job well — a read that surprises newcomers expecting "Asia casualizing" headlines to apply evenly.*

> *Philippine Daily Inquirer, reported anecdote — A business columnist cited research on small entrepreneurs who preferred informal moneylenders over banks specifically because smartly dressed tellers intimidated them — a reminder that formal dress, meant to project competence, can just as easily read as a closed door.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is almost the reverse of what the climates suggest. Manila's heat gives you permission structures — the barong, the casual Friday, the "whatever's decent and comfortable" default — that make formality negotiable in a way Hong Kong's cold, rule-bound offices generally don't. In Hong Kong, pack the suit and the scarf in the same bag; you'll need the first in the meeting and the second to survive the fifteen minutes before it. If you find comfort in clear rules, Hong Kong's written codes will feel more legible, even as its low uncertainty-avoidance score says the opposite should be true; if you'd rather read the room than the handbook, Manila rewards that instinct more often.

If a friend asked me over a drink which one to take, I'd say: take the Manila job if you want your wardrobe to match your mood, and take the Hong Kong job if you want your wardrobe to match your bank balance — then bring a cardigan either way.
