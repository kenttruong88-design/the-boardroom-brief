**"Treated Like a Parent": Why Your Boss in Taiwan or Singapore Is Not Just Your Manager**

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Singapore, management guides describe bosses as receiving "nearly the same respect as one shows their parents." In Taiwan, one American who spent a decade working there warns newcomers plainly that "the boss will take the side of the snitch" in almost any office dispute. Both countries run on hierarchy. Only one of them frames it as filial devotion rather than a survival skill.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a real personal relationship with your direct boss — it genuinely determines your long-term trajectory | Treat the boss relationship as purely transactional, the way you might at home |
| Raise disagreement privately, through a careful face-to-face conversation | Criticize a colleague or a decision in front of the team |
| Stay visibly present during normal hours, even if your actual output doesn't require it | Leave the moment the clock hits quitting time and assume no one notices |
| Respond to work messages reasonably promptly, including some after-hours contact | Set a hard boundary on evening or weekend messages in your first months |
| Identify who actually holds executive decision-making power before routing a request through lower levels | Assume the person you're emailing has the authority to approve what you're asking for |
| Address workplace conflicts indirectly, through a trusted third party if needed | Confront a colleague or manager head-on about a grievance |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait to be asked for your opinion in meetings, especially early in a role | Jump in with unsolicited critiques of a senior colleague's plan |
| Take "ASAP" requests seriously even when the actual deadline is vague — it's a cultural default, not always a real emergency | Assume every urgent-sounding request has a hard deadline attached |
| Build relationships at a deliberate pace — trust here is earned slowly, not instantly | Expect instant social warmth from colleagues the way you might in a more informal culture |
| Write detailed, complete instructions when delegating, since direct questions back to you are culturally discouraged | Expect subordinates to ask clarifying questions if your initial instructions were unclear |
| Accept that a "yes" may not mean full agreement — watch for hesitation, not just the word itself | Take a verbal "yes" at face value without checking non-verbal cues |
| Give feedback a track record before expecting enthusiastic buy-in — change here moves cautiously | Pitch a radical new process and expect quick, confident adoption |

Taiwan's hierarchy runs less through formal titles than through an accumulated, personal sense of obligation. The island's own Gold Card program — the government's own pitch to skilled foreign workers — is remarkably candid about this in its official guidance: seniority "carries major weight," junior staff are generally expected to carry out instructions exactly as given, and proposing improvements too early in a working relationship risks reading as criticism or arrogance. Presenteeism is named explicitly: being visibly present for long hours is still how effort gets recognized in many offices, and the guide notes flexible work "has been slow to take hold." One American professional's decade-long account from the ground, published on ForeignersInTaiwan.com, goes further than any official guide would: bosses may criticize employees directly and expect no pushback in return, HR tends to side with management in disputes, and — in the account's own blunt phrasing — "the boss will take the side of the snitch" in office conflicts almost by default. None of this reads as exceptional or scandalous within the system. It reads as the system working as designed.

Singapore's version of hierarchy is quieter, slower, and more procedural, but no less real. A Western-oriented management guide from Commisceo Global describes a workplace where "bosses are treated with nearly the same respect as one shows their parents," where subordinates generally "do not ask their boss questions" because a question implies the boss explained the task poorly, and where group consensus among colleagues substitutes for direct clarification. One Australian expat, writing under her own name to a Singapore outlet, put the contrast with her home country's flatter structure in a single line: "I value low power distance as a way to contribute to and challenge ideas," a value she found genuinely harder to exercise in Singaporean meetings, where juniors are expected to "stay quiet unless someone asks for their input." She also flagged something less discussed: a culture of urgency — "ASAP, even when no actual deadline exists" — that can create real stress disconnected from any actual business need.

### The Reckoning

Here's the genuine divergence: Taiwan's hierarchy is personal and relational — your standing depends on a specific bond with a specific boss, built through loyalty, visible effort, and the right kind of private deference. Singapore's hierarchy is structural and procedural — your standing depends on correctly reading your formal place in a system that moves cautiously and rewards earned trust over demonstrated boldness. A new arrival in Taipei who focuses on building one strong personal relationship with their direct manager will likely do well, even if the broader org chart stays opaque to them. A new arrival in Singapore who tries that same personal-relationship strategy with their boss, while ignoring the formal chain and its procedural expectations, will likely find it doesn't translate the way it did back home — or in Taipei. Hofstede's power-distance scores put both cultures on the hierarchical side of the global scale — Taiwan at 58, Singapore higher still at 74 — though, as the accounts above suggest, that hierarchy expresses itself very differently: personally in Taiwan, procedurally in Singapore.

Both systems share one real trap for outsiders: mistaking politeness for agreement. A Taiwanese "yes" delivered to avoid confrontation and a Singaporean "yes" delivered to avoid embarrassing anyone in the room can both mean something closer to "no, but I'm not going to be the one to say so." Learning to hear that gap, in both places, matters more than learning any specific etiquette rule.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *ForeignersInTaiwan.com (personal blog, 10-year account) — The author describes learning "the hard way" that speaking freely to superiors reads as insubordination, and that a boss expecting email replies at night, on weekends, and during vacation isn't an edge case: "It has taken me months and years to recover from snitch attacks," he writes, of the broader office-politics culture around reporting colleagues to management.*

> *Blind — A commenter who had spent four months at a major Taiwanese chipmaker's Phoenix site described the management style in blunt terms: "the most toxic and demeaning top down leadership you will ever experience," citing favoritism and arrogance as recurring problems.*

> *Blind — A different commenter, a former Intel employee who moved to the same company, attributed the friction specifically to "East Asian seniority culture," describing middle managers as "coasters" who make heavy demands and let blame feel personal — and advised against the move "unless you are also from East Asia."*

> *Must Share News (named interview) — Nim, an Australian expat working in Singapore, described the gap in power distance directly: used to flatter Australian office structures, she found herself having to actively hold back the instinct to challenge ideas in meetings, framing her preference plainly as "I value low power distance as a way to contribute to and challenge ideas" — a value she said Singapore's workplace culture didn't share by default.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, the honest test isn't "which culture respects hierarchy more" — they both do, intensely, by most Western standards. It's which kind of deference you're better at performing convincingly. Taiwan will ask you to build real personal loyalty to one person and trust that relationship to carry you. Singapore will ask you to read a room correctly, wait your turn, and accept that a slow, procedural kind of trust is the only kind on offer.

Neither country is going to tell you outright when you've gotten it wrong. They'll just quietly stop including you in the decisions that matter — and by the time you notice, in both places, it will already have been happening for a while.
