# Finland Will Force You to Take a Vacation. Taiwan Will Make You Feel Guilty for Wanting One.

**🇫🇮 Finland · 🇹🇼 Taiwan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Finland, an employer who lets a worker skip their mandated summer holiday is, technically, in violation of the law. In Taiwan, a worker who asks for two consecutive weeks off is, culturally, asking for a favor. Both arrangements get filed under the same HR heading — "annual leave." Only one of them actually means it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your full 24-day summer block between May and September — it's protected by law, and colleagues will quietly judge you if you don't. | Expect to reach anyone, for anything, in July. The country functionally closes. |
| Book your mökki (summer cottage) season early; Finns plan their whole year around it. | Schedule a major launch for Juhannus (Midsummer) weekend. It will not go well, and nobody will come. |
| Use your 9 paid sick days without guilt, and know Kela (social insurance) extends support well past that if needed. | Assume a denied leave-transfer request is final — employers need a documented reason to refuse it. |
| Ask HR for the exact accrual math (2.5 days per month) — it's generous and predictable. | Treat "I'll check my calendar" as an answer. Finns mean it literally and will check. |

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate extra leave and written time-off guarantees into your contract before signing, especially for long-haul home visits. | Assume your legal leave entitlement arrives in full on day one — new hires get just 3 days after six months. |
| Take shorter, more frequent breaks, ideally around Lunar New Year, when the whole country travels and it's culturally unremarkable. | Request two consecutive weeks off without a strong justification — it reads as a burden shifted onto teammates. |
| Track your unused leave carefully; by law, employers must pay it out in cash if you don't use it. | Forget that Taiwan's 14 days of unpaid personal leave is a right an employer can legally decline to honor. |
| Ask colleagues how leave requests are "actually" handled, not just what the handbook says. | Expect sympathy for culture-shock-driven leave requests — foreigners are sometimes seen as asking for special treatment. |

**Finland**

The Finnish Annual Holidays Act entitles employees to five weeks — 25 days, accruing at 2.5 days per month — of paid vacation after a year of service, on top of 15 public holidays. The law doesn't just grant this time; it structurally protects it, requiring a justifiable reason before an employer can deny a request to carry leave over, and defaulting a chunk of it into a mandatory summer block. Add Finland's parental leave system, which provides parents a combined 320 working days — roughly 14 months — split into 160-day allotments that can be partially transferred between them, and 9 days of paid sick leave backstopped by government support (Kela) for up to 300 working days, and the picture is less "generous benefit" than "default state of being rested."

This isn't abstract generosity. Finland has topped or near-topped the OECD's work-life balance rankings for years running, and the lived texture of that statistic is specific: July, in particular, is when Finland effectively shuts off. Offices empty, inboxes go unanswered, and the national retreat to the lakeside mökki (summer cottage) functions as something between a tradition and a civic duty. One Filipino immigrant who moved to Finland for work describes a workplace built around *yhteistyö* — collaborative work — where colleagues function more like an extended family, organizing celebrations for weddings, births, and graduations, and where the much-discussed Finnish punctuality turned out to be less about rigidity and more about genuine respect for other people's time, his own included.

**Taiwan**

Taiwan's leave entitlement is tenure-gated and, for anyone newly arrived, thin: three days after six months, seven after a year, ten after two years, climbing to a maximum of 30 only after a decade on the job. Taiwan's Ministry of Labor clocked average annual working hours at 2,008 in 2022 — the sixth-longest among 39 tracked economies, ahead of South Korea's 1,904 and well ahead of Japan's 1,626, though still behind Singapore's 2,293. The law does have a worker-protective feature Finland's lacks: unused leave must be converted to cash compensation rather than simply forfeited. In practice, that provision reveals the whole system's priorities — it assumes, correctly, that a meaningful share of employees won't use their allotted days at all, whether from workload, habit, or simply forgetting, and it solves that with a payout rather than a mandate to rest.

The cultural mechanics matter as much as the legal ones. Forum threads from foreign workers in Taiwan describe the country's statutory 14 days of unpaid personal leave as an "empty right with no remedy" — employers can decline it outright, and extended, Western-style vacations (two consecutive weeks, say, to visit family overseas) are treated as unusual requests requiring negotiation, not entitlements to exercise. Taiwanese employees visiting relatives over Lunar New Year face no such friction, since that block of time off is a shared cultural default rather than an individual ask — which leaves foreign workers, whose families are rarely a short train ride away, navigating a system that wasn't really built with their travel distances in mind.

**The Reckoning**

On paper, the two systems aren't as far apart as they feel. Taiwan's leave cap (30 days after a decade) actually exceeds Finland's statutory 25. The divergence is in what each system optimizes for: Finland's law assumes leave should be taken and builds in friction against not taking it; Taiwan's law assumes leave might not be taken and builds in a financial consolation prize instead. Hofstede's framework puts a fine point on why: Finland scores low on both power distance (33) and masculinity (26), consistent with a workplace where rest doesn't require anyone's permission to be legitimate, while Taiwan's long-term orientation score (93, among the highest ever recorded) helps explain why deferring gratification — cashing out instead of disappearing for a fortnight — reads as the more sensible, even virtuous, choice.

The real trap is the entry period. A new arrival comparing headline numbers might assume Taiwan's eventual 30-day ceiling makes it broadly comparable to Finland. It does not, for the first several years, during which Finland is already operating at full statutory generosity and Taiwan is still counting single-digit days.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Forumosa — A longtime foreign poster described Taiwan's unpaid personal leave as an "empty right," since employers face no real penalty for refusing it; the practical workaround, repeated across the thread, was to get specific leave commitments written into the employment contract before signing rather than negotiating them after the fact.*

> *finnoytravel.com — A Filipino immigrant who relocated to Finland for work wrote that the biggest adjustment wasn't the cold but the punctuality — arriving even a few minutes late to a meeting is considered genuinely impolite — and that the payoff was a workplace that felt more like an extended family, down to colleagues organizing celebrations for each other's weddings and graduations.*

> *CNBC (named interview) — A 28-year-old American who relocated to Finland for work put it bluntly in a published interview: the country "essentially force[s] you to take a vacation," a level of institutional insistence on rest that she said took real adjustment to accept rather than resist.*

> *Axios (named interview) — One of TSMC's first Arizona hires, describing the cultural gap with incoming Taiwanese colleagues, noted that American employees tend to question the end goal before committing to a plan, while Taiwanese colleagues typically align on the objective first and debate approach afterward — a sequencing difference that, transplanted into leave requests, helps explain why an upfront "why do you need two weeks" lands so differently than it would at home.*

### Conclusion

If you're early in your career, the comparison that matters isn't the eventual ceiling — it's the first three to five years, during which Finland hands you a fully-loaded 25 days immediately and Taiwan asks you to earn your way up from three. If you're coming from a hustle-oriented work culture, Finland's challenge is psychological: learning to actually stop, because the system has already decided you will. Taiwan's challenge is also psychological, but inverted: learning that wanting more time off isn't an imposition, even when the law treats it as a courtesy rather than a right. Either way, read the fine print on day one, because by the time you need the leave, it's too late to negotiate it.

The honest version, the one I'd actually tell a friend: take the Finland job if you want a system that assumes you deserve rest without having to ask; take the Taiwan job if you're prepared to ask anyway, and to get the money instead when the answer is no.
