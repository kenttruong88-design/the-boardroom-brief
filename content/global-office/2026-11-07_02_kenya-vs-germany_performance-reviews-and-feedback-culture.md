**Kenya Will Tell You You're Doing Fine. Germany Will Tell You Exactly How You Are Failing.**

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two employees walk out of their annual review. The one in Nairobi got a warm handshake, three minutes of small talk about his family, and the word "satisfactory." He has no idea what to improve, and neither, structurally speaking, does his manager. The one in Berlin got eleven minutes of a spreadsheet, a line-by-line account of a missed deadline, and the words "this needs to be better." He knows precisely what to improve, and he is also, by Gallup's reckoning, one of a shrinking minority of German workers who still feel like getting out of bed for it. Both reviews happened, both were filed, both employees nodded at the appropriate moments. Only one of them, depending on whom you ask, was actually feedback, and the other country would be appalled to learn which one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address your manager by title and surname until told otherwise | Publicly question a superior's decision in a meeting |
| Treat the pre-meeting small talk as the actual warm-up act, not a delay | Skip it and jump straight to the agenda |
| Ask privately, one-on-one, for specific areas to improve | Expect a written appraisal form to spell it out for you |
| Read "satisfactory" and silence as a genuinely good sign | Assume no news is bad news |
| Keep your own written record of wins for your own file | Wait for your boss to volunteer praise unprompted |
| Escalate concerns through your direct line, patiently | Go over your manager's head to resolve a grievance |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, specific criticism aimed at the work itself | Take blunt feedback as a comment on your character |
| Ask for feedback early and often; silence just means "adequate" | Wait passively for your manager to offer unsolicited praise |
| Bring your own list of concrete achievements to the Mitarbeitergespräch | Assume the meeting will open with recognition first |
| Respond to criticism with facts and a counter-proposal | Get visibly emotional or defensive in the room |
| Separate the critique of your work from your sense of self-worth | Carry the meeting's tone home with you |

---

In Kenya, the performance review exists mostly as a ritual that confirms the hierarchy rather than disturbs it. Hofstede Insights' country comparison tool puts Kenya well above Germany on power distance, meaning authority flows one direction and is rarely interrupted from below, and the practical result, documented in detail by Nairobi-based leadership writers, is that juniors who offer unsolicited upward feedback risk being read as insubordinate rather than helpful, with consequences ranging from a quietly reassigned desk to a stalled career. The Kenya National Bureau of Statistics reports that roughly nine in ten new jobs created in the country over the past two years have been informal, which matters here because a formal, documented review cycle is itself something of a luxury good: for the large majority of the workforce, "performance management" is whatever your boss decides to tell you, whenever he decides to tell you.

For the minority in formal employment, Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data shows Kenyan engagement climbing steadily, now at 21 percent, above both the Sub-Saharan African and global averages, even as 24 percent of workers remain actively disengaged. That is a workforce that is, on balance, more hopeful than exhausted, which tracks with a review culture built on encouragement over correction. The cost is precision: an employee who has never been told specifically what to fix has little incentive, and less information, to fix it.

There is also a generational hinge the brochures tend to skip. Younger managers in Nairobi's tech and finance sectors, trained abroad or at multinational employers, increasingly talk about "structured feedback" the way their counterparts talk about standing desks: an import, adopted self-consciously, not a habit anyone grew up with. For now it remains the exception. The default is still the model where your appraisal is a formality and your real performance review happens over lunch, in the tone of voice your boss uses to greet you, and in whether you are still invited to the lunch at all.

Germany runs the opposite experiment. The Mitarbeitergespräch is scheduled, documented, and unsentimental, built on the assumption, confirmed in multiple accounts from relocation advisers, that criticizing the work is a form of respect and criticizing the person would be rude. Hofstede's figures put Germany near the bottom of the power-distance table and firmly toward the individualist end, which is the academic way of saying your opinion is expected to survive contact with your boss's opinion, in both directions, in the same meeting. Works councils formalize the process further: in unionized and larger firms, an employee who feels an appraisal was unfair has a genuine institutional channel to contest it, a level of procedural seriousness that would strike a Nairobi office as faintly absurd and strike a German one as simply Tuesday.

The trouble, per Gallup, is that the system has started eating its own tail: only 11 percent of German employees report being engaged at work, barely above the European floor, and the firm's economists put the annual cost of disengagement to the German economy at roughly $164 billion a year. Only 12 percent of German managers are themselves engaged, and just 27 percent feel adequately supported or developed in their own role, which raises the obvious question of who, exactly, is meant to be delivering all this rigorous feedback with any enthusiasm. Gallup names weak feedback practice, alongside underdeveloped managers and limited strengths-based leadership, as one of three specific leadership gaps driving the number, which is an awkward thing for a country built on the premise that it does feedback better than anyone.

The irony sharpens on inspection. Germans work, per the OECD, among the fewest annual hours of any major European economy, and yet report strikingly low enthusiasm about the time they do spend at the desk; Kenyans work longer, under far less formal management infrastructure, and report comparatively higher engagement. The review that tells you everything apparently does not guarantee you will want to come back for more of it, and the review that tells you almost nothing does not seem to be driving people away either. A widely discussed Reddit thread in r/germany captured the mechanism neatly: a South Asian employee, baffled that his annual review contained nothing but criticism, was told by his supervisor that people cannot improve if they only hear good things. The commenters split almost evenly between "that's just German directness" and "that's just a bad manager," which is itself the most German possible way to argue about feedback.

What also seems similar but isn't is the silence. In Kenya, silence from a manager is read as approval, a kindness in the system's own logic. In Germany, silence is read as approval too, but it feels, to anyone raised on reassurance, like withholding; a German manager who says nothing considers the job well done, while the newcomer waits anxiously for a compliment that was never coming because, structurally, it was never required. The two systems arrive at the identical instruction, interpret silence as a passing grade, by entirely incompatible routes, one built on deference and one built on efficiency, and a transplant from either country reliably mistakes the other's silence for the thing it means at home.

Then there is the question of who the feedback is actually for. The Kenyan review, filtered through a hierarchy that discourages upward correction, mostly protects the manager's authority; the German review, filtered through directness and a works council, mostly protects the process. Neither is optimized for the thing a Western HR deck would claim it is optimized for, the employee's own development, and pretending otherwise is the part of the onboarding slides that ages worst.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/germany — A South Asian employee at a German financial firm described receiving an annual review made up entirely of criticism, and was told by his manager that praise alone doesn't help anyone improve; the thread's commenters disagreed over whether that was simply German management or a specific bad boss.*

> *The Local — A British reader in Hamburg described being openly criticized about her appearance twice by the same colleague, once while sick, and said she still hasn't fully adjusted to comments she would consider rude back home.*

> *LeapScholar Q&A — An advice column aimed at newcomers to German workplaces noted there is no "compliment sandwich": criticism is aimed squarely at the document or the deliverable, and new hires are advised to ask their manager directly for feedback in the first month, since it won't be offered unprompted.*

> *Nuru International (leadership blog) — A Kenyan leadership writer described how a junior employee who performs well can be quietly sidelined, transferred, or given harder assignments rather than promoted, because visible competence can read as a challenge to a superior's authority.*

> *Expat.com (Kenya forum/guide) — A relocation guide for new arrivals in Kenya stressed that hierarchy governs almost every workplace interaction, down to the expectation that juniors use full titles, and warned newcomers that confusing warmth over lunch for informality can lead to real missteps in a review.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on the strength of their feedback culture alone, choose based on what you can tolerate hearing, not what you assume you want. Germany will tell you, with documentation, exactly where you stand, and the telling will not be gentle; Kenya will tell you almost nothing, and the nothing will usually mean you are fine, right up until the one time it doesn't. Neither system is actually about helping you improve as fast as possible; Gallup's numbers on disengagement in both countries suggest that neither one is managing that particularly well regardless, but each is honest, in its own idiom, about what it values. Germany values the correction. Kenya values the relationship. The person who thrives is whoever stops expecting the other place to work like home.

The practical test is simpler than any cultural dimensions chart: recall how you took the last unprompted, public criticism of your work. If the honest answer is "badly," budget six difficult months in Berlin before it clicks. If you'd instead handle total silence badly, budget the same six months in Nairobi, wondering what you did wrong when the answer was almost certainly nothing. If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: go to Germany if you'd rather be told you're wrong than wonder if you are, and go to Kenya if you'd rather be liked than audited, but don't go to either one expecting a hug and a spreadsheet at the same time.
