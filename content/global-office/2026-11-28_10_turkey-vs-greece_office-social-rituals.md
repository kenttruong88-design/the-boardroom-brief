# Turkey Pours the Tea, Greece Orders the Coffee, and Nobody Leaves Before the Third Round

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Eurostat reported in 2025 that people in the EU worked an average of 36.0 hours a week in 2024, which Greece exceeded at 39.8 hours, and Turkey exceeded further at 43.1, the longest week among the European countries compared, according to Turkiye Today's reading of the data. Two countries that work this much would be expected to treat the office as a place of austerity. Instead they have built two of the most elaborate social rituals in professional life, one organised around tea and the other around coffee, and both so firmly embedded that skipping them is read as a statement.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea when the tea server arrives, at least most of the time | Decline repeatedly and let the refusal look like a verdict on your colleagues |
| Say kolay gelsin, a wish that someone's work goes smoothly, when passing busy colleagues | Walk past a working desk in silence |
| Use the formal "siz" with seniors, older colleagues and new contacts until invited to switch | Move to the informal "sen" before someone offers |
| Expect meetings to open with personal conversation and refreshments | Hurry to the agenda, which Cultural Atlas describes as rude |
| Accept an invitation to lunch or dinner when a colleague extends one | Decline a meal and leave the invitation unreturned |
| Stay a little past closing time in your first months | Leave on the minute, which Turkish workplace guides advise against for newcomers |

### Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your coffee order, because the break is a social event | Skip the break to keep working, or eat lunch alone at your desk |
| Begin with personal small talk before business | Open a conversation with the task |
| Allow a meeting to start a quarter of an hour late | Treat a ten-minute delay as a discourtesy |
| Treat a loud, animated exchange as discussion rather than conflict | Mistake passion for hostility |
| Mark name days with a sweet or small gift where colleagues do | Assume only birthdays count |
| Build relationships over meals and follow verbal agreements with a phone call | Rely on the written agenda to govern the meeting |

## Turkey: Tea as Infrastructure

Euronews described Turkey in May 2024 as the world's biggest per-person tea consumer, and noted that workplaces have tea breaks and that meetings are often arranged over tea. Daily Sabah, writing in September 2025, said afternoon tea breaks at work are part of an everyday tea culture in which the point is togetherness rather than beverage. In practice this means the office has a dedicated figure, the çaycı, who circulates with a tray several times a day. A Turkish workplace-conversation guide advises accepting most offers and joining the break, adding that repeated refusal can look unfriendly. This is not a hospitality perk. It is attendance taken at the level of the glass.

The ritual sits inside a hierarchy that is more formal than it first looks. Cultural Atlas notes that decisions tend to move up the chain, that colleagues may be addressed by rank, as in "Müdür Bey" for a manager, and that early meetings are for getting acquainted, since it is considered rude to hurry people towards the point. Hofstede's scores, as reproduced in a PwC Greece publication that does not state the year of the data, put Turkey's power distance at 66, individualism at 37 and uncertainty avoidance at 85. A hierarchical, relationship-heavy culture that also dislikes ambiguity explains why the tea is not optional: the rituals are how ambiguity is managed. Expat Arrivals adds that personal rapport over tea is central to how work functions, and that personal connections, tanıdık, can matter as much as formal rules.

Turkish ritual also yields to the calendar of faith. Cultural Atlas advises that meetings may pause for prayer, that mornings before midday prayer or the hours after lunch tend to work best, and that Ramadan and the summer months of July and August are best avoided for business meetings. A newcomer used to a single global schedule should treat this as information about which weeks the tea will be slower and the lunch longer, not as a delay.

The cost is paid in hours. An Expat.com guide to working in Istanbul describes a standard 9am to 6pm week, tea breaks and after-work outings as bonding mechanisms, and business meetings that extend into meals because sharing food builds relationships. Add Eurostat's 43.1 hours and the arithmetic is straightforward: the social ritual does not replace the long day, it is stacked on top of it. Birthdays follow the same pattern: the Turkish workplace-conversation guide says the birthday person brings the treats, a custom that makes the celebrant the caterer.

## Greece: Coffee as a Calendar Entry

Greece's equivalent is coffee, and it is taken more slowly. A job-culture guide to Greek workplaces says coffee breaks are important moments for socialising and building team spirit, advises newcomers to learn their order, with the freddo espresso as the usual choice, and warns against eating alone or skipping breaks to keep working. Lunch, it adds, is light and late, often between 2pm and 4pm. Expat.com's 2018 habits feature says coffee meetings, often followed by food and alcohol, can run two to four hours. A guide from the outsourcing firm TTEC says a 9am meeting may start around 9:15 and that a team leader might invite the whole team to dinner.

Greeks are not, however, idle. Hofstede's scores in the same PwC publication put Greece at 60 for power distance, 35 for individualism and 100 for uncertainty avoidance, the highest possible reading. That is a culture that wants rules and distrusts them in equal measure, and Cultural Atlas confirms the pattern: agendas are loose guides, rules are expected to flex, heated exchanges are seen as healthy, verbal agreements carry weight and trust is built over meals. Eurostat's 39.8-hour week, the longest in the EU, suggests that the long coffee is not evidence of leisure so much as a high-bandwidth negotiating session conducted at a low temperature.

The Greek system also has a hierarchy, though it wears it lightly. TTEC's guide says seniority is respected and decisions often come from the top, but that rules are applied flexibly and trusted employees may have one bent for them. Sensitive feedback is softened to keep the team harmonious. Another guide says it is often more about who you know than what you know, which is the sort of observation that explains the coffee, since the coffee is where you come to be known. The ritual is the recruitment pipeline, the performance review and the grapevine in one cup.

Name days add a Greek layer. Expat.com reports that they are celebrated with parties, sweets and small gifts, and a 2026 Greek Reporter piece says they can matter as much as birthdays. Meetings, by contrast, are where the style differs most from Turkey. Cultural Atlas notes that arriving up to ten minutes late is generally acceptable, that meetings can run hours over, and that pressing for time can derail negotiations. A Turkish colleague would pour tea and wait. A Greek colleague would order another coffee and begin a different conversation.

## The Reckoning

Both countries treat the office as a social institution first and a productive one second, though they work both jobs harder than most of Europe. The difference is in the grammar. Turkey's ritual is hospitality delivered inside a hierarchy: the tea arrives, rank is acknowledged, the formal "siz" persists until invited otherwise, and relationships deepen in stages. Greece's ritual is conversation delivered inside flexibility: the coffee has no end time, the agenda bends, the disagreement is loud and nobody takes it home. Turkey asks you to show respect. Greece asks you to show up.

For a newcomer, Turkey is easier to read and harder to exit, since the rituals are visible, frequent and expected, and declining them has consequences. Greece is easier to enter and harder to decode, since the same warmth that welcomes you also hides how decisions are made. Neither will tell you which meeting mattered. In both, the person who left early to finish the report will find the decision was taken over the drinks.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, paraphrased experience: A member living in Turkey said colleagues there are more eager than their Western European counterparts to offer help, at least verbally, and that people are very friendly, though punctuality and keeping agreements are sometimes a problem.*

> *Expat Arrivals, paraphrased experience: An American expat in Turkey described the culture as community-oriented, said it is routine to be asked very personal questions, and admitted it took some getting used to.*

> *ExpatExchange, paraphrased experience: An expat in Greece found the pace of life slower than they were used to, the bureaucracy difficult, and the local social norms and expectations hard to read because they differ so much from other countries.*

> *Expat.com, paraphrased experience: A long-ago poster in Greece, in a thread now about ten years old, said they had taken up drinking coffee often, switched from iced frappe to iced black coffee, and liked watching older men gather over coffee.*

> *Substack newsletter, paraphrased experience: A Canadian journalist writing in 2026 about Turkey observed that workplace tea servers are part of a service culture that depends on cheap, poorly protected labour, which he contrasted with the stronger worker protections behind Europe's less attentive service.*

## Conclusion

If you take a job in Turkey, learn kolay gelsin, accept the tea and treat the first three months as an attendance record. If you take a job in Greece, learn your coffee order, loosen your calendar and treat the first three months as a series of long conversations that will, eventually, produce a decision. In neither place is the ritual a break from work, which is the point most relocation briefings omit.

The honest summary is that the drinks differ and the obligation does not. Turkey will ask whether you have had your tea. Greece will ask whether you have time. In both cases, the correct answer is yes, and the report can wait until after.
