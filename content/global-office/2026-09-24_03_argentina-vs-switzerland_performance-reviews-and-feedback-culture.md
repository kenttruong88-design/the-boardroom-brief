# The Review Meeting: Warmth in Buenos Aires, a Spreadsheet in Zurich

**🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Argentine performance review often begins with a genuine question about your family and ends, eventually, with feedback wrapped so thoroughly in warmth that you may need to replay the conversation later to identify what was actually being said about your work. A Swiss performance review begins on time, states the facts, and does not pretend the criticism is anything other than criticism — which, once you adjust, turns out to be its own form of respect.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a personal rapport with your manager before expecting candid feedback | Expect written, formal review documentation to be the primary feedback channel |
| Speak up in meetings — direct input and debate are genuinely welcomed | Mistake warmth and friendliness for an absence of real evaluation happening |
| Read between the lines when feedback is softened with encouragement | Assume a positive-sounding review means no changes are expected |
| Socialize with colleagues outside work — it directly affects how feedback lands | Push for blunt, written critique in the American style; it can read as cold |
| Expect leadership to be visibly present and personally invested in your wellbeing | Assume an approachable boss is a pushover in the actual review |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, fact-based criticism tied to agreed standards, not personality | Interpret blunt feedback as hostility or a sign you're about to be let go |
| Come prepared with concrete data on your own performance | Pad your self-assessment with enthusiasm rather than substance |
| Treat frankness as a sign you're being taken seriously as a professional | Expect praise to be frequent or effusive, even when performance is strong |
| Keep your own reactions measured and factual in response | Respond to criticism with visible emotion — it reads as unprofessional |
| Ask precise, specific questions if feedback is unclear | Assume silence on a topic means it wasn't noticed — ask directly |

---

Argentina's feedback culture is inseparable from its broader relationship-first workplace. Sorwe's Global Employee Experience Report finds that Argentine job satisfaction correlates strongly with the quality of workplace relationships, communication style, and trust in leadership — arguably more than with compensation or role clarity. Think Global HR's guide to managing in Argentina describes a workforce that is expressive and opinionated by default, where meetings run dynamic and managers are expected not just to tolerate direct input but to welcome it. Hofstede Insights puts Argentina at a Power Distance score of 49 and Individualism at 46 — moderate on both counts, consistent with a culture that blends real hierarchy with a strong pull toward group cohesion and loyalty. The net effect on a performance review specifically is that criticism tends to arrive cushioned: a manager who has spent months learning about your family and checking in on your wellbeing is unlikely to deliver a sharp critique without first softening the ground, which means the substance of the feedback sometimes has to be inferred rather than read literally off the page.

Switzerland runs on the opposite assumption entirely. Upreer's 2026 guide to Swiss workplace culture describes performance reviews as structured, factual, and rarely padded with praise — negative feedback in particular is markedly more direct than in neighboring France, delivered without much detour toward diplomacy. Expatriation.io's comparative research on direct versus indirect feedback across cultures confirms the pattern: Swiss employees are conditioned to expect measured, factual communication and, notably, tend not to decode the exaggerated positivity that American-style feedback often comes wrapped in — a mismatch that catches plenty of transplanted managers off guard in the other direction. Hofstede's numbers back this up structurally: Switzerland scores 34 on Power Distance against Argentina's 49, and 68 on Individualism against Argentina's 46 — a flatter, more individually accountable culture where frank criticism is treated as a form of professional respect rather than a breach of it.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony here is that Argentina's warmer, more relationship-driven review process can leave an employee less certain of where they stand than Switzerland's blunter one does. When feedback is delivered gently and wrapped in personal rapport, a foreign hire used to more explicit critique can walk out of an Argentine review believing they're doing fine, only to be surprised later that expectations weren't met. In Switzerland, the discomfort is front-loaded — the criticism stings in the room — but there's rarely ambiguity afterward about what needs to change. Argentina optimizes for the relationship surviving the review; Switzerland optimizes for the information surviving the review.

The other axis worth watching is what each culture considers unprofessional. In Buenos Aires, delivering criticism without first establishing genuine personal investment in the employee can come across as cold, transactional, even a little contemptuous — the relationship has to exist before the correction is credible. In Zurich, the opposite failure mode applies: visible emotional reaction to criticism, or feedback dressed up in excessive enthusiasm to soften the blow, reads as a lack of seriousness. Get either one backward — deliver Swiss-style bluntness in Buenos Aires, or Argentine-style warmth-first framing in Zurich — and you risk being seen as either harsh or evasive, depending on which direction you crossed the border.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asked directly what it's like to work in Argentina and respondents converged on the same point: meetings are genuinely participatory, with staff expected to push back and debate ideas openly, which surprised people coming from more top-down corporate cultures elsewhere in Latin America.*

> *InterNations (Switzerland community, referenced via expat culture-shock reporting) — A commonly cited adjustment for newcomers is that Swiss colleagues rarely become personal friends through work alone, and that most of an expat's social life outside the office tends to form within international and InterNations-adjacent circles rather than with Swiss-born coworkers.*

> *Quora — A respondent asking about the realistic chances of finding work in Switzerland without a Swiss degree or local experience was told that credentials and demonstrated precision carry unusual weight there, and that the hiring bar itself often previews how factual and criteria-driven the eventual performance conversations will be.*

> *Substack (Emanuela B., writing on direct vs. indirect feedback across cultures) — The piece specifically flags Switzerland as a culture where negative feedback is delivered with noticeably less cushioning than in neighboring France, and warns that professionals moving between the two should recalibrate how much diplomatic framing to expect built into a critique.*

> *Quora — Multiple respondents describing daily work in Argentina noted that colleagues socialize together outside office hours as a matter of course, and that skipping this social layer entirely tends to flatten the quality and honesty of the feedback you eventually receive from teammates.*

### Conclusion

If you take feedback personally, Switzerland will initially feel harsh and Argentina will initially feel evasive — but each is solving for something real. Argentina is protecting the relationship that makes future feedback possible at all; Switzerland is protecting the accuracy of the information itself. The practical move is to adjust your own decoding, not the other culture's style: in Buenos Aires, invest in the relationship before you expect the unvarnished version; in Zurich, don't wait for warmth before you take the criticism seriously. My honest advice over a drink: if blunt feedback wounds you, do the relationship-building work in Argentina first — it's the price of admission to the real conversation.
