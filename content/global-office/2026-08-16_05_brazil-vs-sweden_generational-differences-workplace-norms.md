# Sweden Suspends Its Hierarchy for Coffee. Brazil's Youngest Workers Are Asking Why Theirs Doesn't Take a Break at All.

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Twice a day in most Swedish offices, work stops for fika — a coffee break where, as one workplace guide puts it, CEOs and interns sit side by side and titles temporarily dissolve. In Brazil, the boss is still expected to give direct instructions that get carried out largely without public debate; disagreement, if it happens, happens privately, to avoid the appearance of disrespecting the hierarchy in front of others. Generation Z, representing roughly 20% of Brazil's population, has grown up watching that hierarchy from the outside and has started, gently but persistently, asking why it can't dissolve a little more often than a coffee break allows.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Voice disagreement with a superior privately, never in the open meeting | Publicly challenge a manager's decision in front of the team, regardless of your generation — it still reads as a hierarchy violation |
| Expect younger staff to want immediate, visible recognition for work performed | Assume Gen Z's push for recognition is impatience — both Gen Z and millennials in Brazil report expecting it as standard, not a luxury |
| Build relationships before business — punctuality is valued but relational trust often matters more | Treat a slightly late meeting start as a red flag; deadlines and start times flex more than in northern-European offices |
| Ask younger colleagues directly about their views on company values and social responsibility | Assume company culture is neutral ground — nearly half of Gen Z and millennial Brazilian workers actively want employers whose values visibly match their own |
| Use "jeitinho" — creative, relationship-based problem solving — as the norm it is | Mistake jeitinho for rule-breaking; it's a recognized cultural approach to navigating bureaucracy, not a shortcut around ethics |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to fika — it's a real forum for cross-level input, not a coffee-flavored formality | Skip it to "save time" — you'll miss the informal channel where a large share of actual decisions get pressure-tested |
| Expect consensus-based decisions to take longer than you're used to | Push for a fast top-down call — Swedish managers are structurally reluctant to decide without input from the people the decision affects |
| Speak up as a junior employee — flat hierarchy means your input is genuinely solicited | Wait to be invited to contribute; in a low power-distance culture, silence reads as disengagement, not deference |
| Take work-life balance boundaries at face value | Assume junior staff are hungrier for hierarchy or recognition than they let on — younger Swedish employees report similar comfort with independent, flat-structure work as their older colleagues |
| Ask directly for feedback if you want it more explicitly | Expect frequent unsolicited praise — Swedish management culture runs on modesty, and effusive recognition isn't the default currency it is elsewhere |

### The Republic of the Private Objection

Brazilian hierarchy, per Hofstede Insights, sits on a firmly high power-distance foundation — instructions from above are expected to be followed without extensive public negotiation, and disagreement is a private, relationship-preserving act rather than an open one. What's shifting is not the hierarchy itself so much as who's willing to quietly test its edges. McKinsey's research on Brazilian Gen Z describes a generation "anchored in the search for truth," radically inclusive, and oriented toward dialogue as a conflict-resolution tool — values that sit awkwardly against a workplace culture built to route disagreement through private channels and preserve public harmony above all else.

The generational pressure shows up most clearly around recognition and values alignment: both Gen Z and millennial Brazilian workers report expecting immediate, visible recognition for their work and actively favor employers whose social and corporate values match their own. None of this has dismantled Brazil's underlying structure — "jeitinho," the culturally sanctioned art of creative, relationship-based problem-solving around rigid systems, remains the operating logic for how things actually get done — but it has created a generation more likely to ask, privately and repeatedly, why the system requires so much informal workaround in the first place.

### The Kingdom of the Suspended Title

Sweden starts from nearly the opposite structural position. Hofstede scores it among the lowest power-distance cultures globally, and the description isn't theoretical — corporate structures are, per multiple workplace guides, internationally famous for lacking meaningful middle-management layers, with the physical and social distance between a new hire and the CEO reported as genuinely short. Fika institutionalizes this twice daily: a scheduled pause where hierarchy is suspended by design, not by generational demand, and junior employees are expected to speak as freely as senior ones.

The interesting wrinkle, per research on young tech professionals across Sweden and comparable European markets, is that Swedish Gen Z isn't pushing against hierarchy the way their Brazilian counterparts are — there's comparatively little left to push against. Instead, the generational negotiation in Sweden centers on pace: consensus decision-making takes longer than more hierarchical systems by design, since input is deliberately sought from every affected stakeholder before a call is made, and younger employees raised on faster digital feedback loops are, per multiple accounts, the cohort most likely to find that deliberate slowness genuinely frustrating rather than admirably egalitarian.

### The Reckoning

Both countries' younger workers want more voice in real time — the difference is how much voice the system already grants them by default. A 24-year-old in São Paulo is negotiating for the right to disagree publicly in a culture built around private disagreement; a 24-year-old in Stockholm already has that right structurally, and is instead negotiating for the system to move faster once everyone's had their say. Brazil's generational friction is about access to the conversation. Sweden's is about the conversation's length.

There's a shared irony underneath both: Brazil's rigid hierarchy runs on jeitinho, an informal workaround so normalized it functions as a second, unofficial operating system, while Sweden's famously flat hierarchy runs on fika, a formal ritual so normalized it functions as a first, official one. Each country has built exactly the informal mechanism its formal structure was missing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *LinkedIn Pulse — A European manager who spent three years in Brazil described the real skill as learning to distinguish between an instruction that was actually flexible and one that only appeared flexible, since younger Brazilian colleagues were far more willing than their managers to test which was which, quietly, before acting.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about Swedish workplace culture described new hires from more hierarchical countries as consistently underestimating fika, treating it as optional downtime rather than realizing that skipping it meant missing the informal room where half the actual influence in the office got exercised.*

> *Aventura do Brasil (expat blog) — A foreign professional wrote that the hardest early lesson in a Brazilian office was resisting the urge to raise a disagreement in the room it happened in, since doing so, even respectfully, cost more trust than waiting to raise it privately afterward ever would.*

> *Internations Stockholm — A relocating manager noted that her Swedish reports, regardless of age, pushed back just as readily as senior staff during meetings, and that her biggest miscalibration was assuming junior employees needed explicit permission to disagree — permission that, in Sweden, nobody had ever withheld.*

> *Reddit — A Brazilian tech worker in their mid-20s described choosing to raise a process complaint through a private message to their manager rather than in a team retro, and said colleagues from Nordic-headquartered offices found this baffling, since in their experience the retro existed precisely for that conversation.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're moving into either workplace, calibrate around what's actually being negotiated. In Brazil, invest in the relationship-based, private channel — that's where influence genuinely moves, regardless of how flat the org chart looks on paper, and younger staff are testing that channel harder than their managers may realize. In Sweden, show up to fika and expect real input from everyone in the room, but budget real patience for how long consensus takes to reach, since that's the actual generational flashpoint, not who gets to speak. If a friend asked me over a drink: in São Paulo, save your objection for the hallway; in Stockholm, bring your objection to the coffee break, because that's where the hallway is.
