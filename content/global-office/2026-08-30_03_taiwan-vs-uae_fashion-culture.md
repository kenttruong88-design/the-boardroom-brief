# Shorts in Taipei, Shoulders Covered in Dubai: Two Ways to Get Dressed for Work

## 🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Taiwan has no dress-code law and will still make you feel judged for a wrinkled collar; the UAE has an actual line in its Federal Penal Code about what you can wear, yet nobody has been arrested for wearing shorts at a mall in years. One country enforces modesty through peer disapproval, the other through statute, and both arrive at the same instruction for a newly arrived professional: cover more than you think you need to, and bring a cardigan.

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark, well-pressed suit or blouse-and-skirt combination to a first business meeting | Show up in jeans or a t-shirt, even to an informal-seeming interview |
| Choose lightweight, breathable fabrics — linen, technical synthetics — for the subtropical humidity | Assume tech-company casual means anything-goes; neatness still counts |
| Cover shoulders and knees when visiting a temple | Wear lace, bright colors, or anything resembling formalwear to a funeral |
| Let leg or thigh coverage be flexible in casual and even some office settings | Show cleavage in a professional setting — this line is watched more closely |
| Keep a light jacket on hand; office air conditioning runs cold, weather runs humid | Overdress in a way that visibly outshines colleagues — coordinated, not competitive |

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Cover shoulders and knees in malls, souks, and government buildings | Wear tight, sheer, or overtly revealing clothing in public spaces |
| Wear a suit for corporate and traditional-sector business meetings | Assume startup or tech-company informality extends to client-facing roles |
| Carry a cardigan or pashmina for aggressively air-conditioned malls and offices | Wear swimwear anywhere but a beach or pool |
| Dress modestly and respectfully for mosque visits — full-body coverage | Wear abaya, hijab, kandura, or ghutra as an expat under the assumption it's required — it isn't, but respect it |
| Expect more relaxed norms in Dubai than in Sharjah or Ajman | Treat "no one will arrest you" as "no one will notice" — locals and expats alike will say something |

### Taiwan: dressed to blend, not to stand out

Taiwanese business attire runs conservative by design: dark suits for men, tailored skirts or pantsuits for women, and a general aversion to anything flashy in a first meeting ([InternsInAsia](https://www.internsinasia.com/blog/things-to-know-about-business-etiquette-in-taiwan); [Cyborlink](https://www.cyborlink.com/besite/taiwan.htm)). Hofstede Insights scores Taiwan low on individualism — the culture leans collectivist, and people dress to fit the group rather than distinguish themselves, a pattern visible in office wardrobes as much as seating charts. That instinct produces a genuinely odd inversion at funerals: where an American might reach for formalwear to signal respect, Taiwanese mourning etiquette favors plain, muted, almost sloppy dress, on the logic that visible grief outranks visible polish — a lace dress or sharp tuxedo reads as tone-deaf, not respectful.

Outside the office, the picture loosens. Casualwear in Taipei skews comfortable and unpretentious, and Quora threads note that leg and thigh coverage barely registers as an issue — even in some office contexts — while cleavage draws far more scrutiny, a distinction that surprises visitors expecting one uniform "modest" standard. Youth street fashion has spent decades absorbing Japanese, Korean, and Western influences into something distinctly its own, occasionally theatrically mismatched — one expat blogger who arrived two decades ago called the aesthetic "Haute Clown Couture," worth photographing for friends back home. The gap between office conservatism and street exuberance is the whole story: Taiwan doesn't lack fashion opinions, it keeps them out of the boardroom.

### UAE: modesty as market and as law

The UAE's dress expectations are unusual among the countries this column covers in that they are partly codified. Federal Penal Code Article 358 criminalizes "flagrant indecent acts" against public decency, and the UAE Government Portal specifies, more practically, that clothing shouldn't be transparent, indecently exposing, or emblazoned with offensive imagery — shoulders, arms, and legs ideally covered in public. Enforcement is light-touch: arrests for dress-code violations among tourists are rare, and the more common consequence is a security guard or a stranger politely saying something. Dubai runs more liberal than Sharjah or Ajman, and corporate dress varies by sector — suits in banking and government-adjacent firms, smart-casual increasingly normal in tech and startups.

What distinguishes the UAE from a simple modesty checklist is the industry built around it. Vogue Arabia estimates global modest-fashion spending at roughly $254 billion, with McKinsey putting the broader GCC fashion market near $50 billion — and Dubai has positioned itself as its commercial center, hosting design houses that treat the abaya less as a uniform than a canvas, moving from monochrome black toward color, structure, and print aimed at younger GCC consumers. It's a rule-bound wardrobe that became a growth industry rather than a constraint.

### The Reckoning

Line them up and the contrast isn't really "strict versus relaxed" — both countries expect more coverage than a Western newcomer typically packs. The difference is where the pressure comes from. Taiwan's modesty is unwritten and socially policed: no law bars a low-cut top at the office, but a colleague's raised eyebrow does the enforcement work just as effectively, wrapped in the broader cultural priority on harmony and face. The UAE's modesty is written into the penal code, yet the lived experience is friendlier than the statute suggests — sanctions are rare, and the main risk is mild social correction, not detention.

The sharper irony is what each country does with its restrictions. Taiwan keeps its expressive fashion confined to the street and weekend, walling it off from a boardroom culture that rewards blending in. The UAE turned legally mandated modesty into an export: an industry worth tens of billions that treats "covered" as a design constraint to be solved elegantly, not an aesthetic dead end. One country separates fashion from formality; the other fused them into a single product category.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing Taipei dress norms noted that leg and thigh exposure barely registers as a problem, even in some office settings, but cleavage is judged far more harshly — a distinction that trips up newcomers expecting one uniform "modest" standard.*

> *ExpatForum.com — A thread on Dubai office dress settled on a rule of thumb from a longtime expat: jeans and sneakers are never acceptable at the office, a jacket goes on for client meetings once weather cools, and beyond that the standard varies enormously by employer — so ask before assuming.*

> *Quora — Answering a tourist's question about Dubai's dress code, one respondent flagged the gap between marketing and reality: visitors expect the liberal, beach-club Dubai from hotel photos, then get quietly corrected by a guard, colleague, or stranger the first time they wear shorts into a mall.*

> *The Salty Egg (Taiwan expat blog) — A longtime resident recalled that on arriving in Taipei two decades ago, local fashion felt like it was "reading from a different script" entirely — loud, mismatched by Western standards — prompting them to photograph outfits to show people back home; the aesthetic has since converged closer to global norms.*

> *PackSlight (expat packing guide) — Practical advice offered after the fact, the kind travelers wish they'd had before landing: Dubai's malls run the air conditioning cold enough that a cardigan or pashmina over otherwise modest summer clothing isn't optional politeness, it's basic comfort.*

## Conclusion

The packing list, reduced to essentials: for Taiwan, bring one sharp interview outfit and trust that nobody watches your hemline as closely as your composure; for the UAE, bring layers, keep shoulders and knees covered by default, and treat mall air conditioning as a bigger threat than the law is to your ankles. Both countries reward a newcomer who dresses to not be noticed for the wrong reasons — most of what "professional attire" has ever meant anywhere. The honest advice is less about hemlines than instinct: read the room before the rulebook, because in both places the rulebook is the smaller part of the story.
