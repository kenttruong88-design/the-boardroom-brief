# Ghana Legislates Parity by 2030; Nigeria Leaves the Question to the Employer

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ghana's parliament passed an Affirmative Action Act in 2024 promising gender parity in public posts and parliament by 2030, a goal that arrived after a legislative effort begun in 1998. Nigeria, meanwhile, has no such statute, and the World Bank reports that only 10.5% of employed Nigerian women hold wage jobs. Both countries score below the global average on the World Bank's legal index for women, and neither guarantees paid leave for fathers. The question for the sensible expatriate is therefore not which country is more enlightened, but which one fails more informatively.

## Ghana: Do's and Don'ts
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR for the written harassment policy and the name of whoever enforces it | Assume the 2024 Affirmative Action Act has changed daily office conduct |
| Set personal boundaries early and plainly; colleagues tend to respond to them | Laugh off persistent personal remarks as local colour and hope they stop |
| Put verbal arrangements on leave and flexibility into your contract | Rely on statute for paternity leave; the World Bank found none paid |
| Keep dated records of incidents, messages and responses | Count on recruitment channels to be transparent for junior roles |
| Check whether your employer reports progress toward the Act's targets | Treat 27% of top private-sector posts as evidence that your department is typical |
| Respect formal titles and hierarchy until the working relationship is established | Presume informality is an invitation, in either direction |

## Nigeria: Do's and Don'ts
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm maternity terms in writing: the Labour Act sets 12 weeks, paid at half wages after six months of service | Assume casual or temporary status gets you the same protection |
| Report harassment early and in writing, not after months of tolerating small incidents | Wait for a perfect, unambiguous incident before going to HR |
| Ask whether the company has a harassment policy; only 38% of surveyed workers said theirs did | Assume a prestigious employer means a functioning complaints process |
| Read the contract for marital-status or pregnancy clauses before signing | Assume private recruiters are barred from asking about your marriage |
| Learn which law applies to you: federal rules, Lagos state criminal law, or both | Treat the courts as a quick remedy for a hiring dispute |
| Build a record and a network outside your direct reporting line | Make your direct manager your only witness |

## Ghana: A Law With a Deadline

The Affirmative Action Act has real ambition. Agence France-Presse, reporting for EWN, said Parliament passed the bill unanimously, targeting 50:50 parity in public posts and parliament by 2030, with up to a year in jail for anyone obstructing it and proposed tax breaks to coax private-sector quotas. The starting line is modest. According to the same report, women hold about 15% of parliamentary seats and, citing World Bank figures, 27% of top private-sector posts. Sheila Minkah-Premo of the Affirmative Action Coalition told AFP that women are not seen as natural leaders, a gap that a statute can describe but not close. Another commentator in the report, Rodaline Imoru Ayarna, said broader consultation was needed to make the law work because of cultural norms and values, which is the polite way of saying that the culture was not consulted. The report also notes that women are often sidelined at work, and that a 2016 government survey found nearly 39% of married women aged 15 to 49 had been physically abused.

The labour market is more equal in quantity than in quality. The Ghana Statistical Service's 2023 household survey found more women than men in employment, by an average gap of about 900,000. Services and sales employed 41.7% of working women. But 77.8% of employed women were in vulnerable employment against 57.7% of men, and female unemployment ran at 17.7% in the third quarter against 10.9% for men. The World Bank's Women, Business and the Law 2024 report scores Ghana at 55.0 on legal frameworks, below the global average of 64.2, and found no paid paternity leave and no mandate for equal pay for work of equal value. On harassment the record is contradictory. The World Bank dataset could not locate an applicable provision, while Deutsche Welle says labour and criminal law already ban it. In May President Mahama proposed criminalising "sex for jobs," which DW notes is aimed at the pre-hiring abuse that existing law largely misses. The broadcaster attributes the practice to high youth unemployment, fierce competition for public-sector posts and opaque recruitment, and warns that without whistleblower protections and confidential reporting channels the measure could stay symbolic. Ghana does outperform its region on one measure: the World Bank gives it 35.8 for supportive frameworks, the institutions that make laws work, against a sub-Saharan average of 24.5.

## Nigeria: A Large Labour Force, Narrow Doors

Nigeria's numbers describe participation without protection. Dataphyte, using 2023 data, puts the employment rate at 73.7% for men and 70.7% for women, a gap of about three points. The World Bank figures reported by Nairametrics are starker on quality: 10.5% of women are in wage jobs against 17.0% of men, 79.1% of women are in vulnerable employment against 54.8% of men, and 52.2% of women hold a bank account against 74.3% of men. The same data put Nigeria's female wage employment well below the sub-Saharan average of 16.9% and the global average of 54.6%, and show young women out of work or school at a rate of 13.4%. Women's access to the formal economy, in other words, is a rumour that has not reached the branch.

The legal environment is thinner than in Ghana. The World Bank's 2024 profile, which covers Lagos, scores Nigeria 50.0 on legal frameworks and 21.7 on supportive frameworks, and finds that gender discrimination in employment is not prohibited, that pregnant workers cannot be protected from dismissal by law, and that fathers get no paid leave. Overall, the World Bank's Women, Business and the Law index gives Nigeria 51%, with enforcement of rights at 34% of full potential. The one institution with a mandate to hear gender discrimination complaints is the National Human Rights Commission. The Labour Act does grant women 12 weeks of maternity leave, per WageIndicator, though only half-paid and only after six months of service. A Lagos lawyer quoted by Legit.ng explained that the Constitution bars marital-status discrimination in government jobs, while private employers keep wide latitude and courts rarely compel anyone to hire. The harassment research is blunter. The Stand to End Rape Initiative surveyed 493 workers and found that 64% had experienced at least one incident, 85% of those affected were women, and 91% of harassers were men.

## The Reckoning

Ghana has the stronger framework on paper and the louder public debate, with a statute, a deadline and a president promising to criminalise the worst conduct. Nigeria has the larger economy and the thinner protection, with enforcement left to employer goodwill. Neither has solved pay, parenthood or harassment, and the World Bank found no equal-pay mandate in either, and both stand some way below the global average on the World Bank's legal score. The practical difference is that Ghana's problems are at least on the legislative calendar.

Hofstede's published regional row for West Africa, which lumps Ghana, Nigeria and Sierra Leone together, gives power distance of 77 and individualism of 20. A deferential, group-oriented office will not welcome a complaint filed outside the chain of command, and the numbers suggest why many victims weigh silence against retaliation. Expect a hierarchy that rewards patience in both countries, and a complaints process that rewards documentation in both. Hofstede's masculinity score for the same regional row is 46, which is to say neither a boardroom of fierce competitors nor a commune, and which tells you little that a single Tuesday in the office would not.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Zikoko — paraphrased experience: A Nigerian lawyer reported a colleague who had grabbed her on a work trip. HR responded by asking what she had been wearing, and rumours that she was trying to ruin his career followed. A product manager in tech recounted a manager's escalating comments, then explicit images; she reported only the images and now wishes she had spoken earlier. He was fired after an investigation found a pattern.*

> *Zikoko — paraphrased experience: A women's-rights advocate at a Kaduna non-profit described years of exclusion from presentations and trips, then discovered she had been quietly replaced during her pregnancy. She was placed on unpaid leave and told by WhatsApp on 31 December that she was no longer staff, while her colleagues believed she was still on leave.*

> *Legit.ng — paraphrased experience: A Lagos mother of three passed an aptitude test and interviewed well, then met a polite rejection once the interviewer learned she was married. A lawyer's reply was that private employers have wide freedom in recruitment and that courts cannot force an unwilling employer to hire.*

> *University of Oregon student blog — paraphrased experience: An intern at a Ghanaian advertising firm found colleagues respectful of her ideas, one of which reached a client pitch, yet received repeated questions about her rings. She concluded that respect and inappropriate jokes can coexist, and that colleagues responded well once she set boundaries.*

> *theGrio — paraphrased experience: A US-born talent sourcing manager working remotely from Accra for an American firm said she could dress and present herself naturally without ridicule. The article adds that patriarchy and corruption remain prevalent in the country.*

## Conclusion

If gender dynamics weigh heavily in your decision, ask three questions of any employer in either country: who receives a harassment complaint, what the contract says about maternity and paternity, and how many women are in the room where decisions are made. Ghana offers a stronger legal direction and a public conversation to join. Nigeria offers a bigger market and, on current evidence, a greater need to negotiate protections yourself.

Neither country will protect you simply because the brochure said it would. The law, in both, arrives as intention, and the office provides the implementation.
