# Kenya Ranks Its Ministries in Public; Britain Rates You in Private and Describes the Result as Fine

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Kenya, the performance contract has been a fixture of public-sector management since 2004, and institutions are moderated and ranked against one another each year. In Britain, according to Gallup, 10 percent of employees are engaged at work, and the instrument used to discover this is typically a conversation in which the manager says things are broadly fine. One system produces feedback that is formal, numerical and public. The other produces feedback that is private, courteous and open to interpretation. Anyone relocating between them will need to learn which kind of silence is a good sign.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn how your employer's targets are set and who ranks the results, particularly in a public or parastatal role | Assume the annual review is a private conversation with a single manager |
| Raise disagreement with a senior colleague respectfully, at a well-chosen moment, and preferably in private | Contradict a boss in front of a team |
| Build real relationships with colleagues; offices are warm, communal and expect reciprocity | Decline every social obligation and expect to be read as simply busy |
| Set quiet personal limits on how much you take on before the pressure to go above and beyond becomes habit | Announce a limit publicly or treat leaving on time as a casual matter |
| Offer constructive ideas as a contribution to the senior person's goals | Present a new approach as a replacement for how the organisation already does things |
| Document what you were asked to do and when | Rely on a memo to change a decision that has already been made |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat phrases such as "I suggest you think about" as instructions | Take softened language as an invitation to ignore the point |
| Ask your manager, early, what good looks like in specifics | Wait for the formal review to find out |
| Request your appraisal if none is offered, and keep your own record of achievements | Assume silence means satisfaction, or that the process will run itself |
| Start feedback you give with something positive, then state the issue plainly but gently | Open with unvarnished criticism in the manner of the German finance director in Erin Meyer's account |
| Ask what an unexpected rating is based on, and whether a forced distribution is in use | Argue the label before you understand the system |
| Use HR and any appeal process, calmly and in writing, if a rating seems unfair | Escalate by email at midnight |

## Kenya

Kenyan performance management is formalised from the top. According to a Kenyan government (gaa.go.ke) briefing on performance management, performance contracting has been the flagship tool in the public sector since 2004, with contracts cascaded from ministries, departments and agencies down to all cadres of employees, a Staff Performance Appraisal System alongside, and a central unit moderating and ranking institutions at the end of each year. The result is a culture in which targets, scorecards and league tables are familiar vocabulary. A Standard opinion column from August 2023 observed that the practice has now survived three administrations, and wondered whether an annual routine invites tedium. That is the sort of question that follows any system older than its authors.

The informal layer is where newcomers meet resistance. K24 Digital describes Kenyan offices as warm and communal, with colleagues sharing food and knowing each other's families, but with obligations attached: showing up for colleagues, for bosses and for family at home, and an expectation of going above and beyond because formal jobs are competitive. The same piece observes that respect for hierarchy is strong enough to discourage honest upward feedback, and that speaking up takes careful diplomacy. Hofstede Insights scores Kenya at 70 for power distance and 25 for individualism, and the office conforms to the numbers. Feedback travels in one direction, with grace.

Engagement figures are respectable by regional standards. Gallup's 2026 report puts 21 percent of Kenyan employees as engaged, against 19 percent across sub-Saharan Africa and 20 percent globally, though 71 percent are classed as struggling in their lives. A Filipina college head who spent three months in a Nairobi institution recounted a rigid hierarchy, a forum for student concerns closed down by the owner, and a memo recommending a hearing for students returned torn up. Her account is a single case from 2005 and should not be generalised, but its mechanics will be familiar to anyone who has tried to give feedback to a founder. Gallup's other Kenyan figures are mildly encouraging: 33 percent of employees reported daily stress, against 46 percent across sub-Saharan Africa, and 47 percent thought it a good time to find a job. The warmth the press describes appears to carry some weight, even if the cost of it is paid in obligations rather than in salary.

## United Kingdom

British feedback is less formal and, for a newcomer, harder to decode. Erin Meyer's work for Harvard Business Review notes that the British soften criticism with downgraders such as kind of, sort of and a little bit, and tell the story of a German finance director who took his British boss's remark that he suggested he think about changing his approach as optional. It was a firm instruction, and he was later reprimanded for insubordination. A thread on r/london about a coffee-shop probation failure shows the same dynamic at the other end of the pay scale: one commenter wrote simply that Britons are indirect and expect others to read between the lines.

The formal process is also less reliable than it appears. According to the CIPD, 48 percent of employees had specific objectives set, but only 28 percent had a systematic version with objectives, monitoring, feedback and accountability, and only 21 percent said their pay was affected by achieving their objectives. A 2015 Enterprise Study survey reported by HR Review found that one in five staff employed for over a year had not yet had a first annual review, and that of those who did, 43 percent had to ask for it. The CIPD's central finding is the useful one: the quality of the line manager had a larger effect than introducing any system. HR Review's survey gave the commonest reason for postponed appraisals as managers being too busy, cited by 38 percent. The CIPD also found no meaningful difference in task performance between employees with performance-related pay and those without. The British appraisal, in short, is a ritual whose benefits depend almost entirely on the person conducting it, and whose absence is rarely noticed until it matters.

The same index gives the United Kingdom 35 for power distance and 89 for individualism, a pairing that explains the casual first names and the unspoken rules. Gallup's 2026 figure of 10 percent engaged suggests the arrangement is not entirely satisfying. Employees are expected to manage their own careers, which is liberating for the confident and baffling for the rest. A Mumsnet thread about a 20-year financial services employee rated underperforming at mid-year review showed readers advising a calm, documented challenge, and noting that the rating might reflect a forced distribution in which someone in each group must score lower.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries solve opposite problems. Kenya has plenty of structure and not much candour upward; Britain has plenty of candour in theory and not much structure in practice. A Kenyan employee knows the rating cycle, the targets and the ranking, and may not know what the senior person actually thinks. A British employee may hear what the manager thinks, delivered in understatement, and not know how it will translate into a number. Power distance of 70 against 35 accounts for much of it, and individualism of 25 against 89 for the rest.

For the expat, the adjustment runs in the expected directions. The Briton moving to Nairobi must learn to read deference as respect rather than weakness, to respect hierarchy without surrendering judgment, and to value the relationship before the critique. The Kenyan moving to London must learn to translate understatement upward and to treat their own career as a personal project, since no ranking will arrive to do it for them. Money and mobility sharpen the point. In Kenya, where the Gallup survey finds 47 percent of employees think it is a good time to find a job, the ranking is a reason to stay visible to the right people. In Britain, where the formal link between performance and pay is weak for most staff, the review is better treated as a source of evidence for the next negotiation than as a verdict. Both will discover that a pleasant meeting is not evidence of anything.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/london — a poster who failed probation at a specialty coffee shop was told they were not friendly enough and too slow on tasks, and asked whether this was British indirectness. Commenters split between those who saw underperformance and those who saw a harsh manager, and several agreed the feedback was too vague to act on.*

> *Mumsnet — a long-serving employee at a UK financial services firm was placed in an underperforming category at mid-year with no warning despite positive peer feedback. Replies advised documenting everything, asking the manager to explain the framework, and noting that in financial services, meeting expectations is increasingly read as underperforming.*

> *Expat.com — a Swedish intern in Nairobi found Kenyan business similar to British in systems and language but with looser timekeeping, and saw superiors use lateness to assert power over smaller firms. The same thread was full of warnings about bribery and caution over unsolicited friendships.*

> *The Filipino Expat — a Filipina college head recalled staff uneasy when she ate lunch with them, a founder who insisted his way was the only way, and a three-month stint that ended the day her memo came back torn up. She later concluded she had been hired mainly as a foreign face.*

> *K24 Digital — a Kenyan writer described offices as warm and generous, but said leaving on time can feel like a statement, deference to bosses discourages honest upward feedback, and communal support can quietly turn into pressure nobody acknowledges.*

## Conclusion

The practical advice is to ask the same question in both places: what does a good rating look like, and who decides? In Kenya the answer will be written in a performance contract and moderated by someone senior, so find out early who that is, and build trust before you offer any critique. In Britain the answer will be in a conversation, and the real feedback will be what the manager hesitated over, so ask for specifics, keep a record, and request the review if nobody schedules one.

A final precaution applies to both: do not treat a good first review as a settled matter. In Nairobi it may reflect a relationship that needs maintaining, and in London it may reflect nothing more than a manager who was too busy to say otherwise. Revisit the conversation after three months, and ask what has changed.

Neither country is hostile, and neither is clear. The Kenyan system tells you precisely where you stand and says little about what anyone thinks. The British system tells you how everyone feels, in a manner that may require a glossary.
