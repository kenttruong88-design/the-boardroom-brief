**Mongolia Is the Most Socially Connected Country on Earth, Gallup Says. Australia Just Found Out Its Own Social Isolation Rate Has Doubled Since 2001.**

🇲🇳 Mongolia · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In a 142-country Gallup survey on emotional closeness to others, Mongolia ranked first in the world, with 95 percent of respondents reporting feeling "very" or "fairly" connected — ahead of Kosovo, Taiwan, and every other country surveyed. In Australia, a two-decade University of Sydney study using HILDA panel data found chronic social isolation has roughly doubled since 2001, from 10.3 percent to 20.5 percent, and that trend held even after accounting for COVID-19. One nation lives in round, wall-less tents its own researchers credit with enforced togetherness. The other invented "mateship" as a national personality trait and is now measurably lonelier than it's ever been.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇳 Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect close physical proximity to double as the main bonding mechanism — traditional gers have no interior walls, and families share one space by design | Assume urban Mongolian life replicates this automatically; city dwellers often deliberately return to hometowns in summer specifically to maintain these ties |
| Treat an unlocked door as a genuine cultural signal, not carelessness — leaving gers open for guests to eat and rest reflects a real norm of mutual help | Expect this hospitality norm to translate directly into formal office etiquette; it's a household and community practice first |
| Expect extended family roles to be clearly defined and active, including grandparents, in daily caregiving and household function | Assume family closeness means geographic permanence; Mongolians maintain strong bonds across significant distances, including abroad |
| Navigate Ulaanbaatar's ger districts socially, not just by map — many lack street grids, so relationships with neighbors functionally replace GPS | Expect the isolation that comes from a language barrier to resolve quickly; outsiders without Mongolian describe real, sustained social friction despite the culture's overall warmth |
| Expect cohesion to be framed partly as a survival mechanism — mutual reliance in extreme weather and remote geography is cited as a direct driver of the bonding itself | Mistake strong family cohesion for complete cultural openness toward newcomers; the two aren't the same thing |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect shared language and cultural familiarity to make bonding genuinely easy if you fit the existing social mold — multiple accounts describe Australians as authentically "fun and friendly" | Assume the stereotype guarantees deep connection; chronic social isolation has doubled over two decades even as the "friendly" reputation persists |
| Expect alcohol to remain a default social lubricant at work events, but a declining one — only 3% of workers now attend weekly Friday drinks | Assume everyone wants in on drink-centered bonding; 22% specifically want alcohol-free events, and half prefer activity-based bonding instead |
| Offer or expect alternatives — mini-golf, axe-throwing, coffee meetups — as increasingly normal substitutes for the traditional pub-based team event | Treat missing a work social event as low-stakes; the research is explicit that skipping these can be "a barrier to making closer connections and friendships" |
| Note that workplace loneliness has real productivity costs here — lonely employees report 35% work impairment versus 24% for others | Assume younger workers are immune; 18–24-year-olds are among the loneliest age groups, alongside those aged 45–54 |
| Expect genuine generational change in drinking norms — non-drinking among people in their 20s nearly tripled, 9% to 22%, between 2001 and 2019 | Expect colleagues to prioritize office friendships over outside ones; 87% say they socialize more with friends and family than coworkers |

Mongolia's social-cohesion numbers aren't a cultural stereotype dressed up as data — they're genuinely exceptional by any global measure. Gallup's Global State of Social Connections survey, covering 142 countries, found 95 percent of Mongolians report feeling very or fairly emotionally connected to others, the highest of any country measured, with men and women scoring almost identically. Researchers point specifically to the ger — the traditional round felt tent with no interior walls, where families of varying sizes share one space, sleep on the floor, and cook on a single stove — as a structural, architectural driver of closeness, not just a cultural preference. Mongolia has no individually published Hofstede cultural-dimension score, so there's no tidy numeric shorthand here, but the on-the-ground explanation is consistent across sources: extreme weather and remote geography make mutual reliance a practical necessity, not a lifestyle choice, and the social bonds follow directly from that necessity.

Australia's story is the inverse: a strong cultural reputation for friendliness sitting on top of a genuinely worsening isolation trend. The University of Sydney's analysis of two decades of HILDA panel data found chronic social isolation nearly doubled, from 10.3 percent in 2002 to 20.5 percent in 2023, with older adults showing the largest increases — and the authors found this held even controlling for the pandemic, so it isn't simply a COVID hangover. Hofstede scores Australia's individualism at a very high 90, among the highest in the world, which tracks with a culture built around personal choice in how and whether to bond, including, increasingly, opting out of alcohol-centered workplace rituals: a 2025 Hub Australia survey found only 3 percent of workers still attend weekly Friday drinks, and 87 percent say they socialize more with friends and family than with colleagues.

### The Reckoning

The uncomfortable comparison is architectural versus elective. Mongolia's cohesion is substantially involuntary — you share a one-room ger with your family because that is the ger, and the bonding is a byproduct of the physical setup as much as any chosen value. Australia's bonding is almost entirely elective, built from optional rituals — the Friday drink, the Christmas party, the mateship ethos — that individuals can increasingly and visibly decline, and that a measurable share of them now do. Neither approach is inherently superior: involuntary closeness can curdle into claustrophobia or exclusion of outsiders, as the language-barrier isolation one visitor described makes clear, while elective bonding can simply erode to nothing once enough people opt out, which the Australian isolation data suggests may already be happening.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *NPR/WUSF feature — A Mongolian wildlife disease specialist described the physical intimacy of ger life as a direct source of family coziness, noting that in rural areas every family member, grandparents included, holds a defined daily role, with siblings often settling in gers nearby rather than scattering.*

> *NPR/WUSF feature (same piece) — A Mongolian band manager who has lived in Chicago for years while managing his brother's band back home said nomadic life simply requires people to depend on one another, and that family stays central to Mongolian identity regardless of the physical distance separating people — his own long-distance arrangement being a case in point.*

> *Northeastern University study-abroad blog — A student living first in Mongolia and then in Australia described a stark before-and-after: in Mongolia, a language barrier left her "constantly drawing stares just for being there," feeling more alien than welcomed, while in Australia she found it "actually very easy to make friends" and confirmed the country's reputation for friendliness firsthand, crediting shared language for most of the difference.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Mongolia, understand that the connectedness you'll read about in survey after survey is largely inherited through family and household structure, not something you'll be automatically folded into as an outsider without the language — budget for real initial friction even in the world's most socially connected country. If you're moving to Australia, take the "friendly" reputation seriously, but don't mistake it for a guarantee of deep bonds; the country's own isolation data suggests the warmth is real but the follow-through has been quietly declining for twenty years. Ask, in either place, not whether people are nice to you, but who actually shows up when something goes wrong — because that's the only version of "bonding" either country's statistics actually agree on.
