# Bosnia Will Tell You What It Thinks of Your Salary. Sweden Will Tell You Nothing At All

🇧🇦 Bosnia and Herzegovina · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Sarajevo, ask a colleague what they make and you will get an answer, an opinion on whether it's fair, and probably a story about a cousin who makes more in Germany. In Stockholm, ask the same question and you will get a long pause, a faint flicker of alarm, and — eventually, if you are very persistent and possibly a journalist — a number so carefully hedged it might as well be classified. According to N1 Info's 2024 reporting on regional pay surveys, Bosnian men negotiate assertively for higher starting offers while women take a markedly more cautious approach; according to the Medlingsinstitutet, Sweden still carries a measured gender pay gap of roughly 10% in 2024, a figure that has barely moved in six years despite the country's reputation for having solved this sort of thing decades ago. Reputation, it turns out, is not the same as data.

## Bosnia and Herzegovina: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly what the role pays before you invest a week in interviews — nobody considers this rude | Assume the advertised salary band is the real ceiling; it rarely is |
| Expect to negotiate hard, especially for private-sector and foreign-employer roles | Expect the state sector to negotiate at all — those scales are fixed and public |
| Lean on comparisons to German, Austrian, or Swiss pay for diaspora-adjacent roles | Be surprised when a colleague volunteers their exact salary unprompted |
| Treat a verbal number from HR as provisional until it's in writing | Assume degrees automatically command premium pay — connections often matter more |
| Factor in that net pay is eaten by high informal costs of living relative to wages | Expect transparency from management about how raises are decided |

## Sweden: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your collective agreement (*kollektivavtal*) — it sets your real floor, not the job ad | Ask a Swedish colleague what they earn; it reads as deeply invasive |
| Negotiate once, calmly, with a number backed by market data — theatrics backfire | Expect public salary bands even after the EU Pay Transparency Directive lands |
| Use your annual *lönesamtal* (salary conversation) as the one sanctioned moment to push | Assume silence about pay means pay is fair — the gap just isn't discussed |
| Join a union; unions, not individuals, do most of the real negotiating here | Bring US-style aggressive counter-offers to the table — it reads as a threat, not leverage |
| Expect anonymised pay-gap statistics to be public even when individual pay isn't | Expect your manager to proactively flag that you're underpaid relative to peers |

## Bosnia and Herzegovina: The Haggle as a Civic Duty

Bosnian workplace culture treats a salary offer the way a Sarajevo market treats a price tag — as an opening position rather than a conclusion. According to BHAS's Q4 2025 labour force data, formal wages remain low relative to the cost of imported goods and regional peers, which means the unofficial economy of negotiation — asking for meal vouchers, transport allowances, informal bonuses — does real work that base salary numbers don't capture. N1 Info's reporting found a familiar and uncomfortable pattern: men in BiH ask for 10-20% more than their initial offer as a matter of course, while women are statistically far more likely to accept the first number, a gap that tracks almost exactly with patterns N1 found across the wider European comparison set.

What foreign observers tend to miss is that this openness about money coexists with almost no formal transparency from employers. There are no published pay bands, no legally mandated disclosure, and raises are frequently a matter of manager discretion rather than policy. The result is a culture where individuals are candid about their own numbers — partly as solidarity, partly as competitive benchmarking — while institutions remain opaque. The numbers underneath the candor are modest: N1 Info reported the average net salary in the Federation of BiH at around 1,600-1,700 convertible marks a month through late 2025 (roughly €820-870), a figure that climbs gradually but is still routinely compared, unfavorably, to what a cousin or former classmate earns doing similar work in Germany or Austria. Hofstede's cultural framework, applied loosely to the wider Western Balkans in comparative academic work, points to a higher power-distance, more collectivist orientation than Sweden's, which tracks with the pattern on the ground: authority sets the rules, but the rules get discussed loudly at the kitchen table.

## Sweden: The Silence Is the System

Sweden inverts the arrangement almost perfectly. Individual salaries are a near-taboo topic between colleagues — raising the subject at a *fika* is the fastest way to make a room go quiet — while the system itself is unusually transparent at the aggregate level. Anyone can request another citizen's tax records, including income, from the Swedish Tax Agency, a fact that regularly startles newcomers and that the Culture Trip has described as one of the country's more disorienting bits of radical openness. The paradox is real: you can look up what your neighbor paid in tax last year, but asking your coworker directly what they earn will get you a very different reception.

Formal negotiation happens through channels, not confrontation. Roughly 90% of Swedish employees are covered by a collective agreement, according to figures widely cited in WTW's and DLA Piper's coverage of Sweden's rollout of the EU Pay Transparency Directive — legislation Sweden has approached cautiously, even lobbying in 2026 for a renegotiation of its scope, wary of disrupting a bargaining system built around unions rather than individuals. The annual *lönesamtal* is the one moment individual negotiation is expected and sanctioned; outside it, pushing hard on pay is read as a faintly American imposition. Even immigration policy runs on aggregate wage math rather than individual negotiation: Migrationsverket raised the median-salary threshold required for a work permit in 2025, a single published number that does more to set a newcomer's floor than any conversation with a hiring manager will. Statistics Sweden (SCB) publishes average monthly pay by occupation, region, and sector in granular public tables — the sort of detail a BiH jobseeker would have to triangulate from gossip — and yet, despite all this institutional tidiness, Medlingsinstitutet's own 2024 report shows the gender pay gap has sat near 10% for six straight years, proof that transparency about aggregates and progress on individual outcomes are not the same project.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and you get a tidy inversion: Bosnia is loud about individual numbers and silent about institutional ones; Sweden is silent about individual numbers and comparatively loud about institutional ones. A Bosnian professional moving to Stockholm will have to learn to stop asking colleagues what they make, and to start trusting that the collective agreement and the union rep are doing the negotiating work they used to do themselves over coffee. A Swedish professional landing in Sarajevo will have to get comfortable being asked, bluntly, what they earn — and discover that the number they're offered on day one is an opening bid, not a verdict.

Neither system has actually solved the underlying problem, which is the point this pillar keeps circling back to. Bosnia's candor doesn't close its gender gap, and Sweden's legislated transparency hasn't closed its either. What each country has built is a different theatre for the same disagreement over what people are worth — one performed in person, one performed in spreadsheets nobody individually admits to reading.

## Conclusion

If you're weighing a move, the practical advice diverges sharply by direction. Heading into Bosnia, ask every question about pay you'd normally consider impolite — the ambiguity benefits the employer, not you — and build in the informal perks (housing stipends, flexible hours, under-the-table flexibility) that never show up in the contract. Heading into Sweden, do the opposite: let the union and the collective agreement do the heavy lifting, save your one real ask for the annual *lönesamtal*, and resist the urge to benchmark yourself against a colleague who will never tell you their number anyway. Both systems produce pay gaps their defenders insist are shrinking. Neither brochure will mention that the shrinking has mostly stopped.
