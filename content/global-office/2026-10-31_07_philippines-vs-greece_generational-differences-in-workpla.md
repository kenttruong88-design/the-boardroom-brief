**Respect Your Elders, Inherit Their Debts: A Generational Field Guide to Working in the Philippines and Greece**

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇬🇷 Greece**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Picture two 26-year-olds comparing job offers over video call. One has been warned that calling her new boss by his first name would be a genuine misstep, that titles are non-negotiable, and that the office has a karaoke machine for a reason. The other has been warned that her new boss is also, unofficially, a cousin of her landlord, that the firm has been in the family since before the junta fell, and that nobody has left the company before 55 except by emigrating. Both offers come with a pension system neither believes will exist by the time they need it. Welcome to the Philippines and Greece, two economies where the generation giving the orders and the generation executing them share almost nothing except a paycheck that does not quite cover rent.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep calling your boss "Sir" or "Ma'am" for months after they insist on a first name | Don't actually switch to the first name until they stop reminding you to |
| Accept that a colleague's referral may outrank your résumé | Don't assume merit alone beats someone's *utang na loob* (debt of gratitude) network |
| Route disagreement through a trusted go-between or soft phrasing | Don't contradict a superior directly in front of the team, even when they're wrong |
| Show up to the Friday videoke or team lunch | Don't skip bonding events — absence reads as disrespect, not busyness |
| Expect Gen Z hires to negotiate hybrid schedules on day one | Don't assume a 2026 hire owes you the deference a 1998 hire gave automatically |
| Bring back *pasalubong* from any trip, including work travel | Don't return from a conference empty-handed |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Assume the firm you're joining may be run by someone's grandfather, literally | Don't expect a merit-based org chart — most Greek companies are family-owned |
| Bring a lawyer's patience to payroll, tax office, or contract paperwork | Don't expect same-week resolution on anything bureaucratic |
| Take the two-to-three-hour lunch break seriously, especially outside Athens | Don't stack meetings through the early afternoon |
| Expect loud, direct pushback in meetings | Don't mistake Greek argument-as-sport for a personal attack |
| Budget as if your net salary won't cover Athens rent alone | Don't expect a starting wage that matches the actual cost of living |
| Let senior colleagues set technical precedent before pitching new tools | Don't assume your university's AI fluency outranks three decades of institutional memory |

The Philippine workplace runs on a paradox Deloitte's Gen Z and Millennial Survey captured without quite naming it. In its Philippines country data, 81 percent of Filipino Gen Z respondents report feeling burned out and 65 percent hold a second paying job on top of their main one, with 59 percent living paycheck to paycheck — yet close to 90 percent still say they believe they can personally drive change inside their employer, a figure their global peers trail by roughly 30 points. That is not naivety. It is a generation that has learned to route ambition through deference rather than against it: disagreement is allowed, but it travels quietly, through an intermediary, in a tone that preserves everyone's standing, because the hierarchy itself is rarely up for negotiation even when its specific decisions are.

That hierarchy predates any current org chart. Filipino workplace etiquette still runs on kinship vocabulary — *kuya*, *ate*, Sir, Ma'am — regardless of age gap or actual seniority, and using a boss's first name uninvited is, per guidance circulated to new foreign hires in Manila, a real misstep rather than a quaint formality. What has shifted is what the youngest cohort tolerates underneath that etiquette. Where the generation now running HR absorbed 2008-era scarcity as a reason to stay quiet, research cited by Ateneo de Manila finds today's entrants expect fast feedback and visible growth plans while still saying "Sir" on the way out the door. Most employers have answered with cosmetics — reverse-mentoring schemes, a "Global CEO Hour" nobody quite knows what to do with — while the actual authority structure holds still.

Greece's generational math is blunter. The Institute of Labour of GSEE's 2025 "Youth and Work" survey of workers under 30 found 70 percent saying their income doesn't cover basic needs, 62 percent financially dependent on family, and 79 percent believing their own parents had it better — a rare case of a generation not even disputing the premise. Deloitte's 2025 Greece survey adds texture: roughly half of Gen Z and millennial respondents name cost of living their top concern, two-thirds live paycheck to paycheck, and nearly 70 percent doubt they'll ever retire in comfort. None of this is abstract to the 46 percent of GSEE respondents seriously weighing work abroad, extending a brain-drain pattern documented since the 2010s debt crisis.

Meanwhile, the generation that isn't leaving is not, strictly speaking, stepping aside. Hellenic Statistical Authority figures reported in the Greek press show pensioner-employment registrations roughly doubling between 2023 and 2024 after a pension-withholding penalty was scrapped, and at least half of large Greek companies still keep executives aged 60-plus in the top chair rather than an advisory seat. Add that roughly three-quarters of Greek firms are family-owned, by one Georgetown-cited estimate, and the generational handover isn't so much being negotiated as postponed indefinitely: the people holding the capital are, on average, both older and in no particular hurry.

**The Reckoning**

On paper, both countries look like the same generational standoff — an exhausted, underpaid young workforce facing an immovable older guard — but the mechanics diverge in ways that matter to anyone actually relocating. Filipino hierarchy is performed constantly and loudly, through titles and honorifics, yet proves oddly negotiable underneath: Gen Z workers there win hybrid schedules, side hustles, and informal access to leadership while still calling that leadership "Sir." Greek hierarchy is barely performed at all — meetings are loud, direct, and genuinely argumentative in tone — yet the underlying structure of who owns the company and who inherits it is close to immovable. One culture negotiates around a rigid shell; the other argues freely inside a rigid skeleton.

There's also a mismatch in exit strategy. The Filipino response to a bad generational deal is to moonlight quietly rather than confront it head-on. The Greek response is to leave the country outright, which is why Greece's most durable export since 2010 has arguably been its own university graduates. Hofstede's cultural-dimension data back this at a structural level — the Philippines scoring among the world's more hierarchical societies, Greece scoring among the more uncertainty-avoidant ones — but the daily texture runs almost opposite to what those labels suggest: the "hierarchical" culture turns out more flexible in practice than the nominally flatter one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — in a thread on working under a much older team, one poster described being boxed out by senior colleagues who openly said they no longer cared about promotion and simply refused to cooperate, while replies split between blaming generational rigidity and insisting the real divide was mindset, not birth year.*

> *Cafébabel — a profile of a young English teacher in Chania found that most locals her age expected to eventually take over a family business rather than build a career elsewhere, since the ceiling for responsibility outside the family firm is low and informal, contract-free arrangements are the norm inside it.*

> *Expat.com's Manila guide warns new arrivals that addressing a Filipino supervisor by first name instead of "Sir," "Ma'am," or a professional title can genuinely backfire, since the honorific signals a respect that competence alone does not substitute for.*

> *Lazy Leverage, a newsletter for people who hire Filipino virtual assistants, describes new employers being struck by how far staff will go to keep a relationship harmonious — agreeing readily, avoiding open conflict, treating colleagues as a kind of second family — while warning that an eager "yes" can just as easily mean "maybe."*

> *Quartz, reporting on HSBC's Expat Explorer survey, quoted a British respondent in Greece describing ongoing "feelings of insecurity due to the economic crisis," in a country where the same survey found roughly half of expats saying their earnings didn't cover daily expenses.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two postings for the career rather than the coastline, the honest calculus runs like this: the Philippines will ask you to perform a deference you may not feel, and reward you with more real flexibility than the ritual suggests. Greece will let you say exactly what you think in a meeting and then go home to discover the meeting changed nothing, because the person who could change it inherited the company and has no plans to retire. Neither is dishonest, exactly — both are operating on time horizons longer than any standard employment contract, one measured in family reputation, the other in family ownership.

Pick the Philippines if you'd rather navigate etiquette than equity. Pick Greece if you'd rather argue honestly about a problem nobody currently holding the capital has much incentive to fix. Either way: smile at the hierarchy, budget for the rent, and don't expect either generation to blink first.
