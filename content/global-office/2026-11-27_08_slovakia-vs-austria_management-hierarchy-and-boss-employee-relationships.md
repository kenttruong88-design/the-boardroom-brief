# Austria Salutes the Title, Slovakia Waits for the Memo

🇸🇰 Slovakia · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two capitals sit about sixty kilometres apart, and their offices disagree on nearly everything about the boss. In Vienna, you may address the head of a department as Herr Magister for several years and still be left alone to do your work. In Bratislava, first names arrive quickly, and so, according to Slovak business leaders, can a reluctance to disagree with them. Gallup puts the share of engaged workers in Slovakia at 13 percent, which suggests that friendliness is not the only variable.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Slovakia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a friendly relationship with your manager early, since it is expected | Assume friendliness means your manager wants to be contradicted |
| State your opinion explicitly and invite a response, because silence is common | Wait to be asked what you think in a meeting |
| Agree flexible hours directly with your manager, as many Bratislava employers allow | Presume flexibility is a right rather than an arrangement |
| Learn whether you are in a modern company or one with pre-1990s habits | Expect a clear mission statement or a written process in every firm |
| Take initiative on small matters and say so afterwards | Wait for instructions on tasks you plainly own |
| Respect older colleagues, who tend to be more loyal and cautious | Treat caution as incompetence |

### Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Herr or Frau plus surname until invited to use first names | Switch to du because a younger colleague did |
| Remember academic and professional titles such as Mag., Dr. and Ing. | Drop a title because it seems decorative |
| Speak formally to your boss, using Sie, unless the firm clearly uses du | Assume the office norm is the same in every company |
| Arrive on time, since punctuality is read as professionalism | Treat a meeting agenda as a suggestion |
| Take the goal and run with the method, since bosses often leave the method to you | Ask for approval of every step |
| Use the works council or Chamber of Labour if you have a dispute | Assume you must settle everything privately |

## Slovakia: Friendly, Flat in Manner, Firm in Practice

Slovak workplaces tend to be informal at the surface and quieter beneath it. The Slovak Spectator, compiling answers from six Slovak business leaders in January 2020, reported that employees keep friendly ties with colleagues and want a good atmosphere. The same piece described them as less assertive, unlikely to fight for their rights, and sometimes reluctant to voice opinions, which foreign employers read as a lack of initiative. It also noted that many firms lack a formal corporate culture and handle problems ad hoc, though most owners now respect their staff. That source is from 2020 and the evidence is anecdotal, so treat it as a field report, not a census.

The boss relationship appears to matter commercially. A Paylab survey of 52,120 workers in 11 countries, conducted in 2017, found that about 61 percent described a friendly relationship with their supervisor, 35 percent a reserved one and 4 percent a cold one, and that friendly relationships were associated with pay roughly 14 percent higher. The published figures are regional rather than Slovak, and they are old, but the survey drew 12,157 Slovak responses, so the direction applies. Gallup's 2026 report, which draws on data collected through 2025, puts the 2025 engagement rate for Slovakia at 13 percent, against 12 percent for Europe and 20 percent globally, so Slovak employees are warm, rather than enthusiastic. Generational change complicates the picture: the Spectator's panel said older employees are more loyal and cautious, while younger ones are more self-confident and flexible. Welcome to the Jungle's 2021 guide to Bratislava quotes an accounts assistant who credits colleagues' growing openness to a generation that has studied or interned abroad, and an administrative assistant who says staff can choose their hours between 6 AM and 10 PM by agreement with their manager. Even the flexible hours, note, run through the manager. Eurofound adds that industrial relations are consensus-oriented and that strikes are rare, which fits a workforce that prefers to keep the peace.

## Austria: Formal Surface, Delegating Core

Austria is, by the evidence, a hierarchy with manners. Expatica describes Austrian business as traditional and hierarchical, with senior management making most decisions and staff knowing their defined roles. Titles matter, and colleagues are Frau or Herr until invited otherwise. Expat.com's guide to Vienna adds that you should address your boss as Sie, though some companies have drifted to du, so newcomers should check. A coworking guide describes authority exercised with quiet professionalism rather than dominance. Expatica adds that meetings follow a pre-set agenda with little room for personal opinion, that emails are treated with the formality of business letters, and that advancement is merit-based, so people earn their place in the hierarchy. The system is slow to flatter anyone, but it is reasonably clear about what it is doing.

The curiosity is that Hofstede's 1991 ranking, as reproduced in a Slovak Academy of Sciences paper, gives Austria a power distance score of 11, among the lowest in the world. Titles are formal, and the actual distance is small, which may be the most Austrian paradox available. Formal structures also protect staff: Eurofound notes that works councils can be established in any private workplace with at least five workers, and union density was 26.3 percent in 2019. A first-person account supports the gentler reading. A French writer who spent about eight years in Vienna wrote in 2021 that colleagues' ideas were heard and credited regardless of seniority, and that bosses set goals and left the methods to staff. By contrast, the same paper's Slovak figure of 52 is the authors' own estimate, and an earlier study placed Slovakia higher, so the comparison is shakier than it looks.

## The Reckoning

Austria uses form to protect the relationship. You say Herr Doktor, you accept that the department head decides the strategy, and then you are generally left alone to do the work. Slovakia uses friendliness to avoid the question. First names are easy, disagreement is not, and a manager may receive polite agreement that tells him very little. That is an inference from the Spectator panel's description, not a measured finding.

For the newcomer, the practical difference is feedback. In Vienna, you will know where the line is, because titles mark it. In Bratislava, you will need to ask directly, because nothing about the atmosphere will tell you. Austria also supplies institutions that make deference less risky: a works council, a Chamber of Labour that an Austrian poster on Expat.com says employees fund through a small contribution, and a culture in which the formal address is understood as respect rather than fear. Slovakia has its own channels, including trade unions and works councils where no union exists, but Eurofound notes that only unions take part in collective bargaining, and that union density has declined since 2008 and 2009. Neither is more humane. One is more predictable, and the other is more pleasant until the first misunderstanding.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, a long-standing Austrian worker said benefits vary by sector, that employers often subsidise lunch and transit passes, and that employees pay into the Chamber of Labour, which offers free legal and tax advice*

> *Expat.com, a poster working in IT in Bratislava reported a fully English-speaking workplace where language had not been a problem, though he said nothing about the boss*

> *Welcome to the Jungle, a Bratislava administrative assistant at an international insurer said staff can work flexible hours by agreement with their manager*

> *Welcome to the Jungle, an accounts assistant from Portugal working in Bratislava said Slovak colleagues had become more open over time, which she linked to a generation that had studied or worked abroad*

## Conclusion

If you value being told the rules, Austria suits you, and you should buy business cards that carry your title. If you value early first names and an easy atmosphere, Slovakia will welcome you, as long as you accept that the atmosphere may be doing the work that an org chart would do elsewhere.

In either country, ask your manager in the first week how decisions are made and who may disagree with whom. In Austria that answer will be in writing somewhere, and in Slovakia it will be in someone's head. Expect Austrian employers to offer fewer gimmicks than their reputation suggests, since one Expat.com poster complained that benefits are minimal, while another pointed out that pay arrives in 14 instalments a year and that perks can be negotiated. Expect Slovak employers to be more open to English-speaking staff than the language might suggest, at least in international firms. Either way, the person with the title is rarely the one you will need to ask.
