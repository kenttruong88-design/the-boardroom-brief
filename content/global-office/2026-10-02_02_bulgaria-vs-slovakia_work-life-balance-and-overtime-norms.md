**The 38.7-Hour Lie: Clocking In Across Bulgaria and Slovakia**

**🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇸🇰 Slovakia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Eurostat's 2025 figures put Bulgaria among the longest working weeks in the European Union at 38.7 hours, nearly three full hours above the bloc's 35.9-hour average. Slovakia, on paper, looks almost identical — a standard 40-hour week written into law. The two countries sit a few hundred kilometers apart, share a Soviet-era labor-law ancestry, and arrive at the same number of hours in the office through completely different cultural machinery: one through quiet non-enforcement of boundaries, the other through a statute that caps overtime at a tidy 400 hours a year and nobody dares ask for 401.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇬 Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify your actual job security early — "a job for life" assumptions run deep in older management cultures | Assume a 38.7-hour average week means light workloads; it reflects long hours, not high output |
| Build personal relationships with your direct manager — trust is negotiated one-on-one, not by policy | Expect HR policy to override what your specific manager decides informally |
| Ask directly about unpaid "informal" overtime before accepting an offer | Take a verbal promise about hours at face value — get it in the contract |
| Bring a mix of foreign and local management expectations into the open rather than assuming alignment | Assume foreign managers and local staff read "reasonable hours" the same way — this is a cited source of friction |
| Use annual leave fully — Bulgarian law entitles you to it, but uptake is inconsistently encouraged | Wait for your employer to suggest you take leave; the burden sits with the employee to ask |

#### 🇸🇰 Slovakia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a firm 9-to-5 and 40-hour structure — it's the real norm, not aspirational | Volunteer for regular overtime to look committed — "regular overtime is not the norm" and signals a staffing problem, not ambition |
| Arrive on time, every time — lateness without notice reads as disrespect, not quirk | Mistake directness in meetings for hostility — Slovak communication style treats bluntness as efficient, not rude |
| Confirm your overtime cap in writing — 150 hours employer-mandated, up to 400 with consent | Expect the senior voice in a meeting to open the floor for debate — hierarchy sets the agenda, not consensus |
| Use formal titles until a relationship earns first names | Push for remote work as a default — Slovakia's home-working rate sits well below the EU average |
| Treat the 12-hour daily and 35-hour weekly rest rules as inviolable — they are legal minimums, not suggestions | Assume Bratislava's Western veneer means Western flexibility — core hierarchy and scheduling norms run more traditionally |

### Bulgaria: long hours, loose enforcement

Bulgaria's workweek has crept past the EU average largely in the agricultural, forestry, and managerial categories, per Eurostat's statistical breakdown — managers alone average 40.6 actual hours, well above the clocked standard. What the raw hours figure doesn't capture is the informality underneath it: a contributor on the Expat.com Bulgaria forum, describing a hospitality business, noted that some workers "believe they have a job for life and believe they cannot be fired," a legacy expectation from the command-economy era that collides awkwardly with modern at-will-adjacent management practices. The same thread flagged a specific friction point for foreign managers: mixed teams of local and international supervisors reportedly produced worse outcomes than either group managing alone, because expectations about what counts as "reasonable" pushback or "reasonable" hours simply didn't match.

The structural picture: Bulgarian labor law guarantees paid annual leave and overtime premiums, but enforcement culture leans informal, and the gap between what's written in the Labour Code and what happens in a given office is wider than in richer EU states. Novinite's reporting on the workweek dipping just under 39 hours "for the first time this century" reads, in context, less as a work-life balance triumph than as a very slow grind downward from an unusually long baseline.

### Slovakia: the 400-hour ceiling

Slovakia runs the opposite instinct: codify first, negotiate never. The standard week is 40 hours, the standard day runs 9-to-5, and overtime is capped by statute at 150 employer-mandated hours a year, extendable to 400 total only with the employee's written consent — a number precise enough to suggest a legislature that has seen what happens without one. Overtime pay sits at a minimum 125 percent of base rate, climbing to 200 percent on public holidays, which makes unpaid "informal" overtime far less common than in Bulgaria simply because it's expensive to pretend it isn't happening.

Slovak office culture pairs this legal precision with a hierarchy that Hofstede Insights scores among the most pronounced in Europe — Slovakia typically registers very high on power distance and, distinctively, the highest masculinity score of any country in Hofstede's original dataset, a dimension associated with assertiveness, competition, and a sharp line between work performance and leisure time. In practice that shows up as meetings where the senior figure sets the agenda and communicates decisions rather than opening debate, and a workplace where remote work remains unusually rare — under 8 percent of Slovaks worked from home even occasionally as of 2023, notably below the EU norm.

### The Reckoning

The paradox: Bulgaria, the country with the objectively longer average workweek, is the one where "work-life balance" is a matter of individual negotiation and quiet non-enforcement, while Slovakia, with a shorter legal week, is the one where the hours are more rigidly separated because the law treats overtime as something to be metered, consented to in writing, and paid for at a premium. A Bulgarian employee's extra hours are often invisible in the data because they're informal; a Slovak employee's extra hours are extremely visible because they're priced.

The other reversal worth noting: Slovakia's rigidity produces more *predictable* boundaries even though its hierarchy is stricter, while Bulgaria's relative informality produces less predictable ones even though its culture reads, on the surface, as more relationship-driven and flexible. Neither country optimizes for what an American or British arrival would recognize as "balance" — both optimize for control, just at different points in the system: Bulgaria at the level of the individual manager, Slovakia at the level of the statute.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Bulgaria work-culture forum) — An employer running a hospitality business in Bulgaria described a recurring staffing problem: longtime local employees operating on an old assumption of guaranteed job security clashed with newer performance expectations, and mixed teams of foreign and Bulgarian managers produced worse results than either group running the floor alone, because the two groups disagreed on what "reasonable" supervision looked like.*

> *Expat.com (Bratislava relocation forum) — Someone relocating to Bratislava for work got a blunt practical reality check from existing residents: a salary that sounds modest by Western European standards, around 784 euros a month, was described as merely "okay" locally once rent, food, and a monthly bus pass were accounted for — a reminder that the headline salary number means nothing without the local cost context.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com, EU relocation thread) — A tech worker comparing European offers to a U.S. package noted that FAANG-adjacent roles in Europe typically run 40–45 hours a week against a 50–60 percent total-compensation cut, concluding that the trade genuinely favors the hours over the money, but warned that job mobility across the EU is far more limited than in the U.S. once you've made that trade.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing Bulgaria, the honest question isn't "how many hours will I work" — it's "whose word is the limit." Hours there bend to the relationship with your direct manager, for better and worse. If you're weighing Slovakia, the honest question is whether you can tolerate a workplace where the ceiling is high, precise, and paid for, but the floor — who talks first in a meeting, who sets the agenda — is just as fixed. The line I'd give a friend: Bulgaria will ask more of your patience with people; Slovakia will ask more of your patience with structure. Pick the kind of rigidity you can actually live with.
