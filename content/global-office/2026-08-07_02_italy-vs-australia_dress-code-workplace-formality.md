**The Suit and the Thong (Sandal): What Your Wardrobe Actually Signals in Milan vs. Sydney**

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Milan, what you wear to the office is understood to be a statement about your judgment, your employer, and possibly your family; in Sydney, the most common workplace dress code is a uniform, and the second most common is "smart casual," a category flexible enough to include sneakers (Michael Page Australia). One country treats clothing as a professional credential. The other treats it as, at most, a mild suggestion. Neither is wrong, but showing up to the wrong one dressed for the other will cost you more social capital than you'd expect.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a well-tailored suit or dress before your first week, not after | Wear anything visibly worn, wrinkled, or ill-fitting to a client meeting |
| Match your formality to the city — Milan is sharper than the south | Assume startup or creative-industry roles mean no effort at all |
| Treat email formality (Gentile Dottore/Dottoressa) as seriously as clothing | Default to casual American-style email openers with new contacts |
| Notice that quality trumps quantity — fewer, better pieces | Confuse "expensive-looking" with "flashy" — bella figura rewards restraint |
| Dress up slightly more for dinner than you did for the office | Assume the office dress code carries over to evening plans |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly what "smart casual" means at your specific office — it varies | Assume smart casual matches its meaning in the UK or US |
| Default to lighter, breathable fabrics — it's a hot-climate wardrobe | Show up over-dressed to a meeting; it can read as trying too hard |
| Keep one genuinely formal outfit on hand for client-facing exceptions | Assume every industry is as relaxed as tech or media |
| Expect footwear norms to loosen substantially outside finance and law | Wear thongs (flip-flops) to the office even in a beach city — it's still a line |
| Read dress-down as informality, not lack of professionalism | Mistake casual dress for a casual attitude toward deadlines or output |

---

Italian formality is not vanity, or not only vanity — it is a load-bearing part of how trust gets built. Fare bella figura, "making a good impression," extends well past appearance into behavior, manners, and how seriously you're seen to take your obligations; in the workplace this translates into the belief that looking sharp is itself evidence you take the job seriously (Italoamericano.org). World Business Culture's guidance is blunt on the mechanics: dark, well-tailored suits and ties are standard for men in most client-facing settings, and the standard barely relaxes even in tech and creative firms, where employees still dress "a step above their American counterparts." Business correspondence follows the same logic — formal openings like *Gentile Dottore/Dottoressa*, formal closings, and a general polish that would look stiff in an American inbox are simply table stakes (Expatica). None of this is about rules for their own sake. It's a high-context signaling system: your clothes are doing communication work that, in a lower-context culture, would be handled by a job title or a firm handshake.

Australia runs the opposite experiment. Survey data on workplace dress puts uniforms first at 26% and smart casual second at 25%, with fully formal business attire trailing at just 11% (Michael Page Australia). The distinction locals actually track is smart casual versus business casual — the former allows brighter colors, bolder accessories, and sneakers; the latter stays closer to trousers, blouses, and collared shirts (SEEK, Indeed Australia) — and getting that distinction wrong is a more common faux pas than under- or over-dressing outright. Regional variation compounds it: in tropical Brisbane, some workplaces swap trousers for Bermuda shorts, a concession to climate that would be unthinkable in a Milanese office regardless of the temperature outside.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part isn't that Italy is formal and Australia is casual — everyone expects that going in. It's that both cultures treat their dress norms as *moral* signals, just pointed in opposite directions. In Italy, dressing down can read as disrespect for the people you're meeting. In Australia, dressing up can read as insecurity, or worse, an attempt to pull rank. Import either instinct into the other country and you won't just look wrong — you'll be misread as arrogant, careless, or socially tone-deaf, which is a much costlier mistake than a bad outfit.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone advising on business travel to Italy noted that fashion there functions almost as a form of communication that "changes rapidly," and that Italians notice small sartorial details foreigners wouldn't think to track — a slightly wrong shoe or an unpressed collar registers immediately, even if no one says anything.*

> *TeamBlind — A finance employee comparing dress codes across offices noted that even within Australia, banking and law hold onto a formality closer to the American or British norm, while tech and creative firms operate on an almost entirely different, much looser standard — meaning the "casual Australia" reputation depends heavily on which industry you land in.*

> *InterNations — A relocating manager in Milan wrote that dressing one notch too casually for an early client meeting produced a coolness in the room she didn't fully register until a colleague later explained, gently, that people had quietly downgraded their first impression of her seriousness.*

> *A recurring theme among expats working in Sydney: the phrase "smart casual" on a job posting means something different at nearly every company, and the safest first-week strategy is simply to ask HR directly rather than guess and risk being the only person in the room in a full suit.*

> *A common observation among Northern European transplants to Italy: the effort required to maintain "bella figura" daily is genuinely time-consuming, and colleagues judge consistency — looking sharp only some days reads worse than dressing modestly but reliably well every day.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Italy, budget both money and time for your wardrobe before you budget for your commute — it is not optional infrastructure, it's part of the job. If you're moving to Australia, resist the urge to overdress as a hedge; ask what your specific office actually wears, because "casual" there is doing a lot more variable work than the word suggests. The one honest tip Priya would give a friend packing for either move: in Italy, dress for the job you want colleagues to believe you already deserve; in Australia, dress for the job, not for the impression — and if in doubt, undress one notch rather than over-suit the room.
