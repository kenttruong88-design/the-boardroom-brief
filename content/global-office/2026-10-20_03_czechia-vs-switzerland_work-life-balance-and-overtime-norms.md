# Czechia Works the Hours. Switzerland Works the System.

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ask a Czech office worker and a Swiss banker how much they work, and both will insist, with a straight face, that overtime is not really a thing where they are. Eurostat and Switzerland's own Federal Statistical Office disagree. Czechs post one of the European Union's longest average working weeks even before anyone mentions overtime, and Swiss full-time employees clock the single longest working week in Europe, at 42 hours and 33 minutes. Switzerland disguises this by having more than a third of its workforce, overwhelmingly women, work part-time, which drags the national average back down to something that photographs well. Two countries, two entirely different ways of making a long week look short on paper.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the word "přesčas" (overtime) and ask what your contract says about it before signing | Assume your 40-hour contract is the real number — unpaid overtime is routine in white-collar jobs despite the legal cap |
| Negotiate salary hard and in writing — Czechia ranks near the bottom of Europe for expat pay satisfaction | Expect Prague rents to track Czech wages — budget for the gap in advance |
| Clarify whether you're a standard employee or an OSVČ (self-employed) contractor | Sign an OSVČ arrangement without realizing it waives most sick leave, overtime, and notice protections |
| Use your full statutory leave and sick days — doctor's notes are respected | Mistake a manager's friendliness for flat hierarchy — it's softer, not absent |
| Consider Brno, Plzeň, or Olomouc if Prague's cost of living outpaces your offer | Expect retail, hospitality, or manufacturing jobs to mirror the better-paid IT and corporate sector |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether your role is "trust-based" (cadre) — overtime pay and time-tracking often don't apply | Assume Swiss precision extends to logging your hours — senior staff can work unrecorded overtime entirely legally |
| Expect genuine disconnection after hours — checking email on holiday reads as a personal failing, not dedication | Plan on six weeks of annual leave — the statutory minimum is four, and voters rejected six in a 2012 referendum |
| Price in childcare before assuming both partners will work full-time | Underestimate childcare costs — Swiss families spend roughly 41% of net income on it, against an OECD average of 13% |
| Confirm your contracted hours in writing — most firms run 40 to 44 a week, with legal sector ceilings of 45 to 50 | Treat 0.6 hours of average weekly overtime as the whole picture if you work in finance or a start-up |
| Ask about reduced-schedule options (often 90% pay-for-90%-hours) if burnout is a risk | Assume part-time is a junior or unusual arrangement — over a third of the workforce works it |

### 🇨🇿 Czechia

Eurostat's numbers, reported by Expats.cz in 2021, put Czechs at an average of 39.9 hours a week once part-time work is folded in, against an EU average of 37. That makes Czechia the fifth-hardest-working nation in the bloc, trailing only Greece (43.8 hours), Austria (42.1), Malta (42), and Poland (41.4). Part of the explanation is structural rather than cultural: only 5.7% of Czech employees work part-time, versus an EU average of 18.2%, so there is less of a shorter-hours cohort to pull the national figure down. The gender gap in hours is also unusually narrow by European standards — Czech men average 41.2 hours to women's 38.3 — which is either a quiet equity win or evidence that nobody gets the easy version of the week.

The law caps ordered overtime at eight hours a week, averaged over a 26-week reference period, with an annual ceiling of 150 hours imposed unilaterally and up to 416 hours total if the employee agrees, paid at a minimum 25% premium or banked as time off. On paper, that is a reasonably protective regime. In practice, a widely read Reddit thread on r/Prague, surfaced through a Reddit Threat Intelligence snapshot, found commenters split along class lines: white-collar workers describe unpaid overtime as something "nearly everyone is expecting," technically capped but socially unavoidable, while others in IT and corporate roles call the hours-to-salary ratio "incomparable with 90% of the world." Both can be true in the same country, often in the same office building.

InterNations' Expat Insider 2026 survey, reported by Expats.cz, backs the optimists on the narrow question of hours: expats work an average 40.6-hour week, below the global expat average of 42.2, and 75% say they're satisfied with their work-life balance, placing Czechia 13th of 31 destinations on that measure. The catch is pay. Czechia ranks 30th of 31 for fair compensation, with only 48% of expats feeling adequately paid. A 40-hour week that doesn't cover the rent reads longer than the contract suggests. Hofstede Insights scores Czechia at 58 for individualism and a notably low 29 for indulgence, a combination its own analysts read as a culture that keeps work and personal life firmly separated rather than blended — which may explain why so many Czechs resent the overtime creep without quite stopping it.

### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

Switzerland's Federal Statistical Office reported, as covered by IamExpat.ch in 2024, that full-time employees average 42 hours and 33 minutes a week including paid and unpaid overtime — the longest full-time week in Europe, four hours ahead of the EU average of 38 hours and 5 minutes. The purely contractual figure has eased slightly, down 46 minutes since 2018 to 40 hours and 12 minutes, but the headline number that gets quoted abroad is the "real" average across the whole workforce: 35 hours and 30 minutes, among the shortest in Europe. The gap exists because 37.6% of the Swiss workforce works part-time, and 72.1% of those part-timers are women — meaning Switzerland's enviable work-life reputation is subsidized by a labor split that looks considerably less enviable once broken down by gender.

Swiss law sets weekly maximums of 45 hours for industrial, office, and large-retail staff and 50 hours for most other sectors, with contractual overtime paid at a 25% premium or banked as leave, and a separate, harder-capped category of statutory overtime limited to 140 or 170 hours a year depending on sector. The average full-time employee reportedly works just 0.6 hours of overtime a week — a tidy number that excludes an entire category of staff. "Cadre," or trust-based, employees above a certain pay grade are exempt from mandatory time-tracking altogether, which is precisely what a Swiss bank-employee lobby group alleged was being abused at Goldman Sachs' Zurich office, prompting a labour-inspectorate visit. Denise Chervet of the Schweizerischer Bankpersonalverband put the concern plainly: "It is important to us that banks comply with rules governing working hours in Switzerland." The inspectorate's findings were never made public, which is itself a very Swiss outcome.

What the statistics don't fully capture is the texture of the boundary once you clock out. Matthew Deiter, a Silicon Valley engineer who spent two years working in Switzerland and Luxembourg, has described a workplace almost nobody stayed late in, even mid-crunch, and vacations that genuinely meant disconnection rather than an inbox quietly filling up. Switzerland's Hofstede scores — 68 for individualism, 66 for indulgence, both noticeably higher than Czechia's — track with that account: a culture comfortable both asserting personal boundaries and enjoying leisure once the boundary is set. KOF economist Jan-Egbert Sturm, cited by swissinfo.ch, frames the underlying ethic bluntly: "Hard work is seen as being a very good quality," a residue, he argues, of Calvinist tradition that keeps the hours long for those who are expected to work and genuinely short for everyone else.

### The Reckoning

On the official numbers, Switzerland works its full-time staff harder than Czechia does — 42 hours and 33 minutes against 39.9 — and yet leaves abroad with the better work-life balance reputation. The difference isn't the contract; it's what surrounds it. Swiss wages, four weeks of genuinely respected leave, and a culture that treats checking email on holiday as an embarrassment buy back the extra hours for most workers, while a trust-based loophole quietly lets the hardest-driven sectors exceed even that. Czechia's lower headline average, by contrast, hides real variation: a well-paid IT and corporate class working close to the InterNations figure of 40.6 hours with decent satisfaction, and a retail, hospitality, and manufacturing workforce for whom the 40-hour week, and the law that is supposed to cap overtime at it, functions more as a suggestion.

The honest comparison, then, isn't hours on paper but what the hours buy. A 42-hour Swiss week with a four-figure franc paycheck and a hard stop at 6pm can feel shorter than a nominally 40-hour Czech week undermined by unpaid "přesčas" and a salary that, by InterNations' own numbers, only 48% of people consider fair. Work-life balance, it turns out, is less a feature of the calendar than of the currency.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/Prague — one commenter on Czech white-collar work described unpaid overtime as something "nearly everyone is expecting," technically capped by law but socially unavoidable once you're salaried.*

> *r/Prague — a different commenter, working in IT, pushed back on the gloom, calling the local hours-to-salary ratio in corporate tech jobs "incomparable with 90% of the world" — a reminder that sector matters more than nationality.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider 2026, via Expats.cz — a British expat in Prague described the bureaucratic reception foreigners get as "very demeaning" when they don't speak Czech, a cultural misread that catches newcomers who assumed English-language competence at the office would extend to the paperwork around it.*

> *Matthew Deiter, a Silicon Valley engineer who worked in Switzerland and Luxembourg, via Gigazine — he was struck that almost no one stayed in the office after hours even during a startup crunch, and that Swiss colleagues were, in his words, "completely removed from work" on vacation, unlike the US habit of holidays interrupted by email.*

> *Denise Chervet of the Schweizerischer Bankpersonalverband, via HR Reporter — on the advice front, her union's complaint against Goldman Sachs' Zurich office is a pointed lesson for anyone assuming Swiss labor law protects everyone equally: ask, before you sign, whether your pay grade puts you in the "trust-based hours" category that nobody is tracking.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is lying about its work-life balance, exactly; both are just measuring it selectively. Czechia's lower average hides a split between a genuinely reasonable professional-class week and a less reasonable one everywhere else, with pay dissatisfaction doing the damage that hours alone don't. Switzerland's higher average hides behind a part-time workforce that is mostly female and a trust-based carve-out that exempts precisely the people working the most. If you're choosing between them for the actual experience of working there, the honest question isn't "how many hours," but "which sector, which contract category, and whose salary." Pick the wrong answer in either country, and you'll be checking your email on holiday regardless of what the national statistics office says about your week.
