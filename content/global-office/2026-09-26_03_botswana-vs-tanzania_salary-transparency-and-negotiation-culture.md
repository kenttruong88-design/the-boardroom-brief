# The Paperwork Is Transparent, the Paycheck Isn't: Salary Culture in Botswana and Tanzania

**🇧🇼 Botswana · 🇹🇿 Tanzania**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Botswana will hand you a contract specifying your base salary, your bonus formula, your housing allowance, and your pension contribution down to the percentage point — and then, in its flagship diamond industry, pay the people who actually cut and polish those diamonds as little as P1,900 a month. Tanzania will do the opposite: negotiate your salary through weeks of indirect, relationship-mediated conversation that never quite states a number until it has to — and Quora is still full of people, expats included, asking what a "reasonable" salary even looks like there. Transparency and fairness, it turns out, are not the same thing, and neither country has fully solved for both.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇼 Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get everything — base pay, bonus formula, housing allowance, pension percentage — in the written contract | Accept a verbal promise on benefits; the written contract is what governs, and locals know it |
| Benchmark against Gaborone specifically — it runs 5-15% above secondary cities | Assume a national average salary figure applies evenly across the country |
| Bring a language skill (Setswana helps even for expats) as real negotiating leverage | Expect qualifications alone to move the number without any local-market context |
| Expect a formal, well-documented process even for entry-level roles | Assume Botswana's careful paperwork means every sector pays fairly — extractive industries are a documented exception |
| Plan for a lengthy work-permit process if you're not on an embassy or diplomatic track | Assume local hiring will move quickly outside of specialized or diplomatic channels |

#### 🇹🇿 Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect negotiation to happen gradually and indirectly, often through a third party or over multiple conversations | Push for a fast, direct, one-meeting salary conversation — it can read as aggressive |
| Research comparable compensation carefully before you arrive — public benchmarks are thin | Assume your home-market salary expectations translate directly |
| Build the relationship with your manager or HR contact before raising money at all | Bring up compensation in your very first real conversation with a new employer |
| Expect decision-making on your offer to take longer than expected | Interpret a slow answer as a rejection or a bad sign |
| Ask multiple sources (recruiters, other expats, Quora threads, however imperfect) for a benchmark | Rely on a single data point for what counts as a "good" salary |

---

Botswana's approach to compensation is, on paper, one of the more formal and documented systems in the region. Salary guides for the country emphasize written contracts that spell out base pay, bonuses (typically one to three months' salary annually), housing allowances, medical insurance, and pension contributions of five to ten percent — a level of specificity that would read as unusually thorough almost anywhere. Gaborone commands a five-to-fifteen-percent premium over secondary cities, and qualifications or additional language skills, including Setswana fluency for expatriates, are explicitly recognized as leverage. And yet the country's most valuable export tells a different story: workers cutting, polishing, and sorting diamonds — the industry underpinning much of Botswana's wealth — earn between P1,900 and P3,000 a month, prompting the Botswana Diamond Workers Union to publicly demand, in the words of its national chairperson Dominic Mapoka, that "Botswana's diamonds must benefit those who create their value." Formal paperwork, in other words, doesn't automatically produce equitable outcomes.

Tanzania runs on a noticeably less codified system. Expat-focused labour guides describe Tanzanian workplace communication as indirect and decision-making as drawn-out — traits that extend naturally into how compensation gets discussed. There isn't a robust public benchmark culture to lean on: a real Quora thread asks, plainly, "What would be a reasonable compensation package for a senior position in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, at a European company managing the Tanzanian office?" — a question that, in a market with more salary transparency, would have an easy answer already circulating. It doesn't, which means most incoming professionals are negotiating semi-blind, calibrating against word-of-mouth and recruiter guidance rather than published data. On the Hofstede framework, Tanzania scores 64 on power distance — moderately hierarchical, consistent with a culture where a salary number often has to move through a relationship and a chain of approval before it becomes final. Botswana, notably, was never included in Hofstede's original country set, so no comparable official score exists — worth naming rather than inventing a number for.

Both systems produce their own kind of frustration for a newcomer. In Botswana, you'll get a beautifully specific document and then have to do independent homework to figure out whether the number in it is actually fair by any external standard. In Tanzania, you'll get a fair-minded, relationship-driven process and have to do independent homework because there's no document, or public data, to check anything against in the first place.

**The Reckoning:** The real irony here is that Botswana's formality creates a paper trail that makes its inequities easier to see and organize against — the diamond workers' union has hard percentages and a documented pay structure to point to when making its public case. Tanzania's informality does the opposite: it makes underpayment harder to prove because there was never a public benchmark to be underpaid against in the first place. A transparent contract with an unfair number and an opaque process with a fair-ish outcome will both leave you, as an individual negotiating your own move, needing to do far more independent research than either country's brochure suggests.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking what constitutes "a good salary in Tanzania" received answers spanning a wide range with little consensus, reflecting just how thin the public benchmarking data really is compared to markets with more established salary-transparency norms.*

> *Quora — A specific question about a senior, Europe-managed role based in Dar es Salaam noted that the asker had struggled to find any comparable compensation data before the interview process began, and was essentially negotiating from a cold start.*

> *Expat Arrivals (Tanzania) — Their working-culture guide describes decision-making as consistently "drawn-out" and communication as indirect, which contributors connected to how long it typically takes for any number, salary included, to become final.*

> *Tales Mag "Real Post Report" (Gaborone, Botswana) — Contributors describing the realities of local employment for expatriate spouses noted that most viable jobs run through the embassy circuit, that local hiring outside it is "pretty rare" due to protectionist work-permit policy, and that unreliable internet made remote/telecommute options genuinely unworkable for several respondents.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two, the practical question isn't "which country pays better" — it's "which kind of homework are you better equipped to do." Botswana will hand you the paperwork and expect you to independently judge whether the number is fair; Tanzania will make you build the relationship first and expect you to independently find the number at all. Bring your own external benchmark either way, because neither country's system is going to hand you one for free — and if you end up negotiating a diamond-sector job in Gaborone, read the union's public wage data before you sign anything, not after.
