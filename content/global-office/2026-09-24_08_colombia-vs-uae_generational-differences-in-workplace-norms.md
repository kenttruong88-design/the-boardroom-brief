# The Boss Is Younger Than You Now: Generations at Work in Colombia vs. the UAE

**🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇦🇪 UAE**


*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Colombia's younger workers are quietly rewriting a historically high-power-distance culture from the inside, trading deference for demands around autonomy and creative input. The UAE's younger nationals are doing something structurally different: choosing, for the first time in real numbers, to work in the private sector at all — a shift less about flattening hierarchy and more about whether to opt into the traditional civil-service path their parents took almost automatically. Both stories are generational, but only one is really about hierarchy.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect younger staff to ask for involvement in decisions, not just instructions | Assume respect for hierarchy has stayed constant across generations |
| Offer creative latitude and skill development — both drive millennial satisfaction | Lean only on pay as a retention lever for younger Colombian employees |
| Recognize tech-sector norms may already run closer to flat, US-style structures | Apply traditional high-power-distance assumptions uniformly across industries |
| Take younger employees' pushback on poor conditions seriously — it's a documented shift | Dismiss critical feedback from junior staff as generational impatience |
| Watch for a widening gap between older, formal managers and younger, vocal staff | Assume a manager's authority alone will resolve generational friction |

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect over half of young Emiratis to prioritize work-life balance explicitly | Assume all young Emiratis default to public-sector jobs, as prior generations did |
| Recognize family influence still shapes career decisions for many young nationals | Assume family input means less individual ambition — entrepreneurship is rising fast |
| Support mentorship and training — both are cited as structural gaps for young hires | Treat "fresher" status as permanent; assess actual competence, not just tenure |
| Understand Gen X still values close supervisor relationships more than Gen Z does | Manage every generation with the same relationship-heavy style |
| Recognize private-sector interest among Emirati youth is a genuinely new trend | Assume this shift means low loyalty — it often reflects broader opportunity-seeking |

---

Colombia's generational shift is documented with unusual clarity in workplace research. Talaera's Colombian business culture guide notes the country sits at a striking collectivism score of just 13, against 91 in the US, and a power distance score of 67, against 40 — numbers that describe a historically formal, hierarchical, group-oriented workplace. But academic research on Colombian millennials, drawn from 167 interviews across five major cities, found this generation prioritizes intrinsic job factors like creativity and applying their own knowledge over extrinsic rewards like pay, and is measurably more critical and less passive about poor working conditions than earlier cohorts. Employee involvement in decision-making raises millennial job satisfaction by 9.4 percentage points, a gap not seen as strongly in older workers, and ColombiaOne's 2026 reporting notes nearly 39 percent of young Colombians still identify access to a first formal job itself as their central economic concern — meaning generational change is unfolding inside a labor market that hasn't fully caught up on opportunity.

The UAE's generational story runs through a different structural lens: Emiratisation. Historically, Emirati nationals defaulted overwhelmingly toward public-sector employment, which offered stability, shorter hours, and strong benefits. Research published via ScienceDirect on UAE generational work values found Generation X places distinctly higher value on supervisor relationships and structured professional growth than Generations Y and Z, who show more individualistic tendencies and, notably, score lower on long-term loyalty to their broader social and organizational group than earlier generations did. Khaleej Times reports that over half of young Emiratis now cite work-life balance as a top priority when evaluating new roles, and PwC's 2024 Emiratisation Survey documents a genuine, growing shift toward private-sector interest among Emirati youth — a meaningful departure from the public-sector default, especially among women. Family still exerts real influence: 46 percent of young Emiratis cite parents and family as the primary shapers of their career decisions, but a close second, 31 percent, now cite the desire to start their own business, a distinctly individualistic and entrepreneurial signal layered on top of continued family involvement.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony is that Colombia's generational shift is primarily about voice — younger workers demanding a say in decisions within a structure that remains formally hierarchical — while the UAE's is primarily about venue — younger nationals choosing a different sector of employment altogether, within a family and cultural structure that remains largely intact. A Colombian manager watching younger staff push back in meetings is witnessing hierarchy being contested from inside; an Emirati employer watching young nationals choose a private-sector job over the traditional government post is witnessing loyalty being redirected, not necessarily hierarchy being flattened, since family influence over the decision itself often remains strong even as the destination changes.

Both countries share one real structural gap across generations: mentorship. Young Emiratis cite limited access to professional training and a shortage of mentorship as concrete barriers to entry-level roles, layered on top of intense competition for those same positions. Colombian millennials, meanwhile, report wanting more involvement and skill development from employers who often still default to top-down, formally hierarchical management styles inherited from an earlier generation. In both countries, the friction isn't really about younger workers rejecting structure outright — it's about structure failing to evolve fast enough to meet workers who want more investment in their development than the traditional model was built to offer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent addressing how millennials are changing corporate workplace culture broadly argued that the biggest shift isn't attitude but leverage: younger workers armed with more visible salary data and job-market information are simply less willing to accept opaque, take-it-or-leave-it terms that earlier generations tolerated by default.*

> *Quora — Someone asked how Colombian people are perceived in a business context and respondents converged on a specific tension: Colombian workers are widely seen as hardworking and loyal, a reputation that longtime employees actively maintain, even as younger entrants are simultaneously pushing back harder on conditions that reputation used to paper over.*

> *Quora — A poster asking about the pros and cons of working in the UAE as an inexperienced fresher was told candidly that early-career hires are treated as juniors regardless of prior credentials unless entering at a senior level, a structural reality that several respondents said shapes how younger nationals and expatriates alike experience their first few years there.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing Generation Z and millennial dynamics at work, answering as a young manager overseeing both groups, said the most visible difference wasn't ambition but expected pace of feedback — Gen Z staff wanted a response almost immediately, where millennials tolerated a longer, more traditional review cycle.*

> *Quora — A respondent researching how to break into the Bogotá job market from abroad noted that Colombian hiring, even for younger applicants, still runs heavily on personal referral and network access rather than open application alone — a reminder that generational change in attitude hasn't yet fully rewritten the mechanics of how people actually get hired.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing across generations in either country, the mistake is assuming the shift means the same thing in both places. In Colombia, younger workers are asking to be heard inside a structure that's staying largely intact — invest in involvement, not just perks. In the UAE, younger nationals are increasingly choosing where to work at all, often still with family blessing rather than in defiance of it — invest in mentorship and a credible private-sector career path, not just flexibility. The honest line I'd give a friend managing either team: don't mistake a request for input, in Bogotá, or a choice of sector, in Dubai, for a rejection of everything that came before it — both are more like renegotiations than revolutions.
