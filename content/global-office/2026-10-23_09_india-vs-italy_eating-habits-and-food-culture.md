**Desk Dabba or Three-Hour Table: A Relocation Guide to Lunch in India and Italy**

🇮🇳 India · 🇮🇹 Italy

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The relocating employee who imagines food culture as the easy part of the move — flavors change, logistics don't — has not yet met the Indian office canteen's default vegetarianism or the Italian colleague who will, without being asked, explain why your lunch is wrong. Indians spend nearly 30 percent of consumer expenditure on food against Italy's 14.7 percent (Our World in Data, USDA Economic Research Service, 2023), a gap that says less about appetite than about income and subsidy structures — and yet it is Italy, the richer, smaller-spending country, that treats the lunch hour as closer to a civic institution than a scheduling inconvenience. The real adjustment ahead has little to do with taste and everything to do with time, hierarchy, and who gets to decide when the meal is over.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the tiffin or dabba delivery system, or sign up for office catering, rather than eating out daily — it is standard professional practice, not a budget admission | Assume colleagues eat meat or eggs by default — many office canteens run strictly vegetarian, and bringing non-vegetarian food to a shared team lunch can cause real offense |
| Ask about dietary categories (pure vegetarian, Jain, no onion-garlic) before ordering a group meal | Eat or serve food with your left hand in shared settings — it reads as unclean regardless of intent |
| Accept at least a token portion when a colleague offers food from their tiffin — refusing outright is read as standoffish | Expect a long lunch hour — most corporate offices allot 30 to 45 minutes, and lingering past it can look like clock-watching |
| Join the recurring chai break even if you don't drink tea — it's a social fixture, not a beverage order | Skip chai invitations repeatedly — doing so signals aloofness more than health-consciousness |
| Keep your desk tolerant of spice gradually; ask for "less spicy" explicitly if ordering in | Make a show of your spice tolerance as a point of pride — it reads as performance, not camaraderie |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out the full team lunch when invited to a sit-down meal — leaving early is read as disrespecting the occasion | Ask for cheese on a seafood pasta, or otherwise improvise classic pairings — colleagues will correct you, gently or not |
| Learn the bar protocol: espresso standing at the counter, paid first at the register in most regions | Order a cappuccino or any milk-based coffee after a meal — espresso is the norm post-lunch and post-dinner |
| Expect office catering to run on a tight, 30-to-40-minute clock in most corporate settings, despite the national reputation for leisure | Schedule a meeting across the lunch window — it will be seen as oblivious, not efficient |
| Compliment the food sincerely and specifically, without comparing it to your home country's version | Deliver a lecture on "authentic" Italian food from your home country's diaspora restaurant — it rarely lands as intended |
| Plan around August, when much of the country, including corporate canteens, slows or shuts for weeks | Bring the same packed desk lunch every day while colleagues go out together — it reads as antisocial rather than thrifty |

Eighty percent of Indians limit meat in some way and roughly four in ten identify as vegetarian outright (Pew Research Center, 2021), which is why the default setting in most corporate cafeterias is vegetarian-first rather than vegetarian-as-option — the opposite assumption a European office makes. This is compounded by hierarchy: India's Hofstede Power Distance score of 77, among the highest measured, shows up at lunch in small, persistent ways — separate dining rooms for senior staff, who orders first, who waits. The tiffin and dabba system, epitomized by Mumbai's famously error-free dabbawalas — a logistics network accurate enough that it has been studied as a Six Sigma case by business schools — remains the backbone of how white-collar India actually eats on a workday: home-cooked food delivered mid-morning, eaten fast, often at the desk. A relocating employee who skips the tiffin ecosystem entirely, relying instead on delivery apps or restaurant lunches, will spend considerably more than the colleague receiving a dabba, and will also miss the daily social beat that comes with waiting for it to arrive.

That speed is not incidental. India's own statistical office, in its 2024 Time Use Survey, does not even break "eating" out as its own category — it is folded into a broad "self-care and maintenance" bucket averaging 708 minutes a day (MoSPI, 2025), a framing that quietly tells you meals are not treated as a protected, discrete block the way a European time-use survey would isolate them. One Gurgaon-based employee's complaint, aired on the professional forum Blind, was blunt: stale salads, dry chapatis, visible palm oil, and reported food poisoning, tied explicitly to the push for more in-office days. The advice from fellow commenters — bring your own lunch, escalate to HR, or report to India's food safety regulator — is itself a fair preview of how little institutional recourse a junior arrival should expect on this particular front.

Italy inverts almost every one of those defaults, and does so less generously than the brochure suggests. CIRFOOD's "Good Lunch Break Observatory," surveying workers who use company catering, found lunches outside the home rarely exceed 40 minutes, that one in five workers eats in under 20, and that only 43 percent of office workers eat lunch at the workplace two to three times a week at all — the long, wine-soaked lunch is largely folklore for anyone with a corporate badge. The same survey found 65 percent of workers now bring food cooked at home rather than dine out, and 52 percent buy ready-to-eat salads, soups, or sandwiches on the days they don't — habits that would look entirely ordinary in a Bangalore tech park, if slightly less likely to involve a dabba. What survives is the ritual coding around food rather than its duration: an American transplant quoted by The Local Italy described discovering that everything — shops, some offices, the whole rhythm of the day — can shut "for like three hours" in smaller towns, a schedule she'd never experienced eating at her desk in New York, even as she found herself eating dinner at nine at night within a year. A separate account in The Florentine recorded an American colleague nicknamed "impermeabile" — impervious — by her Italian office after two years of lunches (shrimp étouffée, a pear-and-ricotta pizza) that colleagues considered borderline alarming, less for the ingredients than for what they implied about her digestion.

The reckoning, then, is this: India polices what you eat — vegetarian defaults, hierarchy at the table, a tiffin system built for speed — while Italy polices how and when you eat, with far more tolerance for what's actually on the plate once you've accepted the clock and the rules around it. Italy's higher Hofstede Individualism score (76, against India's 48) would predict more personal latitude at the table, and in one sense there is — nobody tells you what to order. But the social surveillance around timing, pairing, and post-meal coffee choices is, if anything, more exacting than anything an Indian office canteen enforces on taste. Both countries, in short, have simply chosen different things to be strict about. For the relocating employee, the practical upshot is that neither country's rules are optional just because they're unwritten: an Indian office will forgive an unfamiliar accent long before it forgives non-vegetarian food turning up at a shared desk lunch, and an Italian office will forgive a grammatical mistake long before it forgives a cappuccino ordered at four in the afternoon.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An Expedia employee in Gurgaon described office food as "feeling like punishment": stale salads, dry chapatis cooked in excess palm oil, and recurring food poisoning complaints, worsened as the company pushed more in-office days. Commenters' advice was uniformly practical: bring your own tiffin and don't expect it to improve soon.*

> *The Local Italy — An American who relocated to Foligno recalled her first encounter with a genuine three-hour midday shutdown, a jolt after years of eating lunch at her desk in New York; within a year she had also drifted her dinner time from 5 p.m. to 9 p.m. without quite noticing the shift.*

> *TalesMag — A diplomatic household's post report on Ahmedabad warned that imported staples, including anything resembling decent coffee, were simply unavailable, and described local food as "sweet and oily Gujarati food" eaten in a dry state with no bar culture to fall back on socially.*

> *The Florentine — An American in an Italian office was teased for two years over lunches her colleagues found bewildering, including a pear-and-ricotta pizza, and was eventually nicknamed "impervious" to local food norms by her own boss — advice she passed on was to stop hiding the lunchbox rather than try to conform.*

> *Fishbowl — A thread comparing desk-lunch norms across companies found the habit considered ordinary in some countries and borderline strange in others, with one commenter dismissing the always-eat-at-your-desk pattern as "stupid work optics" rather than genuine productivity.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will let a newcomer eat on autopilot. India's workplace food culture rewards speed and deference — know the hierarchy, default to vegetarian unless told otherwise, and don't expect the lunch hour to protect itself from meetings. Italy's rewards patience and precision — the clock is shorter than its reputation suggests, but the unwritten rules around what goes with what, and when, are enforced with real social cost for getting them wrong. If there is one number to carry into the first week, it's not a calorie count or a spice level: it's that Italy's lunch break, for most corporate employees, is now barely longer than India's — the difference is that one culture will tell you so, loudly, and the other will just quietly log that you left your desk too long.

Plan for this separately from the cost of the move itself: the employee who treats food as a line item to minimize, rather than a system to learn, will spend their first six months mildly hungry, mildly confused, or both. The cheaper and faster adjustment, in either country, is to ask a colleague to explain the rules out loud, once, rather than to infer them meal by meal.

Pack accordingly, and in Rome, order the espresso standing up.
