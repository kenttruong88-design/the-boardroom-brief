**Two Cities, One Spreadsheet: What Manila and Hong Kong Actually Expect of You at Work**

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Manila, your manager will ask how your mother is doing before asking where the deck is. In Hong Kong, the deck had better already be in the inbox, formatted, and three drafts past the version you thought was final. Both cities will tell you, cheerfully, that this is simply how business gets done — and both are quietly running some of the longest working weeks in Asia while they say it. The University of Hong Kong found the average worker there logs 51.3 hours a week, a quarter above the International Labour Organization's 40-hour benchmark. Manila will not hand you a comparable number, because nobody there is counting; they are too busy asking about your mother.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Spend the first five minutes of any meeting on small talk — skipping it reads as cold, not efficient | Give blunt, public criticism; a flat correction said plainly can be heard as a professional death sentence |
| Learn to read a soft "yes" — if timelines seem to be agreed too easily, double-check scope and deadline out loud | Assume silence in a one-on-one means agreement; it more often means someone is avoiding disagreement |
| Build rapport before asking for favors or overtime; relationships (utang na loob) are the actual currency | Expect your title alone to generate initiative; juniors often wait for explicit instruction out of respect, not laziness |
| Acknowledge tenure and loyalty publicly — long service is a status marker, not just a line on a CV | Schedule big asks around family milestones, religious holidays, or typhoon season without flexibility |
| Use one-on-ones or written forms to collect real feedback, not group settings | Mistake warmth for a lack of seriousness — missed deadlines still carry consequences, just delivered gently |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify unpaid-overtime expectations before you sign, especially outside the civil service | Assume a 6pm contracted finish time is the actual finish time; treat it as the starting gun for "real" work |
| Match the pace — replies within the hour are normal, not eager | Plan on a five-day week by default; 60% of workers surveyed by the University of Hong Kong don't have one |
| Expect decisions at mainland-linked firms to be made in the head office, not the room you're in | Confuse a quiet office with a slow one; silence usually means everyone is already working |
| Negotiate the whole package — flights, housing allowance, schooling — not just base salary | Linger past your actual finish time to avoid leaving before your boss; roughly a quarter of workers admit to doing exactly this and call it normal |
| Learn the shorthand: "ASAP" in an email usually means "today," not "this week" | Expect the old expat package (free housing, guaranteed bonus); most of that disappeared years ago in favor of a bigger lump salary |

### Philippines

The Philippine office runs on relationship infrastructure that an outsider cannot see on an org chart. Kapwa — a sense of shared identity with the people around you — and pakikisama, the active work of getting along, aren't soft HR language; they're load-bearing. A manager who skips the small talk and goes straight to the ask will get technically correct compliance and nothing more. One who invests in it gets a team that will cover for each other during a family emergency without being asked (manilarecruitment.com). The flip side, documented by workplace consultancies advising foreign employers, is a communication style that avoids a direct "no" to anyone senior — so a candidate confirming a tight deadline is agreeing to try, not promising delivery, and the gap only shows up a week later (hiretalent.ph).

Hierarchy here is explicit and comfortable rather than resented: honorifics, deference to tenure, and a "firm but caring" management style rooted in malasakit — genuine concern for a subordinate's wellbeing — coexist with real accountability (manilarecruitment.com). Nearly half the population speaks fluent English, which smooths onboarding for foreign hires faster than almost anywhere else in Southeast Asia, but fluency in the language is not fluency in the subtext. Criticism lands privately; praise lands publicly; and the resourceful improvisation Filipinos call diskarte means problems get worked around quietly, sometimes for longer than a Western manager would tolerate before escalating.

### Hong Kong

Hong Kong's office culture reads, on the surface, as the opposite: fast, English-fluent, internationally fluent, and allergic to small talk. Underneath, it runs two systems in parallel. International and British-legacy firms operate close to a Western corporate rhythm. Mainland-linked and more traditional local firms run a sharper hierarchy, where a Michael Page survey of leavers from China-based companies in Hong Kong found 70% cited office culture and low pay as their reason for quitting, and staff who expected to shape strategy found that "most decisions were only made in the head office" (hrmasia.com). Either way, the hours are real: Hong Kong's Labour Department and a University of Hong Kong/Community Business survey both point to a workweek well above the ILO's 40-hour standard, with 61% of workers logging overtime and about a quarter staying late specifically to avoid being seen leaving before the boss.

The compensation logic has also shifted. The classic expatriate package — subsidized housing, guaranteed bonus, flights home, school fees — has largely given way to a single, larger salary number, with the city's low personal tax rate doing some of the work that benefits used to do. Annual leave typically starts around 7 to 10 days and climbs toward 21 with tenure, offset historically by a generous public holiday calendar. None of this is hidden; it's simply not advertised as a trade-off until you're the one negotiating it.

### The Reckoning

Both cities are collectivist by Hofstede Insights' measure — the Philippines scores 32 and Hong Kong 25 on individualism, both well below the global midpoint — but they organize that collectivism in opposite directions. Manila's version is horizontal and relational: the group is your colleagues, and loyalty flows sideways and up through genuine warmth. Hong Kong's version, especially in mainland-linked firms, is vertical and institutional: the group is the organization, and loyalty flows up through compliance with decisions made somewhere else. The Philippines also scores dramatically higher on power distance (94 versus Hong Kong's 68), which sounds backwards until you notice that Filipino hierarchy is personal and paternal — a boss who cares about your mother — while Hong Kong's is procedural and, in its more traditional corners, impersonal.

The irony is that the city with the gentler bosses has the harder deadlines to read, and the city with the harder-edged pace has the more legible ones. In Manila, a missed cue about unspoken disagreement will sink a project quietly. In Hong Kong, an unread email at 7pm will sink it loudly, by someone noticing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A commenter describing their year at a Big Four firm's Hong Kong office said weekday client engagements routinely ran until 8pm or later, and pinned the cause less on any single bad client than on "less advanced technologies and manual processes" compounding every overnight call with European and US teams.*

> *GoOverseas — Anthony Woo, a finance executive who relocated to Hong Kong, told interviewers to "forget about the laid back environment" and warned that staying past 10pm counts locally as merely "working late." His actual advice to newcomers was blunter than any orientation deck: "Sleep less."*

> *HRM Asia, reporting a Michael Page survey of departing staff — A former employee at a mainland-linked firm described the atmosphere turning visibly stiff the moment the boss walked in, and said decisions they'd expected to help shape were in fact "only made in the head office in China."*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey, via The Standard — A German respondent's complaint wasn't the hours or the hierarchy but the arithmetic underneath both: "The cost of living is too high, especially for housing," turning a competitive salary back into an ordinary one by the second month.*

The voices above lean on Blind, a verified survey, and two named interviews instead, rather than paraphrasing a quote that couldn't be verified.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on lifestyle alone, choose based on which kind of tired you'd rather be: Manila's is emotional — the quiet labor of reading a room that never says what it means — and Hong Kong's is physical, a straightforward accumulation of hours that at least announces itself on the clock. Both will pay you less in flexibility than the job ad implied and more in relationships or efficiency than the job ad bothered to mention. If a friend asked me over drinks, I'd tell them: take Manila if you want to be loved by your team and occasionally baffled by your deadlines, and take Hong Kong if you want to be respected by your team and occasionally horrified by your calendar.
