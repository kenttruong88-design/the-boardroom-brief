**Silence Is a Sentence: A Field Guide to Meetings in Chișinău and Dublin**

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Chișinău, a meeting that ends in polite silence probably means no. In Dublin, a meeting that ends in warm agreement probably also means no. Somewhere between these two forms of saying nothing sits the entire discipline of relocating for corporate work, and nobody puts that on the recruitment brochure. Both countries are, on paper, becoming easier places to build a European career — one as an EU candidate hurrying to align its institutions, the other as the continent's most entrenched multinational tax address — but the paperwork says nothing about what happens once you are actually sitting in the room. This guide exists for the person who has already accepted the offer, is now staring at a calendar invite in a language they half-understand, and needs to know what the silence or the smile actually means.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address senior colleagues by title and surname until explicitly invited to use first names | Assume a soft "that could be challenging" is anything other than a no |
| Send the agenda and expected duration at least a few days ahead of a meeting | Walk in without materials sent in advance — improvisation reads as unprepared, not agile |
| Treat a verbal yes as provisional until it is confirmed in writing or by the next meeting | Skip the hierarchy by raising an issue directly with someone's boss |
| Learn basic Romanian greetings and phrases; Russian still helps in many offices | Expect the agenda to loosen up before trust has been built over several meetings |
| Accept invitations to meals or toasts with business partners — they are where trust actually forms | Rush straight to business before the handshake round and small talk have run their course |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a few minutes of banter before the agenda starts, and join in | Mistake hedged language like "maybe" or "that's one way of looking at it" for agreement |
| Use first names quickly — hierarchy is flat day to day even if someone still owns the final call | Lead with your title or credentials; it reads as insecurity, not authority |
| Follow up any verbal commitment with a short email confirming what was actually agreed | Take a throwaway line like "I'll give you a ring, so" as a scheduled commitment |
| Let the agenda bend when the conversation wanders — that is often where the real decision gets made | Force a rigid agenda through when the room clearly wants to talk around the problem first |
| Use light self-deprecating humour to build rapport | Raise politics or religion unprompted in a work setting |

---

Moldova's meeting culture still carries its Soviet administrative inheritance, and the data on how people in the region actually relate to authority backs that up. An academic analysis out of Moldova's own Academy of Economic Studies, drawing on Hofstede Insights' comparative framework, puts the country at a Power Distance score of 90 and Individualism at just 17 — figures that sit close to neighbouring Romania and signal a workplace where rank is respected by default and group cohesion outranks individual initiative (ASE Moldova, CSEI Working Paper Issue 16). Hofstede Insights itself does not maintain Moldova on its primary public country-comparison page the way it does Ireland, so these numbers should be read as a credible secondary estimate rather than headline doctrine — worth citing once, not leaning on entirely.

What that translates to in practice is a meeting style built around formality first and informality earned later. Business guides and relocation services consistently describe early meetings in Moldova as scripted: handshakes, small talk about family or local cuisine, a fixed agenda, and conservative dress, with disagreement expressed through understatement rather than confrontation. The Moldovan National Bureau of Statistics does not track "meeting style" as a line item, obviously, but its labour force releases do confirm the structural backdrop: a workforce still adjusting to EU-alignment reforms, a shrinking working-age population due to outward migration, and a corporate sector where multinational employers are a recent and still-small addition to a largely domestic, hierarchical business culture (statistica.gov.md, Labour Force in the Republic of Moldova). New arrivals should expect the real decision to happen after the meeting, in a follow-up conversation between the senior people who were technically "just listening" in the room.

The upside, rarely mentioned, is that this formality is legible. Invest Moldova's own analysis of foreign direct investment notes that multinational employers remain a small, recent slice of the economy, which means a newly arrived manager is often the first foreign face a Moldovan team has worked with directly, and the extra ceremony is partly a hedge against a relationship nobody has tested yet (Invest Moldova Agency, 2024 FDI impact study). Earn the trust once, over a few shared meals and a few meetings that go exactly to agenda, and the hierarchy that felt rigid in week one starts working in your favour: everyone already knows who is meant to be listened to, and once that includes you, decisions move.

Ireland sells itself, correctly, as the easy option — and for once the marketing holds up under scrutiny. An InterNations global survey found 71 percent of expats in Ireland describe local business etiquette as easy to understand, versus 60 percent in second-ranked Estonia and 54 percent in the UK, putting Ireland second worldwide behind only Singapore (as reported by GPA.net and Dublin Live). The catch is that "easy to understand" and "easy to interpret" are not the same thing. Ireland's flat day-to-day hierarchy (Hofstede's own published Power Distance score for Ireland sits at 28, against Moldova's estimated 90) and heavy use of first names mask a communication style built on indirectness, hedging, and humour, where a flat "no" is almost never said aloud and a cheerful "grand, sure" can mean anything from genuine agreement to a polite brush-off.

Ireland's Central Statistics Office data on hybrid work adds a practical wrinkle to all this. As of Q2 2026, 64.8 percent of the employed workforce never works from home at all, while fully remote arrangements have fallen from their Q1 2021 pandemic peak of 846,700 people to 529,800 — a steady pull back toward the office since the pandemic eased (CSO, Labour Force Survey Q2 2026). That matters for meeting culture specifically: most of the indirectness a new arrival needs to decode will happen in person, across a conference table or over coffee, not in the relatively legible medium of a written Slack message, where hedging is harder to disguise. Budget for more in-person reading-between-the-lines than the job posting implied.

The Reckoning, then, comes down to a single counterintuitive point: Moldova's formality is easier to decode than Ireland's informality. In Chișinău, hierarchy is visible, so you know exactly whose approval you are missing and can ask for it directly, if respectfully. In Dublin, hierarchy is deliberately invisible, so a newcomer can sit through four meetings' worth of friendly, flat-sounding discussion without realising that one quiet, joking colleague was the actual decision-maker the entire time. Moldova asks you to be patient and deferential; Ireland asks you to be fluent in a dialect of politeness that no onboarding deck will teach you.

Where the two countries genuinely converge is in what they punish. Both, in their own register, punish bluntness. Pushing for a fast yes/no answer reads as rude in Chișinău and as faintly aggressive in Dublin, and in both cities the people who get things done are the ones willing to let a decision breathe rather than forcing it in the room.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TalesMag — A U.S. Embassy spouse posted to Chișinău noted that in some business settings a man entering a room would shake hands with every other man present and walk straight past the women, a pattern she observed "in rural areas" but also "in Chișinau as well" — a small, easy-to-miss handshake etiquette that catches newcomers off guard in their first week of client meetings.*

> *Expat.com forum (Moldova board) — A long-time poster warned that non-citizens find it "not complicated to gain employment" in physical or entry-level roles — mover, assembly-line operator, cashier — but that better-paid office positions such as bookkeeper or office manager effectively require Moldovan citizenship, a distinction relocation packages rarely spell out upfront.*

> *Hacker News — A former engineering manager recalled his first visit to a Google Dublin SRE managers' meeting, where a colleague raised, almost as an aside, that alcohol was too visibly displayed around the office; the group discussed it calmly and had changed the policy by the end of the meeting, which he cited as a pleasant surprise in how quickly Dublin teams converted a soft-spoken concern into a firm decision.*

> *RTÉ Brainstorm — A French newcomer described how her Irish housemates would only accept a cup of tea after being asked roughly three times, and how a casual sign-off like "I'll give you a ring, so" was pure filler rather than a scheduled commitment — a pattern of face-saving indirectness she had to relearn before it cost her a real deadline at work.*

> *InterNations survey (via GPA.net) — The same data that ranks Ireland second-easiest in the world for business etiquette also notes that expats in far more hierarchical, high-context cultures made more workplace errors early on — practical confirmation, for anyone moving from Moldova's end of the spectrum, that the first few Irish meetings are the ones most likely to be misread.*

---

### Conclusion

The practical difference that matters most is this: in Moldova, learn the hierarchy and you can navigate almost everything else by simply respecting it; in Ireland, learn the hierarchy and you have learned almost nothing, because the real information travels through tone, humour, and what gets left unsaid. A new arrival in Chișinău should spend their first month mapping who reports to whom. A new arrival in Dublin should spend their first month learning to hear "maybe" as "no" and a joke as a genuine point being made sideways.

Either way, the same rule applies: whoever is quietest in the room is usually the one actually deciding something, and the fastest way to misread a meeting on either side of the EU border is to mistake politeness for consent.
