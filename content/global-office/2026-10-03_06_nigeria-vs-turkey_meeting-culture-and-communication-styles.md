**In Lagos, the Meeting Starts With Twenty Minutes of Greetings. In Istanbul, It Starts With Tea You're Not Allowed to Refuse**

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇹🇷 Turkey

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A foreign manager who opens a Lagos meeting by immediately launching into the agenda will be technically on time and socially behind by a full round of handshakes, titles, and "how is your family" that everyone else quietly expected first. A foreign manager who opens an Istanbul meeting by declining the offered çay to "save time" will be read, gently but unmistakably, as someone who doesn't yet understand how business actually gets done there. Both countries run on relationship-first communication. Neither one will tell you that directly — you're expected to notice.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for greetings, titles, and small talk before any meeting gets to business | Launch straight into the agenda; it reads as rushed and mildly disrespectful |
| Address people by professional or honorific titles until invited to do otherwise | Default to first names with someone senior to you on a first meeting |
| Expect decisions to route through the most senior person in the room, even if others spoke more | Assume the most talkative person in the meeting is the actual decision-maker |
| Build the relationship across multiple meetings before expecting a firm commitment | Push for a signed decision in a single first session |
| Treat indirect pushback ("we will look into it") as a real signal, not vague politeness | Take a soft "let's see" as agreement; it often isn't |

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the offered tea or coffee — declining it is read as declining the relationship itself | Decline hospitality to "get to the point faster"; it backfires on trust |
| Expect the meeting's real decision-maker to speak last, after everyone else has had their say | Address only the most vocal participant, assuming they hold the authority |
| Allow extended personal conversation before the agenda — it's not a delay, it's the groundwork | Treat small talk as wasted time to be minimized |
| Read a lot into tone, pauses, and what's left unsaid — this is a high-context culture | Take a literal, purely verbal reading of a Turkish counterpart's response |
| Expect follow-up to happen through warm, ongoing personal contact, not just email | Assume one good meeting is enough to secure a lasting business relationship |

### Nigeria

Nigerian business communication runs on a foundation of greeting, title, and relationship that most Western meeting culture treats as optional preamble and Nigerian meeting culture treats as the actual first half of the meeting. Commisceo Global's management guide and multiple business-etiquette primers converge on the same core point: skipping or rushing greetings reads as a genuine social misstep, not mere brusqueness, and seniority-based titles (Chief, Doctor, Engineer, or simply "Sir"/"Ma") carry real social weight that a flattened, first-name Western office style will register as disrespect rather than friendliness.

Hofstede's data situates Nigeria at a notably high power distance, and the practical communication consequence, covered by outlets like The Guardian Nigeria's own reporting on local communication norms, is that direct criticism of someone senior, or a flat "no," is genuinely rare in professional settings — disagreement tends to arrive indirectly, through hedged language, deferred answers, or routing feedback through a trusted intermediary rather than stated outright in the room. A foreign manager who reads Nigerian colleagues' politeness as agreement, rather than as the expected form disagreement takes, will consistently misjudge where a project actually stands.

### Turkey

Turkish business meetings run on a similarly relationship-first logic, delivered through a distinctly different ritual: hospitality. The Cultural Atlas's analysis of Turkish communication describes it as a textbook high-context style, where meaning sits heavily in tone, context, and what's deliberately left unsaid, and where the offer of tea or coffee at the start of any meeting functions as a genuine opening move in the relationship, not a beverage service. Declining it, per multiple expat and business guides including Ikamet's, is read as a small but real signal of disinterest in the relationship itself, regardless of how efficiency-minded the refusal was intended to be.

Hierarchy shapes the room differently than in a flatter culture too: Hofstede's Turkey data shows meaningfully high power distance alongside notably high uncertainty avoidance, and the practical tell, per Commisceo Global's Turkey guide, is that the most senior person present often speaks last, having let others lay out positions first — a sequencing that a foreign counterpart unfamiliar with it can easily misread as that senior figure being disengaged, right up until they deliver the actual decision at the end.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures front-load relationship before business — they just use a different ritual to do it. Nigeria's version is verbal and title-heavy: greetings, honorifics, and acknowledgment of hierarchy, delivered out loud before the agenda even opens. Turkey's version is material and sensory: tea, hospitality, and unhurried small talk, delivered through gesture and pacing rather than spoken formality. A foreign hire who's internalized one country's ritual will often perform it, slightly wrong, in the other — offering tea in Lagos when the moment calls for greetings, or rushing through greetings in Istanbul when the moment calls for tea.

The sharper shared trap is reading politeness as agreement. In both cultures, a flat "no" in a business meeting is genuinely uncommon — Nigerian indirectness tends to route through deferred timelines and intermediaries, Turkish indirectness tends to route through tone and context you're expected to infer. A foreign manager who treats either country's soft "we'll see" as a yes is setting up the exact same disappointment two different ways.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent on the "Nigerian1" Quora space answering what foreigners should know about doing business in Nigeria emphasized that greetings and acknowledgment of seniority aren't a courtesy layered on top of the meeting — skipping them is itself read as the business mistake, independent of how good the actual proposal is.*

> *Quora — A contributor describing what it's like doing business with people in Nigeria noted that the most common foreign misread is treating a warm, agreeable meeting as a closed deal, when in local practice warmth and commitment are two separate signals that don't necessarily arrive together.*

> *Ikamet (Turkish work-culture guide) — The guide specifically flags tea-refusal as a recurring foreign misstep, noting that newcomers trying to seem efficient by declining hospitality routinely create more friction than the time they thought they were saving.*

> *Quora — A respondent answering on risks for foreign business owners in Nigeria noted that the single highest-value adjustment wasn't legal or financial but communicative: learning to sit through what felt like excessive relationship-building before any real negotiation began, rather than treating it as a delay to push past.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading into Nigerian meetings, build in real time for greetings and title-based deference, and listen for what's not being said as closely as what is. If you're heading into Turkish meetings, drink the tea, let the senior voice speak last, and resist the urge to read silence or small talk as stalling. Neither communication style is slower than the direct, agenda-first style you might be used to — it's just front-loaded differently, and skipping the front-loading is the one mistake both cultures will quietly, politely, never let you forget.
