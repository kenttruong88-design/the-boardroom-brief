# Poland Hides the Salary, the Netherlands Hides the Conversation

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Poland, according to a Grant Thornton analysis of roughly 292,000 job postings published in October 2026, only 24 percent of advertisements state a salary, and most of those give a range wide enough to park a tram in. In the Netherlands, pay appears in adverts more often, yet 1,100 workers surveyed this spring reportedly regarded the prospect of discussing it with colleagues as a social hazard. One country hides the number and wishes it would be told. The other knows the number and wishes it would not be mentioned.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the salary range before the first interview; since 23 December 2025 employers are meant to supply it | Answer a question about your current or previous pay, which employers may no longer ask |
| Quote figures as monthly gross (brutto), the way Poles do | Compare offers without checking gross, net and total employer cost |
| Ask which contract type is on offer, employment (UoP) or B2B | Assume a B2B rate and a salary are the same number |
| Check the city: Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław and Gdańsk differ in pay | Anchor on a Warsaw figure for a job in a smaller city |
| Ask for benefits separately: private healthcare, sports card, bonuses | Treat a very wide advertised range as a promise of its upper end |
| Request the pay rules or collective agreement if the employer cites them | Wait until the offer stage to raise money |

### In the Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the gross annual figure and confirm both sides mean the same thing | Accept a first offer on reflex |
| Remember holiday pay of 8 percent is normally paid on top of the stated salary | Forget to ask whether the quoted figure includes it |
| Back your request with a short, specific list of achievements | Pad the ask with "maybe", "just" and "sorry" |
| Ask about extras: travel allowance, pension, training budget, remote days | Fix on base salary alone and ignore the package |
| Stay calm and polite when you counter | Perform bravado; being "too much" reads badly |
| Check whether the 30 percent ruling applies to you before you name a figure | Assume the ruling is automatic because a recruiter mentioned it |

## Poland

Poland has begun the legal part of transparency without much of the cultural part. According to law-firm briefings from the IBA and JD Supra, amendments to the Labour Code took effect on 23 December 2025, requiring employers to give candidates the starting pay or range, in the advertisement, before the interview, or before employment begins. Employers may not ask about previous pay, and advertisements must be gender-neutral. The rest of the EU Pay Transparency Directive, covering pay reporting and employee information rights, was in draft form from December 2025, with a target date of 7 June 2026. Readers should check where it stands before relying on it.

The data suggest habit outlasts statute. Grant Thornton found that 24 percent of postings carried salary information, up from 21 percent a year earlier, and that 93 percent of those disclosures were ranges, many of them very broad. This is not for want of public appetite. A CBOS poll of 1,012 Poles in March 2026, reported by Polskie Radio, found that 92 percent said candidates should learn the salary range before interviews, 66 percent opposed employers barring staff from discussing earnings, and 58 percent opposed questions about previous salary. Poland, in short, wants transparency and is receiving an interpretation of it.

On the gender dimension, Eurostat, as reported by Warsaw Business Journal, put Poland's 2023 unadjusted pay gap at 7.8 percent against an EU average of 12.0 percent. This is among the lowest in the Union, though a narrow gap is not a transparent one. For an applicant the practical hazards are mundane. Pay is usually quoted monthly and gross, contract type changes the arithmetic considerably, and a range that runs from modest to generous tells you the employer has a budget and has declined to describe it. The new rules do not forbid vagueness so much as invite you to ask again, in writing, until it stops.

## Netherlands

The Dutch are famously direct and, on the subject of money, remarkably reserved. The EU directive has been in force since June 2023, but according to Global Workplace Insider the Dutch Minister of Social Affairs and Employment said in September 2025 that the original June 2026 timetable was unachievable, and implementation is now expected on 1 January 2027. The rules will require employers to disclose initial pay or a range in vacancies and bar questions about salary history. The same source notes that courts may read existing Dutch rules in light of the directive in the meantime.

Culture is the bigger obstacle. NL Times, reporting in May 2026 on a survey of 1,100 workers by the payroll platform Deel, said many Dutch employees are wary of the directive chiefly because they would have to discuss pay with colleagues, and that pay remains something of a taboo. The same report noted that women earned 10.5 percent less per hour than men last year, which is what the directive is partly meant to address. Negotiating itself is normal. IamExpat advises that a first offer is often not the final one, that one should negotiate the gross annual figure, and that holiday pay of 8 percent comes on top, behaving like a thirteenth month. Confidence is welcome. Swagger is noticed.

The practical upshot for a newcomer is a negotiation conducted in a low voice. You are expected to know your market value, state it evenly, and justify it with evidence, not volume. The 30 percent tax ruling features in so many expat conversations that it is worth confirming eligibility in writing and not on a recruiter's cheerful word, since a forum member in one thread pointedly declined to guess how the tax authority would rule. Dutch employment also tends to come with documents, collective agreements and sector rules, and the polite course is to read them before, not after, you start asking for more.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries have the same law and opposite problems. Poland has a legal duty to publish and a market that publishes sparingly, and a public that, according to CBOS, overwhelmingly wants more. The Netherlands has less of a publishing problem and a larger awkwardness problem: workers whom the Deel survey found uncomfortable with the very openness the law will encourage. For a job seeker, Poland is the place where you must pry the number out, and the Netherlands is the place where, having been given it, you must discuss it with composure.

Hofstede Insights scores, as tabulated by Clearly Cultural, give a rough frame. Poland scores 68 on power distance and 93 on uncertainty avoidance, against 38 and 53 for the Netherlands, which fits a Polish habit of guarding information and a Dutch habit of flattening hierarchy. The Netherlands scores 14 on masculinity against Poland's 64, which fits the Dutch preference for modest self-presentation and the Polish tolerance for a more assertive pitch. The scores are national averages dating from decades of research and should be handled as such. Neither country will be improved by your reading of them, but your first salary conversation might be.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/thenetherlands — In a 2015 thread, commenters advised newcomers with no Dutch pay history to ask the employer what range they typically offer, to set a floor and a target, and to ask slightly above target. One noted that haggling is less common in the Netherlands, so politeness and readiness to decline matter.*

> *Reddit r/warsaw — Commenters on a 2024 salary-data thread complained that Polish pay is quoted monthly and gross, that B2B and employment contracts differ sharply, and that net, gross and total employer cost are easy to confuse. One advised asking for each figure separately.*

> *Expat.com Netherlands forum — A highly skilled migrant weighing an offer was told by another member that holiday pay is generally paid in arrears around May or June, that its timing may depend on the sector's collective agreement, and that a tax adviser, not a forum, should confirm the 30 percent ruling. The thread is several years old.*

> *Expat.com Poland forum — A Dutch procurement professional hoping for 8,000 to 10,000 PLN a month was told the answer depends on the city, that capitals pay more, and that saving much money in Poland might be optimistic. The thread is roughly a decade old.*

> *Hacker News — In a 2021 discussion about European engineer pay, a commenter living in the Netherlands described high earners as often treated with suspicion, and noted that public and publicly funded bodies cap pay near the prime minister's salary.*

## Conclusion

If you are weighing the two, treat the legal reforms as leverage and not as a guarantee. In Poland, cite the new rules, ask for the range early, insist on a clear statement of gross, net and contract type, and compare offers by city. In the Netherlands, prepare a short written case, negotiate the gross annual figure, ask about holiday pay, pension and allowances, and have the 30 percent ruling confirmed before you sign anything. Both countries are heading, by slightly different routes and timetables, towards the same directive. Check the current status of each before you rely on it, since the Polish second stage and the Dutch law were both still moving in the sources consulted, and ask any prospective employer, in writing, how it intends to comply.

Poland will tell you what it pays when the law and the recruiter finally agree, and the Netherlands will tell you with great calm what you may not ask your colleagues. In both cases, the number is available, provided you ask politely.
