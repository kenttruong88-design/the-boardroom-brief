**Who Pays For The Cake: Office Rituals in Bucharest and Dublin**

**🇷🇴 Romania · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In a Romanian office, if it's your birthday, you buy the cake. In an Irish office, if it's your birthday, your colleagues might buy you a pint — but only after an unspoken debate about whether lunchtime drinking is still socially permitted at all, a debate several Dubliners are visibly still having with each other in real time on the country's own internet forums. Both cultures build real relationships through food and drink at work. Only one of them has fully made peace with who's supposed to be paying.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇴 Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan to bring treats for colleagues on your own birthday — the custom across the region runs in reverse from most Western offices | Expect colleagues to throw you a surprise party; the social obligation points the other direction |
| Acknowledge a colleague's Name Day (their saint's day on the Orthodox calendar) with a small gesture if you know it | Assume Name Days have disappeared entirely; they're less universal than they used to be but still show up in many offices |
| Offer a small Mărțișor trinket to female colleagues around March 1st if the office observes it | Treat it as romantic; it's a seasonal goodwill gesture, not a date invitation |
| Bring treats yourself when you leave a job or join a new team, mirroring the birthday custom | Assume a work friendship automatically includes a wedding invitation; cash-gift envelope customs at Romanian weddings carry real social weight, so don't accept casually |
| Keep client entertaining appropriately formal at first; relationship-building still often runs through structured, not improvised, settings | Assume flat, Western-style team culture is standard everywhere; one well-known local critique argues Romanian workplaces often lack a genuine team-first culture by habit |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to the office coffee round or after-work pub trip as the real relationship-building venue, not the meeting room | Assume everyone drinks, or that declining alcohol marks you out; ordering a soft drink and staying engaged is completely normal |
| Treat Christmas as the one reliably sanctioned moment for a proper work social, drinks included | Assume that's true year-round; several long-time posters on Irish forums describe daytime drinking as flatly "not done" outside that window |
| Use humour early — gentle teasing in a meeting is a sign of acceptance, not hostility | Take a joke at your expense personally; matching the tone back is usually the better response |
| Keep small talk to family, weather, and traffic | Bring up salary in casual conversation; it's reliably named as the one topic to actively avoid |
| Accept a home dinner invitation if one comes, and bring a small gift (wine or chocolates) | Overdo the gift or the affection; a hearty backslap is fine, but cheek-kisses and hugs aren't the local default |

### Romania

Romanian office social rituals run on a reversed version of the Western birthday script: the person having the birthday brings the treats, not the other way around, a custom shared broadly across Central and Eastern European offices and one that regularly surprises newcomers expecting to be celebrated rather than expected to celebrate everyone else. Name Days — marking a colleague's patron saint on the Orthodox calendar — add a second, less universal but still-present layer of office goodwill that has no real Western equivalent; Mărțișor gestures each March 1st add a third, seasonal one specifically for female colleagues (whitemountain.ro).

What's genuinely contested, even among Romanians themselves, is whether a deeper "team culture" exists at all in the Western sense. A long-running local media critique has argued that Romanian hiring and workplace norms still quietly filter for a narrow "standard portrait" of acceptable employee — screening out, among others, young women (over maternity leave concerns) and older candidates (over perceived cost or enthusiasm) — which the columnist connects directly to why genuine team cohesion struggles to take root when the hiring process itself discourages a diverse, trusting group from forming in the first place (romania-insider.com). Team-building as a formal, purchased activity — a real commercial market in Romania — exists partly to manufacture, artificially and after the fact, the cohesion that informal office rituals don't always build on their own.

### Ireland

Ireland's office social culture is built around the pub, the coffee, and the lunch — but it's currently mid-renegotiation about exactly how much of that is still acceptable during work hours. Dublin.ie's own description of local business culture leans into after-work drinks and casual venues (bars, restaurants, even golf courses) as the real site of relationship-building, where "contacts often become friends" and deals get nurtured outside the formal meeting (dublin.ie). But ask actual workers on Ireland's own community forums, and the picture sharpens considerably: multiple long-time posters describe daytime drinking as flatly taboo. One put it as being "frowned upon down here, unless it's around Christmas time"; another said flatly that in Dublin, lunchtime drinking "just is not done" (boards.ie).

The exceptions prove how firm the rule otherwise is. A handful of posters described occasional Thursday or Friday lunchtime pints as a holdover from an earlier, more permissive era, now rare enough that one commenter questioned whether it was ever really common post-pandemic at all. The consequences of ignoring the unwritten rule are social, not legal: regular lunchtime drinkers get quietly read as "problematic," and visible daytime drinking invites direct management scrutiny even where no written policy technically bans it (boards.ie).

### The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights puts Ireland at 70 on individualism against Romania's 30, and the ritual structures reflect that gap almost perfectly. Romania's rituals are collective obligations with clear reciprocal rules — you bring the cake because it's your turn, full stop — while Ireland's rituals are individually negotiated and increasingly contested, with each office, generation, and even each employee deciding in real time how much of the old pub-centric script still applies to them personally. Romania's much higher uncertainty avoidance (90 against Ireland's 35) shows up as fixed, unambiguous custom: nobody debates whether the birthday person buys the cake. Ireland's lower score shows up as exactly the kind of open, unresolved forum argument about lunchtime pints that would simply never happen in a culture with a fixed answer already baked in.

The genuinely funny irony: the culture famous worldwide for pub culture is the one quietly having a very serious, unresolved internal argument about whether that reputation is still earned at the office. The culture with the much stricter, clearer unwritten rules is the one nobody's debating at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Boards.ie — EmmetSpiceland described daytime office drinking as "frowned upon down here, unless it's around Christmas time," adding that management would "raise questions" if staff were seen drinking at lunch any other time of year.*

> *Boards.ie — Mikemac2 put Dublin's actual norm in four words: lunchtime drinking "just is not done," directly contradicting the pub-culture reputation newcomers often arrive expecting.*

> *Boards.ie — Oceanman described a genuine exception: a small, regular group that still has "a few during lunch hour," usually confined to Thursdays and Fridays — proof the old custom survives in isolated pockets rather than having vanished everywhere at once.*

> *Boards.ie — Arghus offered the blunter read on why the custom faded: regular lunchtime drinkers were, in their experience, usually "problematic drinkers," not simply traditionalists holding onto an old social ritual.*

### Conclusion

If there's one piece of advice that travels across both postings, it's this: watch who pays for the first round, in whatever form "the round" takes locally. In Romania, if it's your birthday and you haven't brought anything in, you've already made your first cultural mistake. In Ireland, if you're reaching for your wallet at a lunchtime pub table on a random Tuesday, you've made a different one — not of generosity, but of timing. The ritual that actually matters in both places isn't the drink or the cake itself. It's correctly reading whose turn it is.
