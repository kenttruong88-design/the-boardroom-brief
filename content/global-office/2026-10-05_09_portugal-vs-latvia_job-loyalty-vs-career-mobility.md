**The Permanent Contract and the Stepping Stone: Job Loyalty vs. Mobility in Portugal and Latvia**

**🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇱🇻 Latvia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Portugal still treats a permanent contract — the "efetivo" — as something close to a life achievement, worth protecting and rarely walking away from voluntarily. Latvia, by contrast, has quietly become a place people pass through on the way to somewhere else: a calm, affordable, pleasant stepping stone rather than a career destination. Neither country is wrong about its own labor market. They're just optimizing for opposite goals.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a permanent contract offer as genuinely valuable — job security there carries real cultural and practical weight | Assume job-hopping every 18-24 months reads as ambition here the way it might in a US tech market |
| Expect decision-making to sit with senior leadership, with limited downward consultation, especially in traditional firms | Push hard for flat, consensus-driven team structures on day one; it can read as undermining the hierarchy |
| Invest time in long-term relationship-building before expecting real collaboration or trust from colleagues | Expect younger, multinational-adjacent offices to behave like traditional Portuguese firms — the generational split is real |
| Know that nearly 1 in 5 workers (and disproportionately women) hold temporary rather than permanent contracts — ask directly about your own contract type | Assume full-time, permanent status if it isn't explicitly confirmed; the security gap between contract types is large and consequential |
| Expect caution around organizational change — new ideas need a proven track record before being adopted | Treat a slow "yes" as a "no"; it's often just the system's normal pace for anything untested |

#### 🇱🇻 Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Latvia honestly as a strong stepping-stone market — valuable international experience, not necessarily a 20-year career home | Assume long tenure at one employer is the local norm or expectation; mobility, especially among skilled workers, is common |
| Enjoy the comparatively calmer work culture — multiple accounts describe it as less frantic than larger Western job markets | Expect the pace or intensity of a major Western European or US market; "calmer" is a widely reported feature, not a complaint |
| Budget realistically — shared housing and daily costs run notably low, which supports saving even on a modest salary | Mistake low cost of living for low quality of life; multiple accounts describe the trade-off as a genuine net positive |

| Use Latvia's relatively open job market (59% say it's a good time to find work locally) to negotiate rather than settling for the first offer | Treat every job offer as scarce or precious; the local labor market perception is notably more optimistic than the regional average |

### Portugal: Loyalty as Load-Bearing Infrastructure

Portuguese workplace culture treats hierarchy and job security as load-bearing, not incidental. Commisceo Global's management guide describes supervisors as presumed to hold "more experience and greater knowledge" than those they manage, with roles "clearly defined and strictly followed" because maintaining that structure "helps to keep order" — stability flows from role adherence, not from formal guarantees alone. The culture also runs genuinely paternalistic and relationship-first: trust and personal rapport are preconditions for real collaboration, not pleasant extras, and the system shows real caution toward change, with new approaches needing a proven track record before adoption.

But "job security" in Portugal is unevenly distributed in ways worth knowing before you sign anything. Eurofound data shows roughly 62% of Portuguese workers felt their jobs were secure as of the mid-2000s, though the share feeling strongly secure had already dropped from 40% to under 26% over the prior decade — and the gap between contract types is stark: 63.7% of full-time workers reported feeling secure versus just 41.6% of part-time workers, with women disproportionately holding temporary contracts relative to men. More recent IZA World of Labor research shows the pattern has, if anything, deepened: 53.3% of workers aged 15-24 and 24.3% of those 25-34 held fixed-term contracts in 2024 — among the highest rates in the EU-27 — with that instability stretching well into the mid-30s and measurably delaying young Portuguese adults from moving out of their parents' homes. The permanent contract isn't a cultural preference so much as a genuinely scarce, unevenly allocated asset.

### Latvia: A Good Place to Pass Through

Latvia's labor market tells almost the opposite story: less about guarding a scarce permanent position, more about using a calm, affordable window efficiently before moving on. One relocation-focused account aimed at expats and remote workers described the core pitch bluntly, framing Latvia explicitly as "a stepping stone into larger international careers" rather than a long-term career hub — not a knock on the country, but an honest description of how it functions for a lot of the people who work there.

The lifestyle case behind that framing is concrete. The same account describes shared apartments running roughly €250-450 monthly and coffee at €2-4, a cost structure that supports real saving even on modest pay, alongside a workplace pace that "often feels calmer" than the "extremely fast paced and stressful" cultures some arrivals are leaving behind. Gallup's workplace data adds useful nuance rather than a simple endorsement: Latvia's 20% employee engagement rate beats both the global and European averages, and 50% of workers report thriving in life overall — ahead of regional benchmarks — but daily stress (37%, up 7 points) and anger (19%, up 4 points) have both risen meaningfully in the most recent data, suggesting "calmer" describes the relative baseline, not an absence of pressure. On the market-confidence side, 59% of Latvian workers believe it's currently a good time to find local work, ahead of both regional and global averages — a genuinely favorable mobility environment for anyone treating a Latvian job as one stop on a longer route.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't loyalty versus ambition — it's what each labor market is actually built to protect. Portugal's system is built to protect the position: hierarchy, permanence, and role structure are the point, even as that protection is unevenly distributed by contract type and age, leaving a large share of young workers in exactly the precarious position the system was designed to prevent. Latvia's system isn't really built to protect positions at all; it's built to be a decent, low-friction place to work for a while, which makes "staying long-term" a personal choice rather than a culturally rewarded default — and the data suggests many skilled workers genuinely treat it that way.

The irony: Portugal's reputation for a relaxed, unhurried lifestyle coexists with one of the EU's more precarious youth labor markets, while Latvia's reputation as a brisk, efficiency-minded post-Soviet economy coexists with a workplace multiple accounts describe as unusually calm. Lifestyle reputation and labor-market reality point in opposite directions in both places.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *JobsQD (relocation/lifestyle blog, Latvia) — A guide aimed at prospective movers described Latvia frankly as "a stepping stone into larger international careers," framing short-to-medium tenure not as a failure to commit but as the realistic, common way people actually use the market — useful advice for anyone expecting to be asked, pointedly, about their five-year plan.*

> *JobsQD (relocation/lifestyle blog, Latvia) — The same account offered a concrete practical reassurance: shared housing running €250-450 a month and coffee at €2-4 make saving genuinely achievable even on modest local pay, a detail that changes the math on how "worth it" a shorter stint there actually is.*

> *IZA World of Labor (academic research, Portugal) — Researchers flagged a structural frustration with real downstream effects: fixed-term contracts affecting over half of workers aged 15-24 and nearly a quarter of those 25-34 have measurably delayed young Portuguese adults from moving out of their parents' homes — job instability with consequences well beyond the workplace itself.*

> *Eurofound (EU research body, Portugal) — Data on job security sentiment surfaced a quieter structural gap: full-time workers felt secure at nearly 64%, while part-time workers felt secure at just under 42% — a reminder that "Portuguese job security" is really several very different experiences depending on your contract type, not one uniform cultural guarantee.*

### Conclusion

If you're chasing something closer to the traditional, slow-build career — a long tenure, clear hierarchy, earned trust over years — Portugal rewards that instinct more than most of Western Europe, provided you can land a permanent contract in the first place; if you can't, be honest with yourself about how long you're willing to wait inside that uncertainty. If you'd rather treat a few years abroad as a genuinely good, low-stress chapter rather than a forever-home, Latvia is set up for exactly that, calmly and affordably, with no cultural penalty for eventually moving on. Pick the country that matches the career you actually want, not the one that sounds more impressive at a dinner party.
