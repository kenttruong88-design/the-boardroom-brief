# Greece Dines at Ten, Canada Tips at Twenty, and Both Consider the Other Slightly Alarming

🇬🇷 Greece · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Restaurants in Greece that locals favour tend to look empty at seven in the evening and fill after half past nine. In Canada, a restaurant at that hour is more likely to be stacking chairs. Between these two schedules sits a larger disagreement about what a meal is for: in one country it is an event with an unspecified end time, in the other it is a transaction with a suggested gratuity of twenty percent, displayed on a screen held at arm's length.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat dinner after 9 p.m. and book ahead in July and August, when island tavernas fill after 9:30 | Arrive at 7 p.m. and expect an atmosphere; the staff will be pleased to see you, and nobody else will |
| Order meze-style small plates and share them, letting the meal arrive in stages | Order one main course each and expect them to land simultaneously |
| Ask for the bill when you are ready; you will not be rushed or handed one | Wait for the bill to arrive on its own and conclude you have been forgotten |
| Accept what a host offers, even if only a small amount | Decline food outright; locals regard it as rude |
| Shop at the neighbourhood laiki agora for seasonal produce and fish, with small euro notes in hand | Assume the supermarket is the cheaper or better option for vegetables |
| Take a light breakfast of coffee and a koulouri or a cheese pie | Look for a full cooked breakfast at the corner bakery |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for a restaurant tip of roughly 15 to 20 percent on sit-down service | Treat the tip screen's higher suggestions as mandatory; there is usually a custom or no-tip option |
| Eat dinner early; most Canadian kitchens will not be expecting you at 10 p.m. | Assume a Greek-style late table is on offer everywhere |
| Compare prices across Walmart, Metro and discount chains, and use flyer apps | Do the weekly shop at one store out of habit |
| Expect tax to be added at the till, so the shelf price is not the price | Do mental arithmetic from the shelf label and be pleased with the result |
| In Alberta, plan a separate stop for alcohol | Assume the grocery store sells wine, as it may in British Columbia |
| Cook at home when you can; it is where Canadians eat most of their food | Treat restaurant eating as the cheap option |

## Greece

According to Eurostat figures for 2025, reported by the Greek outlet Dnews in September 2026, 12.8 percent of Greek adults were obese, against an EU average of 16.3 percent. Only Italy, at 7 percent, did better. This is the sort of statistic Greece is pleased to receive, and it is consistent with what resident expats describe: a country of fish and seasonal vegetables bought at the weekly laiki agora, where one long-term expat on Expat.com praises the market produce and the salad that precedes the main course.

The statistic omits the children. The World Health Organization's Europe office describes "an alarming one third" of Greek children as overweight or obese and records a national action plan, running 2022 to 2026, with a target of reducing childhood obesity to 24.5 percent by 2030. The Mediterranean diet, it appears, is better at being exported than eaten by the young.

Eating in Greece is also an exercise in pacing. A piece in Expat.com's magazine notes that bakeries sit on nearly every corner, with bread and pastries fresh by six in the morning, and that the neighbourhood laiki agora runs once or twice a week with prices that fall between eleven and one, though quality may fall with them. Coffee is its own institution: the same piece reports that a single coffee meeting can last two to four hours and often turns into food and alcohol. A separate expat guide to local culture notes that Greeks spend hours in cafés mainly for the company, and that turning down food is considered rude. The prospective resident should regard the coffee as an appointment and the appointment as open-ended.

The schedule is the part that requires adjustment. To Vima, an English-language Greek publication, reports that most locals arrive at restaurants between 9:30 and 10 p.m., partly because summer evenings can remain well above 36 degrees Celsius and partly because the working day itself runs late. A meal may last two or three hours, the dishes arriving gradually. Breakfast, by several Expat.com accounts, barely exists: coffee, perhaps a sesame bread ring, because the previous night's dinner has not entirely finished. Vegetarians do well, by the same forum's accounts, though vegans are advised to bring patience and a phrasebook.

## Canada

Canadians eat at home. Statistics Canada's Health Reports find that 70.1 percent of daily energy is consumed at home and only 9.3 percent in restaurants. They also find that ultra-processed products supplied 45.7 percent of Canadians' calories in 2015, the latest year the agency analysed, and 59.6 percent of the energy eaten in restaurants. Dinner at home had the best profile, at 30.6 percent. The cooking, in other words, remains the healthier option, which is convenient given the prices. Institutions, meaning schools and workplaces, account for another 11.4 percent of energy, and their ultra-processed share, at 45.7 percent, sits above the home figure of 42.9 percent. The Canadian food day, on this evidence, is a domestic affair that is occasionally interrupted by a cafeteria.

TD Economics reported in January 2026 that Canadian grocery prices are over 30 percent higher than in 2019, against roughly 17 percent had pre-pandemic trends held, and that the average household spends more than C$1,600 more on groceries than before. Groceries make up 11 percent of the Canadian price basket, against 8 percent in the United States, and the lowest-income fifth of households spends 14 percent of its budget on food, against 10 percent for the highest. Grocery inflation reached 4.4 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025, roughly twice the headline rate, while consumers perceived inflation at about 4 percent against a reported 2.3. TD also notes that a 25 percent retaliatory tariff on American goods applied from March to August 2025, in a country that sourced about half of its fresh fruit and vegetable imports from the United States. The federal government's response was to rename the GST credit the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit, which is one way to acknowledge a problem. A British newcomer who posted a basket comparison on Reddit found two limes and a head of broccoli at C$5.97 at Metro, C$4.51 at Walmart and the equivalent of C$2.11 at Tesco. A UK-to-Calgary writer for Narcity makes the same complaint more politely, adding that tax appears only at the till.

Then there is the tip. The Globe and Mail reports an average restaurant tip of 17.2 percent among its readers, while a Canadian Press piece in MoneySense quotes a hospitality professor saying norms have drifted from about 15 percent before the pandemic towards 20. An H&R Block survey of 1,545 Canadians found that 93 percent were annoyed by card machines requesting tips where none is customary. CTV News, reporting in January 2026, describes the spread of tip prompts to fast-food counters and self-checkout kiosks, and a Toronto Metropolitan University professor calls rising percentages part of a pattern known as tip creep. Canadians are annoyed. They tip anyway.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Greece offers cheaper fresh produce, a slower table and an adult obesity rate comfortably below the European norm, while Canada offers earlier dinners, healthier home cooking than its restaurants manage and a grocery bill that has become a topic of national conversation. Greece's weakness is its children and its schedule. Canada's is its price level and its terminals. Neither country has a food culture that is unambiguously better; they have food cultures that punish different habits. The Greek newcomer who eats at seven will dine alone and be politely served. The Canadian newcomer who shops by habit will pay a premium and be told, by every acquaintance, that it is the same everywhere. For a household weighing a move, the relevant sums are the weekly grocery bill and the tolerance for a dinner that starts when the children are in bed.

Hofstede's framework, as summarised in an open-access textbook, places Greece among the highest-scoring countries for uncertainty avoidance and Canada among the more individualist ones. The dinner table agrees. Greeks cannot quite be told when the evening will end, and Canadians, who would like to be told, prefer to split the cheque.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a long-term expat in Greece says the restaurant details take months to absorb: wine arrives in small tumblers, bread arrives in quantities nobody ordered, the salad comes first and the main contains remarkably few vegetables, and the fruit or yogurt at the end is simply provided.*

> *Expat.com — several posters in a breakfast thread agree that Greeks do not really eat breakfast. Coffee is the core, a sesame koulouri or a cheese pie is the ceiling, and one explains that late workdays and late dinners leave little appetite in the morning.*

> *Expat.com — vegetarian visitors in a separate thread report being well fed. Falafel souvlaki and spinach pie cover the day trips, restaurants in Athens usually offer a vegetarian main, and one long-time visitor says it is far easier than it was in the 1990s. Vegans, they concede, are on their own.*

> *Reddit — a British newcomer in Canada priced limes and broccoli at three different stores, found Metro roughly three times the cost of Tesco, and asked in print whether Canadians are simply being taken for a ride.*

> *Narcity — a newcomer in Calgary reports that cashiers pack the bags faster than she can, that eggs are refrigerated, that the chicken costs about C$14 at Walmart against roughly the equivalent of C$8 at home, and that alcohol in Alberta requires a separate trip.*

## Conclusion

If you are relocating to Greece, adjust your clock before you adjust your diet. Plan on dinner at nine or later, expect lunch somewhere between two and four, learn the market days and accept what is offered. If you are relocating to Canada, adjust your wallet: expect tax at the till, shop around, budget for a tip near 20 percent and cook at home, which the data suggest is the better option for both health and budget.

For those with children, note that Greece's own national plan treats childhood obesity as a crisis worth a five-year programme, and that the taverna is not the whole of the diet. For those without, the choice is between a country that will not tell you when the meal is over and one that will tell you exactly what you owe. Both have, in their way, an excellent bill of fare.
