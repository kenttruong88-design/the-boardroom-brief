# Nigeria Offers a Benevolent Autocrat, Ghana an Elder You Must Not Contradict: Choose Your Superior Carefully

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇬🇭 Ghana
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two West African neighbours, a short flight apart, share a language of business, a colonial filing system and an abiding suspicion of the open-plan office. Yet according to Gallup, only 17 percent of Nigerian employees count as engaged at work, while in Ghana the figure is 8 percent, against a global average of 20. Both populations are, in other words, working under bosses whom they admire, fear or avoid, and the exact mix differs by border. For anyone weighing a posting in Lagos or Accra, the org chart is the least informative document in the building.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal titles for senior colleagues until told otherwise | Skip the greeting round and open with a task |
| Raise disagreements privately, after the meeting | Correct your boss in front of the team |
| Ask for clearly defined roles and reporting lines in writing | Assume authority is shared because the office has beanbags |
| Invest in relationships before expecting decisions | Expect a deal or approval in a single meeting |
| Use your right hand for greetings and exchanges | Treat seniority as a technicality |
| Offer ideas as suggestions the boss can adopt as his own | Present ideas as improvements to his decisions |

### Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait to be invited before using first names | Address an elder colleague by first name on day one |
| Use academic and professional titles | Drop a doctorate from someone's introduction |
| Soften refusals and give a reason that saves face | Say a flat no in front of others |
| Allow a first meeting to be about whether you are pleasant company | Open with the agenda and a deadline |
| Accept invitations from colleagues and business associates | Treat a lunch invitation as optional |
| Route serious criticism through a trusted intermediary | Voice grievances about a superior in open forum |

## Nigeria

Nigeria's management culture is hierarchical in the way that a pyramid is geometrical. According to Expat Arrivals, management traditionally follows a clear chain of command, bosses are usually older men who expect respect, and they are rarely criticised in public. Hofstede Insights, as reported by BusinessDay in 2021, gives Nigeria a power distance score of 80 out of 100 and describes bosses as benevolent autocrats. The benevolence is the part new arrivals tend to look for first.

The same arrangement is visible in the commentary of Nigerians themselves. BusinessDay reported in 2014 that rank-based divides between bosses and staff are clearly visible in the corporate sector, with poor communication between them, and that while global firms flatten their structures, many Nigerian organisations still follow civil-service-style ones. A contributor to How we made it in Africa, writing as the head of an incentives firm, advised that roles be clearly defined because the society is strongly hierarchical, and added that disputes are settled by fighting them out. That author sells incentive schemes, so some salt is advisable, but the broad picture matches the other sources.

None of this is uniform. Expat Arrivals notes that start-ups and many multinationals are moving towards collaborative styles that value employee input, and Gallup's data suggest the arrangement costs something: 55 percent of Nigerian employees reported a lot of stress the previous day, against 40 percent globally. The practical lesson is to establish early which kind of employer you have joined. The job title will not tell you. The first time the boss is disagreed with will.

The boss-employee relationship is also personal before it is contractual. Expat Arrivals advises that trust comes before formal negotiation, that decisions often take longer than expected, and that several rounds of talks may precede an agreement. Local firms may be favoured in deals, and personal connections to influential people can help, though the guide adds that transparent, ethical approaches are increasingly encouraged. For a new hire this translates into a simple rule: the relationship with your superior is the project, and the deliverables are what you do while it is being built. Public criticism of colleagues is to be avoided in favour of raising matters privately, which is advice that Ghana would endorse with equal firmness and less volume.

## Ghana

Ghana's hierarchy is quieter and, arguably, older. According to Expat Arrivals, respect in Ghanaian business culture derives from age, experience, wealth and position, titles carry weight, and expatriates should wait to be invited before using first names. Communication is often indirect, and meetings are conducted so as to avoid embarrassing anyone. Jobberman Ghana observed in March 2026 that colleagues who praise the boss in meetings are usually expressing a cultural reverence for authority rather than currying favour, which is generous of Jobberman and will still look like currying favour to the unprepared.

The cost of this reverence appears in the local press. In September 2025, Benjamin Quarcoo wrote in Angel Online that Ghanaians learn not to challenge elders openly, that most leaders are older than their critics, and that whistleblower channels exist more on paper than in practice. In August 2026, Henry Atta Nyame wrote in Graphic Online of employees living with a constant worry about dismissal, in a market where jobs are scarce and sacking carries a social stigma. He is candid that his column rests on experience rather than survey data. Gallup's numbers at least agree with the mood: 8 percent of Ghanaian employees are engaged, 14 percent are actively disengaged, and 55 percent reported a lot of stress.

For the arriving manager, the implication is that silence in a Ghanaian meeting is not consent. It is a courtesy, and often the only one on offer. Disagreement tends to travel by other routes, which is useful to know before wondering why a plan approved on Monday has quietly stopped on Wednesday.

The local sense of humour about all this is instructive. A 2012 opinion column on MyJoyOnline, written in the satirical form of ten commandments for the Ghanaian worker, complained that staff resent managers who insist on productivity, efficiency and discipline, and prefer lenient ones, while colleagues shield one another from reporting. It is satire, and it is fourteen years old, so it proves nothing about today's offices. It does show that Ghanaians have long noticed the gap between the formal hierarchy and the informal one, and that the second is the one that decides whether your Tuesday goes well. Expat Arrivals adds that the working environment blends formality with traditional Ghanaian culture, which in practice means a handshake, a title and a long pause.

## The Reckoning

On paper the two countries are close. Both are hierarchical, both prize titles, and both expect the junior to keep disagreements out of the room. The difference is in how the hierarchy expresses itself. Nigerian authority, in the sources surveyed here, is directive and visible: the boss decides, roles are defined, and conflict is argued out, sometimes loudly. Ghanaian authority leans on deference and indirection: the boss is an elder, objections are routed around him, and the loser in a dispute is not told in public. A Nigerian workplace may bruise the ego, and a Ghanaian one may leave it politely unaddressed.

For the foreign hire the trade-off is practical. In Nigeria, the risk is a boss who treats feedback as insubordination and a team that has learned not to offer it. In Ghana, the risk is a boss who is never told what is wrong, because telling him would be disrespectful. Gallup's engagement figures, 17 and 8 percent respectively, suggest that neither system has found the dial that makes people care. The Nigerian version at least tends to announce its flaws in advance. The Ghanaian version is more like a pleasant house with one locked room, and the locked room is where the decisions are made.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter born in Ghana explained that refusing a request there should come with a face-saving reason, that criticism is rarely delivered to your face and instead travels through other people, and that blunt Western refusals tend to offend and set off a cycle of escalating rudeness.*

> *Zikoko — a Nigerian professional recalled a former CEO whose moods set the company rules, with dress codes changing unpredictably and staff dismissed by having their email deleted and their Slack access removed, while he presented himself as a youthful mentor. At the new employer, the hierarchy is formal but people feel equal.*

> *Zikoko — a Nigerian hired as a programs assistant ended up doing a team lead's job, was belittled and silenced in meetings by superiors, and was expected to fetch the boss's coffee and food. Rebuilding their confidence at a better job took time.*

> *Medium (The Flint) — a Ghanaian tech worker who visited Lagos startups in 2017 described CEOs who dropped by one another's offices to swap ideas, a founder who shared an internal strategy document with peers for feedback, and a general readiness to ask for help. He returned to Accra inspired and, by his own account, a little envious.*

> *How we made it in Africa — an expatriate start-up coach interviewed in 2014 said Lagos has no separation between work and pleasure, that socialising counts as work because any new contact could become a partner, and that the Nigeria-Ghana rivalry is mostly friendly and the dire stories he heard about Nigeria while in Ghana were overstated.*

## Conclusion

If you are the type who thrives on clear orders and a visible chain of command, Nigeria will suit you, provided you pick an employer on the collaborative end of the spectrum or are content to be directed. If you prefer to be managed with courtesy and a minimum of confrontation, Ghana offers that, at the price of having to read the room, the pause and the intermediary. In both countries, ask in the interview who the last person was to disagree with the boss, and what happened next.

Either way, learn the titles, hold your objections until the corridor, and arrive with a relationship before you arrive with a request. The org chart is merely a rumour about who is in charge, and in both countries the rumour is usually accurate.
