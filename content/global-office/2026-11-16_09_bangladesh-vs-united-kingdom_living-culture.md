# Dhaka on Four Hundred Dollars, London on Nothing Left: A Cost-of-Living Reality Check

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Livingcost puts the average monthly cost of living for one person in Bangladesh at $429 and in the United Kingdom at $2,399, a gap of about 82 percent. The same site puts average take-home pay at $236 a month in Bangladesh and $3,628 in Britain. The arithmetic is therefore more flattering to the British than the prices suggest, and considerably less flattering to everyone's expectations of what a pound will do once it has met a nursery.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for expat prices, not local ones; expat posters report spending far above calculator estimates | Trust a cost-of-living calculator that was fed local prices |
| Pay for healthcare cash in hand and keep an emergency fund | Assume the state will cover a hospital stay |
| Negotiate rent through a local contact | Accept the first quote offered to a foreigner |
| Plan for backup power and traffic when choosing a neighbourhood | Pick a flat on rent alone and discover the commute later |
| Look at Chittagong if your work allows it | Assume Dhaka is the only sensible address |
| Treat family networks as the real welfare system | Expect a pension, allowance or childcare place to appear on request |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check funded childcare hours and Tax-Free Childcare before choosing a nursery | Assume the sticker price is what a family with eligible hours pays |
| Budget for the Immigration Health Surcharge, then use the NHS freely | Treat the NHS as a thing you pay for twice |
| Keep deposit and first-month rent liquid before you arrive | Land with savings that cover rent but not rent plus everything else |
| Read the habitual residence and waiting-period rules before counting on benefits | Plan your first quarter around a benefit you cannot yet claim |
| Compare regions, because rent varies enormously | Assume London is the only place with jobs, or the only place with prices |
| Claim Child Benefit if you are eligible | Leave eligible money unclaimed out of politeness |

## Bangladesh

The headline is that Bangladesh is cheap. Livingcost estimates rent for one person at $132 a month against $1,485 in Britain, and food at $193 against $570. GDP per capita is given as $2,593 against $53,246, which explains the cheapness better than any virtue of the local economy does. The same source rates Bangladesh at 38 for quality of life against 92 for the UK, and puts life expectancy at 74.7 years against 81.2.

The expat experience is less thrifty than the national average. One Australian poster on Expat.com, about three years in Dhaka, reported that an online calculator suggested $12,000 to $15,000 a year while his real spending was nearer $30,000, a figure he said matched the other expats he knew. He put a decent three-bedroom flat in Gulshan or Baridhara at $1,200 a month or more, and warned that cars, alcohol and private schooling cost much more than the calculators imply. These figures are about a decade old, so treat them as a ratio rather than a quote, but the pattern is durable: the cheap Bangladesh is the one lived by Bangladeshis, and the expatriate version carries a premium.

The welfare picture is thin by British standards, and the World Bank says so politely. In its 2019 feature, it noted that social spending was about 2.5 percent of GDP and that the Old Age Allowance paid BDT 500, about $6, a month to more than four million people. A 2021 World Bank review, reported by The Daily Star, found that between 49 and 66 percent of allowance and food-support beneficiaries were not poor, and that young children were roughly one in eight of the poor but received 1.6 percent of social protection spending. Health insurance barely exists: the Daily Star reports that households pay about 68 percent of health spending out of pocket and that insurance makes up around 1 percent of health financing. Childcare is largely a family matter, which is efficient until the family lives on another continent.

The state is not absent so much as modest. The Daily Star reports that the FY2023-24 social protection allocation was 2.52 percent of GDP, below the government's own target of 3 percent, and that only about 41 percent of it is net safety-net money for vulnerable citizens, the rest being pensions, interest and subsidies. The same reporting says the state spends $54 per person on health against the $88 recommended by the WHO. For a newcomer, the sensible reading is that public provision is a floor you will not want to test. Private arrangements, domestic help and extended family do the work that British departments do, and Bangladesh's low prices are what make that arrangement affordable to the middle class.

## United Kingdom

Britain is where the money arrives and then leaves in the direction of landlords. The Office for National Statistics put average UK private rent at £1,406 a month in September 2026, up 3.8 percent in a year, and £2,338 in London. Livingcost's one-person estimate of $2,399 a month is therefore not an exaggeration so much as a modest introduction.

Childcare is the second act. The OECD found, in its Society at a Glance report, that British families spent more than 30 percent of income on childcare, nearly three times the OECD average of about 13 percent. That figure is a decade old and the policy has since moved. Coram's 2026 survey says funded hours for eligible working parents have reached 30 a week, and that full-time costs for under-threes fell 39 percent on the previous year as a result. But the same survey notes that costs kept rising for families who are not eligible, and that prices in Scotland and Wales rose 5 and 8 percent. Eligibility, in other words, is now the product being sold.

The long-run trend explains the national mood. Coram records that a part-time place for a child under two cost £55 a week in 2001 and £160 a week by spring 2024, nearly triple, and Moorepay's 2026 affordability report shows childcare taking 37.7 percent of disposable income in Nottingham against 10.3 percent in Kensington and Chelsea. In Britain, the cost of raising a child is partly a postcode lottery.

The welfare and insurance side is the part of Britain that works the way the brochure says. Migrants pay a health surcharge of £1,035 a year per adult and £776 per child, according to GOV.UK, after which NHS access is included in the price. Child Benefit is £27.05 a week for the eldest child and £17.90 for each additional one. It is not generous, but it is on time, which in the comparison with Bangladesh counts as an achievement.

## The Reckoning

On the numbers, Bangladesh wins the argument about prices and loses it about everything the prices are meant to buy. Take-home pay of $236 against a cost of living of $429 leaves a household with a gap that family, remittances or a second income must fill. Britain's $3,628 against $2,399 leaves a surplus, until a child turns up and the nursery invoice arrives. The country that spends 2.5 percent of GDP on social protection and the country that charges £1,035 for admission to a health service are offering two different kinds of safety net: one is relatives, the other is paperwork.

Hofstede Insights scores explain some of the temperament. Bangladesh scores 80 on power distance and 20 on individualism, a society that arranges care through hierarchy and kin. The UK scores 35 and 89, which is a society that arranges it through contracts, benefits and queues. For a migrant, the practical consequence is that Bangladesh asks you to rely on people and the UK asks you to rely on forms, and each system punishes the other's habits. A Bangladeshi arriving in Britain will find that asking an uncle is not a recognised application route.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — an Australian in Dhaka found that calculators told him to expect about half what he spent, and said every expat he knew had the same experience. Rent in the expat suburbs, he added, buys much less than the headline suggests, and traffic adds to the bill in minutes.*

> *Mumsnet — in a 2022 thread about nursery fees, parents described rises of £120 to £200 a month, one family paying more than £3,000 a month for two children, and one mother who worked out that three days at nursery came to roughly £10 an hour. Several said childcare pushes mothers out of work.*

> *British Expats — a British citizen who had lived in Bangladesh for over 30 years planned to move to the UK with her two daughters on £4,000 in savings. Replies were blunt that rent alone could exceed that, that benefits have waiting periods and a residence test, and that she should stay with relatives as long as possible.*

> *Blind — a product manager weighing a London offer at roughly half his New York pay was told that rent is the main shock. One commenter said about £110,000 would be needed to match his current standard of living, another said £100,000 was comfortable, and a third had moved back to the US for a raise and found his costs 50 percent higher.*

## Conclusion

If the plan is to move from Bangladesh to Britain, the correct number is not the monthly cost of living. It is the cost of the first three months, plus rent, plus the health surcharge, plus whatever childcare costs before any entitlement begins. The forum voices agree on the same thing from different directions: savings thinner than they look, rules that start later than expected, and rent that arrives first.

Go with a deposit, a job offer if possible, and an exact understanding of which benefits you cannot yet claim. Bangladesh will keep the family. Britain will keep the receipts.
