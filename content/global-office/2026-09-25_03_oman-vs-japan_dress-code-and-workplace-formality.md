**Oman vs. Japan: Two Countries Where Nobody Will Tell You You're Dressed Wrong**

**🇴🇲 Oman · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nobody in Muscat or Tokyo is going to pull you aside and explain the dress code. In Oman, the national dishdasha functions as a kind of silent professional uniform for Omani men in government and much of the private sector, leaving expatriate hires to work out, largely by observation, how formal "business casual" is expected to be in a country where formality and modesty are civic virtues, not just office policy. In Japan, the suit is closer to armor than fashion — a visible signal of seriousness so entrenched that "Cool Biz," the government-endorsed summer dress-down initiative meant to save on air conditioning, still gets treated by many salarymen as an invitation they'd rather not accept. Both countries will judge you by your clothes before you've said a word. Neither will tell you so directly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇴🇲 Oman

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to conservative, modest business attire — covered shoulders and knees for women, full-length trousers and collared shirts for men, at minimum | Wear sleeveless tops, shorts, or anything above the knee to the office, even in Oman's considerable heat |
| Invest in at least one lightweight formal suit for client-facing or government-adjacent meetings, where expectations skew more formal than casual startups | Assume Gulf heat is an excuse for informal dress; air-conditioned offices maintain business-formal standards year-round |
| Notice what senior Omani colleagues wear and calibrate to it — dress conservatism often tracks seniority and client visibility | Wear the national dishdasha or abaya as a foreigner unless specifically invited to for a cultural occasion; it can read as appropriation rather than respect |
| Keep a modest layer on hand for unplanned site visits, government offices, or mosque-adjacent meetings | Assume Western business-casual (open collar, rolled sleeves) reads the same way in Oman as it does at home — it often reads as underdressed |
| Ask HR or a local colleague directly about dress expectations in your first week — it is a normal, welcomed question | Guess based on what you see tourists wearing; tourist norms and office norms in Oman are entirely different standards |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark, conservative suit to any first meeting or interview, regardless of your industry's stated culture | Assume "business casual" on a job posting means the same thing it does in the US or UK — Japanese business casual still often means a full suit |
| Participate in Cool Biz (no jacket, no tie) only once you've confirmed your specific office actually practices it — adoption varies widely by company and seniority | Remove your jacket or tie in a client meeting just because it's officially "Cool Biz" season; many salarymen keep both on regardless |
| Keep shoes polished and plain — small details of formality are noticed more than they would be in many Western offices | Wear visibly casual sneakers, even fashionable ones, to a client-facing role outside of a small number of startup or creative-industry exceptions |
| Ask what women in your specific office wear regarding pants versus skirts before assuming either is the default — this varies by company and is still an active point of quiet debate | Assume a single national "Japanese dress code" exists uniformly; conventions vary meaningfully between traditional corporates and international or startup firms |
| Err formal for the first several weeks, then adjust down only after observing your specific team | Interpret a relaxed office as license to dress like it's the weekend; formality tends to ratchet down slowly, not suddenly |

**Oman**

Oman's approach to dress code sits at the intersection of national identity and workplace hierarchy in a way that has no exact Western analogue. A "real post report" account published by Tales Mag, describing daily life for a foreign resident in Muscat, notes that formal dress is expected in office and government settings well beyond what visiting tourists assume from Oman's reputation as a relatively liberal Gulf state — modesty norms extend into professional dress in ways that ClickUp's business etiquette guide and REAB's overview of Omani business traditions and mentality both frame as a matter of respect rather than mere formality. Expat.com's Muscat forum threads on the subject show a recurring pattern among newly arrived professionals: genuine uncertainty about where the line sits, since the national dishdasha worn by many Omani men in the workplace isn't something an expatriate is expected — or generally welcome — to adopt themselves, leaving a somewhat undefined "respectful but foreign" register to find.

What compensates for the ambiguity is that Omani colleagues are, by multiple accounts, unusually willing to answer a direct question about it. Unlike some cultures where dress expectations are absorbed through unspoken observation alone, several Expat.com threads describe HR departments and colleagues proactively clarifying standards when asked — a rare point of explicit guidance in an otherwise implicit system.

**Japan**

Japan's relationship to business dress is, per JoynTokyo's guide for foreign professionals and PLAZA HOMES' overview of Japanese dress codes, less about individual expression and more about visible signaling of seriousness, group belonging, and respect for hierarchy — the dark suit functions almost as a professional uniform rather than a personal style choice. Venture Japan's business etiquette guide notes that even in companies that have formally adopted Cool Biz — the government-backed initiative encouraging jacket- and tie-free summer dressing to reduce air conditioning costs — actual adoption varies enormously by company culture and individual seniority, with many senior employees continuing to dress formally regardless of official policy, a gap between stated rule and lived norm that several Quora threads on the subject describe as genuinely confusing for newcomers trying to calibrate.

That confusion deepens around gendered dress specifically. Quora threads asking whether women can wear pants instead of skirts in Japanese offices draw answers that vary company to company rather than converging on a single national standard, reflecting a workplace culture still working out, office by office, how far traditionally rigid dress conventions have actually shifted versus merely being officially revised on paper.

**The Reckoning**

Oman and Japan arrive at strict, unspoken dress expectations from almost opposite cultural logics. Oman's formality is rooted in modesty and national identity — clothing as a marker of respect for a specific cultural and religious context that a foreign employee is expected to honor without imitating. Japan's formality is rooted in hierarchy and group cohesion — clothing as a marker of seriousness and belonging within a specific organizational culture, one where even an officially sanctioned relaxation (Cool Biz) struggles against decades of unwritten seniority-linked convention.

The practical convergence is this: in both countries, the safest first move is to overdress relative to your instinct, then calibrate downward only once you've watched what people who are actually senior in your specific office do — not what a generic country guide, this one included, tells you the rule technically is. The written policy and the lived norm are not the same document in either country, and only your actual colleagues can tell you which one is currently in force.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking whether women could wear pants instead of skirts in a Japanese office received answers converging on "it depends entirely on your specific company," with several respondents noting that even asking the question outright in their own office produced hesitant, non-committal answers from HR rather than a clear policy.*

> *Quora — A thread on common mistakes foreigners make in Japanese business meetings included a specific mention of removing a suit jacket during a warm summer meeting under the assumption that Cool Biz applied, only to notice senior colleagues in the room had kept theirs on the entire time.*

> *Expat.com (Muscat forum) — A poster's "Dress Code!" thread asking what was actually expected in a Muscat office drew replies recommending long trousers and collared shirts as a safe minimum, with one respondent noting that overdressing in the first weeks earned quiet approval while a colleague who initially wore shorts to a site visit was gently but firmly corrected.*

> *Tales Mag (Muscat real-post report) — A foreign resident's account of daily life in Muscat described formal dress as expected well beyond what casual observation of tourist areas would suggest, noting that the contrast between beach-adjacent leisure dress and office dress in the same city surprised them more than any other single adjustment.*

### Conclusion

If you are moving to either country for work, the dress code question resolves the same way in both places: ask early, dress conservatively until you have an answer, and watch your most senior colleague more closely than any packing guide. Oman will reward modesty and respect for a dress code rooted in identity you are not expected to replicate; Japan will reward formality and quiet observation of a dress code that is officially relaxing faster than it is actually relaxing. Neither country will embarrass you for asking what to wear — they will only quietly notice if you didn't bother to.
