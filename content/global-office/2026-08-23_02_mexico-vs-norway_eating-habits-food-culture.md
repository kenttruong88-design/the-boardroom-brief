# The Two-Hour Lunch and the Sad Little Sandwich: A Field Guide to Eating on the Clock in Mexico and Norway

🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Mexico City, a business lunch can occupy two full hours of the working day, features multiple courses, and is considered a legitimate venue for closing a deal. In Oslo, lunch lasts roughly thirty minutes, consists of open-faced bread you assembled yourself that morning, and is eaten in near silence because everyone is, in fact, still working. Both countries call this "lunch." Only one of them means it as a compliment to the meal.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to *la comida* — declining without a specific reason reads as a social rebuff, not a scheduling choice | Don't book meetings between 2 and 4pm and expect full attendance; large parts of the office genuinely disappear |
| Learn basic food vocabulary in Spanish before week one — ordering, allergies, and restaurant small talk all run through it | Don't treat comida as a quick refuel; rushing a shared meal signals disinterest in the relationship, not efficiency |
| Seek out a busy fonda or comida corrida stall near the office — high turnover generally means fresher food | Don't assume street food is automatically risky; the informal sector is enormous, normal, and closely watched by regulars |
| Expect the biggest meal of the day at midday, not at night — dinner is often light or skipped entirely | Don't be the one who always insists on splitting the bill to the peso; hosting and reciprocating matters more than accounting |
| Let seniority guide who orders first and who ends up paying — hierarchy shows up at the table, not just on the org chart | Don't plan your afternoon assuming a one-hour lunch; plan around two |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a matpakke — a packed lunch of open-faced rye bread — even if the office has a subsidized hot canteen | Don't expect a hot, multi-course lunch to be the norm; the culture treats that as an occasional indulgence, not daily practice |
| Keep the lunch break to about 30 minutes and get back to your desk | Don't linger over lunch expecting it to double as a meeting; Norwegians generally compartmentalize the two |
| Budget seriously before eating out — a mid-range dinner easily runs 300–500 NOK per person | Don't expect small talk to stay in English once the topic turns to food; lunch-table chat often reverts to Norwegian |
| Join the felles lunsj (communal lunch) if your workplace has one — it is a genuine, if brief, bonding ritual | Don't be surprised colleagues eat near-identical brown bread lunches for years; novelty is not the point |
| Accept brown cheese, pickled fish, and cold cuts as completely normal breakfast and lunch fare | Don't over-tip at restaurants expecting warmer service in return; eating out is priced and treated as an occasion, not a habit |

## Mexico

Mexicans work more hours than any other OECD population — roughly 2,226 a year, against an OECD average considerably lower — and when unpaid domestic labour is folded in, the daily total climbs to around 10 hours, against an OECD average of 8, according to the OECD. A significant share of that unpaid time goes to cooking: Mexico does more housework than any other OECD country, at over three hours a day, and much of it is meal preparation. The math suggests a population that works long hours and then works some more to eat properly, which sounds punishing until you notice what the payoff is: a midday meal, *la comida*, eaten between roughly 2 and 4pm, that is unapologetically the centerpiece of the day rather than an interruption of it.

Comida corrida — the fixed-price, multi-course lunch served at fondas and family-run comedores — exists specifically to make that centerpiece affordable for office workers, factory employees, and students who cannot cook it themselves. A proper comida corrida runs through several *tiempos*: a brothy soup, a "dry soup" of rice or pasta, a main dish in sauce with tortillas, sometimes dessert. According to INEGI's household expenditure survey (ENIGH), a substantial share of food and beverage purchases in Mexico happen outside fully formal restaurants — split between informal street vendors and the vast "mixed" category of fondas and small eateries — which is less a food safety footnote than a description of how the entire white-collar workforce actually eats on a Tuesday.

What this does to the workday is structural, not incidental. Businesses routinely slow or close during the comida window; colectivos and buses fill with people heading home or to a fonda; and turning down a lunch invitation without an explanation reads less as a scheduling decision than as a small act of rejection. Mexico also scores 81 on Hofstede Insights' Power Distance Index, among the higher readings globally, and the hierarchy shows up at the table as much as in the org chart — seniority still tends to dictate who orders, who is served, and who quietly picks up the bill.

## Norway

Norway's food culture starts from the opposite premise: that lunch is a logistical problem to be solved quickly and cheaply, so the rest of the day can proceed. The matpakke — a packed lunch of open-faced sandwiches on dense wholegrain or rye bread, wrapped in wax paper — has been a fixture of Norwegian working life since a 1930s school-meal program normalized it, and it persists even in offices that offer subsidized hot lunches with an in-house chef. Lunch breaks generally run about 30 minutes, per multiple accounts of Norwegian office norms, which is roughly the time it takes to eat bread and cheese without ceremony and return to your desk.

That thrift is not really a choice; it is partly a response to price. Statistics Norway (SSB) data shows Norway has the second-highest household consumption level in Europe, and Norwegian grocery prices have been measured at roughly 186 percent of the EU average, among the highest food price levels on the continent — high enough that SSB has separately tracked Norwegian shoppers crossing into Sweden specifically to buy cheaper groceries. Eating out compounds the effect: a modest restaurant dinner runs 300–700 NOK a head. Against that backdrop, the daily matpakke looks less like austerity and more like arithmetic.

Norway's Hofstede Power Distance score sits at 31, one of the lowest in the world and a sharp contrast with Mexico's 81, and the flatness shows up at lunch too — there is no ritual of the boss ordering first or picking up the check, because the felles lunsj, where one exists, is genuinely communal rather than hierarchical. The trade-off is conversational: English may be the working language in the meeting room, but the lunch table tends to revert to Norwegian, leaving newly arrived foreign hires to eat their identical brown-bread lunch in something close to companionable silence.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real divergence is not what people eat but what eating is understood to be for. In Mexico, the midday meal is deliberately inefficient — a two-hour block that absorbs socializing, hierarchy, and a fair amount of actual business, on the logic that a relationship built over comida will outlast one built only in meetings. In Norway, the midday meal is deliberately efficient — a 30-minute administrative task performed at maximum equality and minimum fuss, on the logic that the real bonding, if it happens, will happen somewhere other than over a sandwich.

The counterintuitive part is which country actually spends more of its day on food. Mexicans, once unpaid cooking labour is counted, spend more total hours a day on food-related activity than Norwegians do, despite Norway's reputation for hygge-adjacent domesticity — the extra Mexican hours go into daily preparation and a long lunch, while Norway outsources both to speed (a pre-made sandwich) and to price (some of the highest grocery bills in Europe, which discourage restaurant habits more than they encourage home cooking marathons).

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — an engineer relocated to Monterrey for a multinational project described the adjustment as needing conversational Spanish almost immediately, not for meetings but for ordering food and navigating comida corrida menus that had no English translation and no interest in acquiring one.*

> *Quora — a longtime observer of Norwegian food habits noted that even well-paid professionals in offices with subsidized hot lunches keep bringing the same brown-bread matpakke day after day, calling it a point of quiet pride rather than a compromise.*

> *TripAdvisor Norway Forum — a visitor thread on grocery and restaurant costs concluded that sticker shock over Norwegian food prices takes months to wear off, and that some travelers have been known to fly home with suitcases partly repacked with food bought more cheaply elsewhere.*

> *The Local Norway — a foreign manager recounted realizing that although the office's official working language was English, the unwritten lunch-table language was Norwegian, which took longer to adjust to than any actual work task.*

> *expat.com magazine — an advice piece on global lunch norms warned newcomers to Mexico that declining a comida invitation without a specific, credible excuse can be read as a personal rebuff rather than a scheduling conflict, and recommended treating the first invitation as effectively mandatory.*

### Conclusion

Neither country's approach to lunch is more "efficient" in any way that would survive contact with the other's definition of the word. Mexico treats the midday meal as an investment in relationships and hierarchy that happens to also involve food; Norway treats it as a cost to be minimized so the rest of a flatter, faster day can proceed. A newcomer's job is not to decide which is correct but to recognize, in the first week, which clock they are now operating on — the one that expects you at the table for two hours, or the one that expects you back at your desk in thirty minutes with brown bread still on your breath. Get the clock wrong in either direction, and you will eat well and still manage to insult somebody.
