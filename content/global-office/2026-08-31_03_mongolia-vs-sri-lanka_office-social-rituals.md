[HEADLINE] One Country Will Make You Sing Karaoke With Your Boss. The Other Will Never Tell You What They Actually Think of the Quarterly Report.

🇲🇳 Mongolia · 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Ulaanbaatar, refusing a drink offered by a colleague is rude enough to warrant a line in expat guidebooks; in Colombo, telling a colleague "no" to their face is rude enough that most people simply won't, leaving newcomers to decode agreement, avoidance, and refusal from an identical, pleasant nod. Between them sits the entire discipline of office social ritual: one culture built on shared bowls and karaoke microphones, the other on shared meals and calibrated deference. Neither national statistics office publishes much on the subject — both track labour participation, not lunch etiquette — so what follows leans more than usual on people who found out the hard way.

#### 🇲🇳 Mongolia
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept food or drink when offered, even a token taste of *airag* (fermented mare's milk) | Refuse a work-dinner toast outright — a small sip is the accepted minimum |
| Shake hands with everyone in the room, on arrival and departure | Assume first names before you're explicitly invited to use them |
| Expect relationships, and sometimes decisions, to actually form over a meal | Skip the karaoke invitation — it's a genuine bonding ritual, not optional |
| Be patient when gatherings start late, especially at state-linked organisations | Mistake early reserve from colleagues for unfriendliness; trust builds slowly |
| Reciprocate hospitality by hosting in return | Expect the punctuality culture people describe to be evenly practiced |

#### 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone warmly; greet management slightly more formally in front of staff | Question or contradict your boss in front of colleagues |
| Treat a lunch or dinner invitation as a real step in the working relationship | Arrive hungry — socialising happens before the food, and lingering after is unusual |
| Read between the lines when a colleague says "yes" | Take a direct "no" at face value — you're unlikely to hear one |
| Dress slightly more conservatively than your line manager, at first | Assume a friendly meeting means a decision was reached; many are largely ceremonial |
| Let a trusted third party make introductions where possible | Skip praising the other side's expertise before getting to business |

### Mongolia

Mongolia's economy is still substantially a mining economy — the sector accounted for roughly 29% of GDP in 2023, per the World Bank, even as it employs a modest 6% of the country's 1.2 million-strong workforce, per ILO figures. Services have grown to nearly half of GDP, and with it an increasingly cosmopolitan Ulaanbaatar office culture that sits alongside older codes: hierarchy is respected, relationships outrank contracts, and a herding heritage of hospitality persists in glass-walled conference rooms. Expats consistently report that shared meals, toasts, and karaoke are where actual working trust gets built, not in the meeting itself. One long-term Fulbright teaching fellow's advice to newcomers was blunt: private karaoke rooms exist "on every corner," Mongolians expect you to perform "regardless of your skill level," and declining food or drink at a gathering is simply rude (ExpatsBlog interview).

What the data can't capture is how slowly that hospitality opens to outsiders. One American who spent five years in Ulaanbaatar described Mongolians as notably less immediately forthcoming on first meeting than the instant warmth Westerners tend to perform; instant embrace by a new friend group is not how integration actually starts (Macongolia blog). Hofstede Insights' figures capture some of this tension, if inconsistently: fieldwork estimates of Mongolia's power-distance score range from a strikingly low 18 to a strikingly high 93 depending on the study — less a data error than a fair reflection of a workplace genuinely caught between steppe egalitarianism and corporate hierarchy.

### Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka's labour force participation rate stood at 49.2%, with unemployment at 3.7%, per the Department of Census and Statistics' most recent quarterly survey, figures the Asian Development Bank tracks alongside a services-heavy economy centred on Colombo. That formality shows up in office social life: multiple business-etiquette guides describe workplaces as hierarchical by age and seniority, where disagreeing with a superior in front of staff is close to unthinkable. One Expat.com forum contributor who'd worked in Sri Lankan offices for decades put it plainly: never question your boss publicly, keep body language conservative, and open any negotiation by praising the other party's expertise, because "most meetings are for a show and achieve very little."

Off the clock, the register shifts. One long-term Colombo-based consultant described the city's after-work culture as a "chill hangout vibe" rather than a nightclub scene — garden restaurants, rooftop bars, Friday evenings on Park Street Mews, "stress-relieving lunches, or after-work drinks" with colleagues turned friends (Spend Life Traveling interview series). Hofstede Insights records Sri Lanka's power-distance score at a firm 80 alongside low individualism, a textbook hierarchical, collectivist profile that tracks neatly with the daytime formality. The rooftop bar, evidently, is where that formality is allowed to exhale.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive finding: the country with the more casual daytime hierarchy asks more of you socially, not less. Mongolian offices forgive an underdressed newcomer faster than a declined toast; Sri Lankan offices forgive social hesitation far more readily than a raised voice in a meeting. Mongolia's ritual centres on consumption — eat what's offered, drink what's poured, sing when handed the microphone — while Sri Lanka's centres on restraint: read the subtext, defer publicly, save real opinions for later. Per Gallup's Meta-Gallup State of Social Connections study, most adults worldwide report interacting regularly with colleagues; these two countries simply disagree, sharply, on what that interaction should look like once the laptops close.

The other inversion: Mongolia, the more remote and less internationally integrated economy, produces accounts of faster, deeper bonding once trust is earned, while Colombo — cosmopolitan, English-fluent, wired into global business — produces accounts of warm but more bounded professional friendliness, where the rooftop bar is a release valve rather than a route into the inner circle. Neither is more "social"; they've simply optimised for different things: Mongolia for intensity, Sri Lanka for equilibrium.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A long-time contributor summarised Sri Lankan office life plainly: workplaces are hierarchical by age and position, decisions come from the top, and questioning your boss publicly simply isn't done — something new hires tend to learn only after doing it once.*

> *Expat.com forum — A returning expat who'd once worked locally in Sri Lanka warned that most business meetings are largely ceremonial; the real work happens in the relationship-building around them, and negotiations should open with genuine praise for the other side, not a pitch.*

> *ExpatsBlog — A Fulbright teaching fellow in Ulaanbaatar recalled karaoke bars as a near-mandatory bonding ritual — "on every corner," with colleagues expecting you to perform regardless of ability — and that declining offered food or drink registers as a real social misstep.*

> *Macongolia (expat blog) — A long-term American resident of Ulaanbaatar described the disorientation of expecting quick Western-style warmth on arrival and instead finding colleagues reserved with new acquaintances; the close friendships he eventually built took years, not the instant camaraderie the exchange-program brochures implied.*

> *Spend Life Traveling — A Colombo-based consultant described the city's after-work scene as a low-key rotation of garden restaurants and rooftop bars rather than a nightlife circuit, with Friday evenings on Park Street Mews the closest thing to a standing office social institution.*

### Conclusion

The practical upshot: in Mongolia, show up hungry, say yes to the toast, and expect real onboarding to happen at a dinner table over months, not in an HR deck. In Sri Lanka, show up observant, keep disagreement private, and treat the rooftop bar invitation as a genuine, bounded olive branch rather than an obligation.

Both systems work, by the only metric that matters to the people living inside them — they simply work by opposite logic, one built on convivial excess, the other on graceful restraint. Try swapping them, and you will learn the difference quickly, and probably socially.
