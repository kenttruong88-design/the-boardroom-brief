# Belgium Pays for Birthdays, Mexico Regulates the Hours: Two Countries Misunderstand Their Under-30s

🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Belgium, according to an Acerta study reported by the Brussels Times, an employee over 55 earns 76 percent more than a 25-year-old colleague, and in banking the older worker can earn up to twice as much. In Mexico, 13.4 million people who work more than 40 hours a week are about to be offered a workweek shortened by two hours a year until 2030, while the youngest cohort of workers reports twice the weekly fatigue of the one before it. Both countries have a generational problem at the office. Belgium's is that the old are expensive and the young are impatient. Mexico's is that the old set the hours and the young are tired of them.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the formal "vous" with older colleagues until they invite you to say "tu" | Assume first names mean flat hierarchy, particularly in Wallonia, which is described as the more hierarchical region |
| Be punctual, a trait Expat.com calls essential in Brussels | Offer to stay late as a loyalty signal; about half of Flemish twenty-somethings will work overtime only for extra pay |
| Ask early whether your employer pays on a seniority scale, which many collective agreements do for white-collar staff | Treat a colleague's higher salary as a verdict on competence; it is frequently a verdict on birth year |
| Offer to coach older colleagues on tools and software, a practice local recruiters call reverse mentoring | Expect the manager to guide you weekly; only about one in four young Belgian workers say theirs does |
| Switch between French, Dutch and English according to your interlocutor | Dismiss the four-day week or part-time requests of younger staff as a fad; they are asking for them in surveys |

### In Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress formally unless the office has told you otherwise | Show up in denim and assume a young company means a casual one |
| Greet everyone on arrival and say goodbye on leaving | Skip the pleasantries to appear efficient |
| Agree in advance whether extra hours are paid or taken as time off | Assume the new 40-hour law will shorten your actual day soon; it phases in from 2027 |
| Read the contract for health insurance, hours and benefits | Rely on a verbal promise about remote work |
| Offer older managers the respect their seniority expects, then propose changes through results | Open with a request to work from home full time |

## Belgium

Belgium is an economy built to reward those who stayed. The National Bank of Belgium noted in a 2014 review that wages keep rising for workers over 50, whereas in the benchmark countries of Scandinavia, the Netherlands and Germany they flatten before retirement. The same review found that in 2012 some 46 percent of new hires were aged 20 to 29 and fewer than 3 percent were 55 or over, and that the employment rate of those over 55 sat nine points below the EU average. Retirement is being nudged later: the statutory age rose from 65 to 66 in 2025 and reaches 67 in 2030, which the Brussels Times notes provoked nationwide strikes when reform plans leaked.

The young, meanwhile, have drawn their own conclusions. Deloitte's 2025 survey in Belgium found that only 8 percent of Gen Z and 9 percent of millennials name reaching leadership as their main career goal, and that four in ten feel stressed or anxious most of the time at work. A Het Nieuwsblad survey of Flemish workers in their twenties, reported by the Brussels Times, found that a third did not want to work more than 30 hours a week and only 9 percent would exceed 40. UGent labour economist Stijn Baert pointed out the awkward footnote: more than half of that cohort actually works more than 40 hours already. The preference is for a better arrangement, not a conspiracy to do nothing.

The friction is practical. A Robert Walters analysis observes that tight labour markets push employers to pay newcomers close to what long-serving colleagues earn, which can leave seniors feeling undervalued, and BDO reports that 72 percent of Belgian employers struggle to find candidates. The resulting truce is cheerfully bureaucratic: reverse mentoring, in which younger staff coach older ones on technology, is promoted as the diplomatic solution. For a newcomer, the lesson is to enter a system where age confers status and pay, and where the people nominally junior to you may be the ones who understand the software.

## Mexico

Mexico's workforce is young, and it knows it. Daucon, citing Mexico Business News, puts millennials at 51.3 percent of the workforce and Gen Z at 12.7 percent, which means the cohorts most likely to resent the old rules are already the majority. PwC's 2025 Hopes and Fears survey, as reported by La Nación, found that 53 percent of Mexican Gen Z workers aged 18 to 28 report weekly fatigue, double the rate of Generation X, and that they are three times as likely to be bored by their tasks. Deloitte's Mexico report adds that 40 percent of Gen Z, against 26 percent of millennials, feel stressed or anxious most of the time, and that 52 percent of Gen Z have left a job for lack of purpose, against 44 percent globally.

The institutions are catching up, slowly. Mexican lawmakers approved a bill in February 2026, with 411 deputies in favour and 58 against in the detailed vote according to Mexico News Daily, that lowers the standard week from 48 to 40 hours in two-hour steps from 2027 to 2030. Critics, including a deputy from the party that supported it, noted that higher permitted overtime may cancel the benefit, and that the six-day week and the single weekly rest day survive. Al Jazeera adds that roughly 55 percent of the workforce is informal, and thus beyond the law's reach altogether. Meanwhile, according to Randstad figures cited by CNN, 71 percent of 18-to-24-year-olds in Mexico would consider another job if made to return to the office full time.

The older generation is not a villain here, merely the owner of the schedule. Expat.com advises formal dress and universal greetings; one forum poster who had worked for Dutch firms contrasts Mexico's strong sense of hierarchy with the flatter offices of the Netherlands. The result is a workplace in which younger staff push for purpose, flexibility and a lower tolerance for unpaid extra hours, while senior managers hold the formalities. If you are the foreign hire, you will be asked to translate between the two.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries have inverted the usual story. Belgium has an old-age premium written into pay scales and a young cohort that responds by asking for less of everything: fewer hours, less leadership, more flexibility. Mexico has a young majority whose demands are largely met by law only on a timetable measured in years, which is why surveys of Mexican youth read like a grievance file and surveys of Belgian youth read like a negotiating position. Hofstede Insights scores Belgium at 94 for uncertainty avoidance and 65 for power distance, which explains the fondness for scales, procedures and the correct form of address; Mexico, in the summary published by Mexico News Daily, scores 82 on uncertainty avoidance and sits high on power distance, which explains the same fondness arriving by a different route.

For the person weighing a move, the question is who holds the clock. In Belgium the clock is collective: wage scales, retirement ages and working-time agreements move slowly and fairly predictably, and a young expat should expect the pay scale, not the individual, to decide the argument about salary. In Mexico the clock is personal: the manager decides when the day ends, and the new law will take four years to bite and does not reach the informal half of the economy at all. Belgian generations quarrel about money. Mexican generations quarrel about time.

## Conclusion

Belgium will pay you more for being older and ask you to be patient about it; Mexico will pay you less, promise you a shorter week eventually, and ask you to be there on time in the meantime. Neither country has solved the generational problem, but they have chosen different places to hide it: in a salary grid in one case and in a schedule in the other.

Before you accept, ask for the pay scale in Belgium and the real hours in Mexico, in writing, and find out whether the relevant manager is closer to 30 or to 60. The surveys suggest you will be happier with whichever of those answers matches what you came for. The generation gap, like the commute, is easier to manage once you know its length.
