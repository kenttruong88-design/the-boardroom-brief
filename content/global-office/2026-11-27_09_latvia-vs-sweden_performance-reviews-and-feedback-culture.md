# Latvia Will Not Tell You You're Doing Well, Sweden Will Not Tell You You're Not

🇱🇻 Latvia · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Performance management is the one corporate ritual that nearly everyone agrees on and almost nobody does well. A study by SSE Riga of organisations in Latvia and Lithuania found that 87 percent agree performance evaluation is integral to meeting strategic goals, while about a quarter admit they are not effective at it. The two countries examined here arrive at the same failure by opposite routes. In Latvia, the review tends to be a form with a polite number on it. In Sweden, it is a pleasant conversation in which the criticism may or may not have been present. For someone moving abroad for work, the practical question is how to learn where you actually stand.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager outright for specific strengths and gaps, since the default review will not volunteer them | Read a neutral, vaguely positive score as a verdict |
| Keep your own written record of results and achievements before any review | Assume praise will be offered when you do good work |
| Check whether your employer runs a probation period and what feedback it includes | Wait for a warning before a bad probation ends |
| Raise concerns early and privately, with facts | Expect a colleague to volunteer difficult news about your work |
| Ask what promotion and pay decisions are based on | Assume promotion criteria are transparent |

### Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the utvecklingssamtal as a dialogue and arrive with your own goals and self-assessment | Treat it as a verdict delivered to you |
| Listen for what is not praised, because that is often the criticism | Take a warm meeting as proof that all is well |
| Frame your own feedback positively and invite discussion | Send a blunt email about a problem and expect gratitude |
| Prepare the ground in informal conversations before a decision meeting | Spring objections for the first time in the room |
| Ask for tasks and responsibility to be written down | Assume a vague yes means a commitment |

## Latvia: The Form Is Completed, the Conversation Is Optional

The most useful single observation about Latvian feedback comes from The Baltic Times, which notes that people in Latvia tend to avoid both praise and open discussion of difficulties. The predictable result is an evaluation that hands out neutral, vaguely positive scores with no explanation, leaving the employee unsure of strengths and weaknesses alike. This is an author's observation rather than a survey, and it should be weighed accordingly, but it fits the wider evidence. According to the SSE Riga study, 26 percent of participating organisations in the two Baltic states use calibration committees, which they associate with more transparency and consistency. That is a minority, though a respectable one. The same study found that about two-thirds of Latvian respondents do not see diversity and inclusiveness as important to performance evaluation, which its authors tie to rather conservative social norms, and a Fontes compensation survey cited alongside it found a gender pay gap of 4.7 percent for men and women in the same job. An appraisal system that does not look closely at fairness will rarely look closely at anything else.

The inclusion data points the same way. PwC's 2025 Baltic survey found that only 29 percent of Latvian employees feel fully included and valued at work, and 42 percent have considered changing jobs because of unfair promotion opportunities or an insufficiently inclusive environment. Among the reasons people stay, the survey lists competitive pay, a friendly team and a supportive manager. Employers, meanwhile, are not exactly overflowing with strategy: 47 percent of Latvian employers have no specific human capital strategy and react as issues arise. The reasonable inference is that feedback in Latvia depends more on the individual manager than on the system, which is excellent news if you draw a good one.

Hofstede Insights offers a framework for such differences, built on dimensions like power distance and individualism, but live country scores could not be retrieved for this piece, so none are quoted here. The anecdotal record on the harder end of management is not reassuring. An anonymous Glassdoor reviewer describing a Riga office of a foreign firm reports probation terminations without prior performance warnings or constructive feedback, and HR that favours its relationship with management. One review is one review. It is also a useful reminder to ask what feedback you will receive before the day you need it.

## Sweden: The Dialogue Is Lovely and the Message May Be Elsewhere

Sweden does not lack a feedback process. It has a famous one. The utvecklingssamtal translates closer to dialogue than review, according to a Swedish communications consultancy, and the conventions are collaborative, with the employee often asked to assess their own work. The difficulty, according to The Irish Times, is that many Swedes find giving or receiving negative feedback hard. The accepted workaround is the sandwich method, criticism between two compliments, and The Local adds that the sandwich can fail in an instructive way: colleagues may seize on the compliments and ignore the filling.

The Local also describes a more subtle deflection, in which a reply begins with a yes and then drifts into praise, leaving the original concern untouched, and a quiet exclusion it calls the Swedish freeze-out, for which it suggests formal channels such as a union-appointed safety ombudsman. It recommends documenting who is responsible for each task, since vague commitments are otherwise easy to mistake for agreement. The Local also describes a work culture of consensus, large meetings in which everyone is heard, and real negotiation in informal pre-meetings. Conflict aversion means problems often surface indirectly, and one executive found that asking staff to raise issues face to face made some of them visibly anxious. Foreign managers add that the flat structure has costs, since staff may bypass the person concerned and go to a senior leader. Hierarchy, in other words, is thin, and feedback is thinner. Anyone expecting a flat hierarchy to produce blunt candour has the mechanism backwards.

None of this makes Swedish workplaces unpleasant, and the same reporting finds sensible hours and less open conflict than elsewhere. It does make them hard to read. The employee who waits to be told that something is wrong may wait through several seasons of pleasant meetings, while the one who learns the vocabulary of understatement can find the bad news in what was left unsaid.

## The Reckoning

On paper the two countries are opposites. Latvia is described as short on both praise and difficult conversation, producing evaluations that say little. Sweden is long on conversation and comfort, producing evaluations that also say little. Both leave the employee to interpret. The difference is the texture of the silence. In Latvia it is the absence of a process, and in Sweden it is the presence of an elaborate one with the content gently removed.

The practical consequence is the same for a newcomer: you are responsible for extracting your own feedback. In Latvia, ask directly and keep records, because the system will not do it for you and a probation can end without a warning. In Sweden, bring your own self-assessment to the dialogue, ask what would make your work stronger, and treat unprompted warmth as ambiguous. The Latvian format rewards persistence. The Swedish one rewards fluency in implication, which takes longer to acquire than the language itself.

> *Ten Years in Sweden blog, paraphrased: the boss answered good news quickly and warmly, then went quiet on reports of problems, and a blunt email about neglected facilities drew outrage rather than action*

> *JustJoin.it interview, a Polish engineer in Sweden, paraphrased: negative feedback is hinted at rather than stated, one-to-ones are mostly positive, and whatever was not praised may be the thing to fix*

## Conclusion

If you value being told where you stand, neither country will do it automatically, so pick the failure mode you can manage. Latvia offers fewer conversations and more room to ask for your own, with the caveat that the system's safeguards vary by employer. Sweden offers a richer process and a more courteous atmosphere, with the caveat that the useful sentence may be the one nobody said.

Whichever you choose, write down your own results, request specific feedback in writing, and ask early what a good year looks like. The Latvian manager may withhold praise, and the Swedish manager may withhold criticism, but both will, with enough polite persistence, tell you something. It may only take a few meetings.
