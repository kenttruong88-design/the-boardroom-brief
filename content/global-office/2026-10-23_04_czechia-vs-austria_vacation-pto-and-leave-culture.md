**Twenty Days, Thirty Days, and the Art of the Window: Vacation in Czechia and Austria**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Austria pays its employees fourteen times a year and gives them twenty-five days off to spend the extra two paychecks; Czechia pays twelve times a year, gives twenty days off, and has quietly invented a second, doctor's-note-free category of leave that nobody puts in the employment contract. Both countries will tell you, with a straight face, that they have excellent work-life balance. Both are, this time, telling the truth. The gap is not in who grants more rest on paper — it is in who actually takes it, and how much creative accounting is required to get there. This is the part of the relocation packet that HR will not walk you through.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract for the real number — many employers in finance and tech quietly add a fifth week on top of the 20-day statutory minimum | Assume you can roll leave indefinitely — the Labour Code requires an annual leave schedule, and unused days can legally be forfeited |
| Ask HR whether "sick days" are a listed benefit — up to a handful of paid days off with no doctor's note required | Confuse a "sick day" with formal sick leave — real illness still requires a doctor-issued neschopenka from day one |
| Submit leave requests through the official rota process, not a casual Slack message — approval is formally the employer's call | Plan a big trip around December 24 assuming it's a full holiday — it's an afternoon/evening observance, not always a full day off |
| Expect your Czech colleagues to take vacation in short bursts rather than one long block | Read a two-week block of leave as the cultural default — most Czechs still split leave into shorter stretches |
| If your firm offers "unlimited" vacation, ask how much people actually take — usage varies wildly by team | Take "unlimited" at face value as more generous than the 20-day statutory floor — it can mean less, if nobody tracks it |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate salary as an annual, not monthly, figure — most contracts pay out over 14 installments, not 12 | Compare a "monthly salary" figure directly against a 12-paycheck home-country offer without adjusting for this |
| Learn the word Fenstertag — a single day taken around a public holiday routinely buys a four-day weekend | Expect to self-approve leave on your own schedule — vacation timing is a two-sided negotiation, and employers can recall you (with compensation) |
| Block out early vacation requests for August — many firms, especially manufacturing, run a Betriebsurlaub shutdown | Book a trip for late August assuming the office will be staffed — entire departments may be closed |
| Track tenure carefully — leave rises from 25 to 30 days per year after 25 years with the same employer | Assume moving employers resets nothing but your title — only service with the same company counts toward the 30-day threshold |
| Confirm in writing when your holiday and Christmas bonuses land — timing varies by collective agreement | Resign abruptly without checking the fine print — some agreements claw back the bonus for an unauthorized early exit |

---

Czechia's statutory floor is twenty working days, roughly four weeks, set by the Zákoník práce, the Labour Code that has governed employment since the 1960s and been patched repeatedly since. On top of that sit thirteen public holidays, for a notional 33 days off a year — solid by global standards, unremarkable by European ones. The more interesting number is informal: Platy.cz's salary survey data, reported by HRnews.cz, found Czech employees receiving roughly one extra week beyond the legal minimum on average, and separate reporting found that 30 percent of employees now get "sick days" as a named perk — paid, doctor's-note-free absences nominally for recovery, colloquially also used, per BusinessInfo.cz's reporting, to recover from a demanding weekend rather than a demanding virus. Access to this benefit is wildly uneven: common in banking (69 percent) and insurance (67 percent), rare in gastronomy (9 percent).

What Czechs do not do, according to a UP Česká republika/ČTK survey covered by Seznam Zprávy, is take that leave in one piece. About a quarter of respondents had not had a single continuous break of fourteen days or more in the past five years, and only 7 percent had managed one so far that year. Finances were the most-cited obstacle, followed by the simple nature of the job. Some employers have responded by going further than the law requires: Expats.cz reported that insurer Direct pojišťovna and grocery-delivery firm Rohlík.cz now offer effectively unlimited leave, with one executive at HR-software firm Seyfor noting that a key employee recovering from burnout took three months off and came back functional — though the same reporting flagged the obvious risk, that uneven uptake just redistributes the exhaustion to whoever still feels too responsible to leave.

Austria sets the floor higher and rarely strays from it: twenty-five working days by the Urlaubsgesetz, rising to thirty after twenty-five years with the same employer — only time with that specific company counts, so job-hopping resets the clock. Add thirteen public holidays and the nominal total reaches 38 days, five more than Czechia's. More structurally important for a transplant's monthly budget is that Austrian salaries are typically paid out fourteen times a year rather than twelve, with the extra two installments — effectively a holiday bonus and a Christmas bonus — taxed more favorably than ordinary pay, per The Local Austria's explainer. OECD figures compiled via Our World in Data put Austrian average annual working hours meaningfully below Czechia's (roughly 1,611 hours versus 1,788 in 2023), suggesting the extra days off are not merely nominal. Austrians have also built an entire micro-vocabulary around stretching a single vacation day into a four-day weekend — Fenstertag, "window day" — around a midweek public holiday, a habit specific enough that using the regional term correctly, rather than the German Brückentag, functions as a minor local credibility signal.

Neither system was designed with a foreign hire in mind, which matters more than it sounds. Austria's leave structure assumes a worker who stays: the climb from 25 to 30 days rewards loyalty to one employer specifically, a design that quietly penalizes exactly the kind of multi-employer, multi-country career path that brings most expatriates to Vienna in the first place. Czechia's structure assumes less loyalty and compensates with flexibility instead — benefits like sick days and unlimited-leave pilots are things an employer can grant or withdraw at will, which makes them a genuine perk at a good firm and a mirage at a mediocre one. A candidate comparing offers should treat the headline leave number in each country as a floor, not a forecast, and ask pointed questions about actual usage before signing anything.

The reckoning: on paper, Austria wins decisively — more statutory days, fewer worked hours, a bonus structure that effectively pre-funds the vacation. Czechia's lower floor is real, and its Labour Code's "use it or forfeit it" posture punishes procrastinators more severely than Austria's two-year carryover window does. But Czechia's informal economy of extra benefit-days and "unlimited" policies at ambitious employers means the actual gap, for someone who lands at the right firm, narrows considerably — and Austria's rigid Betriebsurlaub shutdowns and 25-year tenure cliffs mean its generosity comes with its own strings. Hofstede Insights scores the two countries almost identically on uncertainty avoidance (Czechia 74, Austria 70) — both want rules, not discretion, governing time off — but diverge sharply on indulgence (Czechia 29, Austria 63), a reasonably tidy explanation for why Austrians institutionalize leisure into the calendar while Czechs, structurally entitled to plenty of it, still feel vaguely guilty about using it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — A Silicon Valley transplant who moved to Vienna and later took Austrian citizenship described the shift from 80-hour weeks to a contractual five weeks of paid vacation a year as the single biggest quality-of-life change of the move, framed less as a perk than as a different operating system for work.*

> *Hacker News — A tech worker in Austria noted that 25 vacation days is simply the standard baseline in the sector, not a negotiated extra, and that the harder adjustment was recalibrating expectations around what counts as a generous offer versus a normal one.*

> *Expat.com (Austria forum) — A poster working in Austria pointed out that fourteen monthly salary payments, plus a transit pass and lunch allowance, are typical sector benefits — a reminder that a bare monthly figure on a job offer understates total compensation until the extra two installments are added back in.*

> *Expats.cz — An HR executive at a Czech company that moved to unlimited vacation reported that staff were initially skeptical and barely used any additional days in year one, only gradually working up to a handful of extra days as trust in the policy built — the opposite of the free-for-all a US reader might expect "unlimited" to mean.*

> *BusinessInfo.cz — Reporting on "sick day" benefits in Czech workplaces noted the gap between the formal premise (paid recovery leave, no doctor's note) and the common practice (used to recover from a rough weekend), a distinction new arrivals are rarely told about explicitly and mostly learn by noticing who is suddenly out on a Monday.*

### Conclusion

If the question is simply "which country gives me more days off," Austria answers it and Czechia does not: 38 nominal days against 33, a bonus structure that pays for two of the weeks you take, and fewer hours worked across the year to boot. But the number that actually determines your quality of life is the one neither country prints on a recruitment slide — whether your specific employer's culture lets you use the days you are owed without guilt, recall, or a tenure clock resetting every time you change jobs. Austria's generosity comes bundled with rigidity (Betriebsurlaub shutdowns, 25-year cliffs); Czechia's stinginess comes bundled with loopholes (sick days, unlimited-leave experiments) that a motivated employee can exploit if they pick the right firm.

For a corporate mover choosing between the two on this basis alone, the practical advice is unglamorous: in Austria, read the collective agreement before you read the brochure, and in Czechia, ask your prospective manager how many vacation days they personally took last year, then halve whatever flattering number they give you. Ask about actual usage rates in the interview, not just the headline figure, and you will learn more about either country's real leave culture than its labour code can tell you. Pack sunscreen for Austria's August shutdown and a guilty conscience for Czechia's — you will need the second one far more than you'd like.
