**Suits Are Dying in Seoul, Prints Are Mandatory on Fridays in Accra: A Wardrobe Guide for the Relocating**

**🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇰🇷 South Korea**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two office wardrobes, two completely different definitions of what counts as showing respect through clothing: in Accra, respect looks like more fabric, more color, more visible effort, especially on the one day a week devoted to it by unofficial national consensus. In Seoul, respect used to look like a dark suit and has been quietly negotiating its way toward government-endorsed shorts. Anyone packing a suitcase for either city on the assumption that "business formal" means the same thing everywhere is going to unpack into the wrong closet.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear African print (Ankara, kente-inspired pieces) on Fridays — it's expected, not optional flair | Treat Friday as a generic "casual day" and show up in jeans |
| Invest in well-tailored suits or dresses; visible tailoring effort reads as respect | Assume a westerner gets a pass on formality because of the heat |
| Keep shoes closed-toe and jewelry minimal in corporate and government settings | Wear open sandals or heavy jewelry to a bank or law-firm meeting |
| Expect banks, law firms, and government offices to run genuinely formal | Assume every workplace is as strict as a law firm — startups and creative studios are smarter-casual |
| Get a tailor early; made-to-measure is standard practice, not a luxury | Rely only on imported ready-to-wear sized for a different climate and body-shape norms |
| Ask directly if unsure — dress-code expectations vary enough by employer that guessing wrong is common even for locals | Assume one city guide covers every employer; policies genuinely vary |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default conservative on day one — dark, modest, unpatterned — then recalibrate once you've seen the actual office | Show up underdressed on the assumption "Korea is casual now" |
| Keep shoulders and cleavage covered even in warmer months | Wear sleeveless tops or short skirts assuming summer heat is an exception |
| Watch for "Cool Biz" seasonal signals — many offices now formally permit lighter, short-sleeve wear in peak summer | Wear shorts to the office without an explicit policy allowing it — still taboo in many workplaces |
| Cover visible tattoos and avoid facial piercings or unnatural hair color in traditional corporate settings | Assume personal style choices normalized elsewhere will read as neutral here |
| Notice what your specific team actually wears in the first week and match it | Copy a "Korean office fashion" blog written for a totally different industry or company age |
| Keep footwear conservative — closed shoes, minimal statement pieces | Wear bold accessories expecting them to read as confident rather than unprofessional |

Ghana's professional dress code carries a genuinely dual mandate: formal Western-style tailoring most of the week, and a Friday tradition of wearing African prints — commonly Ankara fabric or kente-inspired pieces — that functions less like "casual Friday" and more like a weekly, semi-mandatory expression of national identity inside otherwise formal institutions. Business etiquette guides consistently describe Ghana as a genuinely formal business culture — banks, law firms, and government offices in particular — where suits, tailored dresses, closed-toe shoes, and minimal jewelry are the baseline, tempered only slightly by an acknowledgment that expatriates sweating through the heat won't be penalized for skipping a jacket. What's notable, and easy for a newcomer to miss, is that the Friday print tradition isn't a relaxation of the dress code at all — it's arguably a stricter one, just aimed at a different value: cultural visibility rather than corporate uniformity. Academic coverage of the trend (including recent fieldwork on career women in Bolgatanga) frames it explicitly as identity-focused professional dressing, not a loosening of standards.

South Korea's story is the inverse: a formal baseline in visible, rapid retreat. As recently as the late 1990s, 85.2% of Korean men reportedly wore formal business attire to work, per Samsung Fashion Institute data cited by the Korea JoongAng Daily; the men's formalwear market has since fallen from a peak of 6.87 trillion won in 2011 to roughly 4 trillion won and holding. The retreat accelerated through COVID-era remote work and has now become government policy: "Cool Biz," a seasonal campaign urging employers to relax dress codes in summer, is explicitly tied to national energy-conservation targets (a 40% emissions cut by 2030), not just comfort. But the loosening has limits that trip up newcomers constantly — shorts remain taboo in many conservative offices even under Cool Biz, and the underlying modesty norms (no bare shoulders, no visible tattoos, no unnatural hair color, closed-toe shoes even in sandal weather) haven't moved nearly as much as the suit-versus-no-suit headline suggests. Hofstede's long-term-orientation framing helps explain why: South Korea's cultural emphasis on long-range planning and social conformity shows up in dress the same way it shows up in career paths — visible change happens, but slowly, and through consensus rather than individual rebellion.

**The Reckoning:** The irony here is that both countries are moving in the direction of more individual visibility within a formal frame, just via opposite mechanisms. Ghana is doing it through a mandated collective ritual — everyone wears prints on Friday, together, which paradoxically makes individual cultural expression feel safe precisely because it's synchronized. Korea is doing it through slow institutional permission-granting — Cool Biz, casual Fridays creeping into more offices — where individual deviation still feels risky without an explicit policy sanctioning it first. A Ghanaian professional transplanted to Seoul might find the lack of a clear "print day" disorienting; a Korean professional transplanted to Accra might be startled that "everyone dresses differently on the same day" is the actual rule, not an oversight.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — In a long-running thread on workplace dress codes, several respondents converged on the same point: people don't just dress differently by country, they dress differently by how visibly the office is watched — a bank teller-facing role gets scrutinized in ways a back-office analyst role in the same building doesn't, which one commenter said mattered more than any national dress-code guide they'd read before relocating.*

> *Blind (via reporting in The Korea Herald and The Investor) — Anonymous Korean office workers discussing the Cool Biz rollout expressed open skepticism alongside cautious hope, with several noting that a company technically permitting shorts didn't mean wearing them wouldn't still get you quietly read as junior or careless — official policy and unspoken norm, multiple posters agreed, were running on different timelines.*

> *FlyerTalk (Africa forum) — A traveler preparing for business meetings in Ghana said they'd gotten genuinely conflicting advice before arriving — some contacts insisted on full suits, others said local colleagues would find a Western visitor overdressed — and that the real answer only became clear by observing what their specific host company's senior staff wore on day one, not from any general country guide.*

> *Talesmag (real post report, U.S. foreign-service community in Accra) — A posted account from someone stationed in Accra noted that formal dress expectations were taken more seriously at government-adjacent institutions than the general "Ghana is hot, dress lightly" travel advice implies, and that erring formal on first meetings was consistently safer than erring casual.*

> *Hanguljobs (unwritten-rules career guide) — The piece argued that new hires who thrive in their first 90 months in a Korean office aren't the ones who prepped the most language study, but the ones who clocked the office's actual, unwritten dress and behavior norms fastest — treating the printed dress code as a floor, not the real answer.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway isn't "Ghana is formal, Korea is casual" — that gets it backwards often enough to be actively misleading. It's that Ghana's formality is culturally legible and consistently signaled (watch for Friday, follow the tailoring standard, you'll rarely be surprised), while Korea's formality is in active, uneven transition, which means the safest strategy is watching your specific office rather than trusting any general rule, including this one. The line I'd actually give a friend packing for either move: buy one good tailored piece before you land in Accra, and don't buy the shorts for Seoul until you've actually seen someone else wearing them first.
