# China Will Hire the "Beautiful Girl." Jordan Won't Let 86% of Its Educated Women Through the Door At All.

**🇨🇳 China · 🇯🇴 Jordan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

China's gender problem at work is mostly a problem of what happens after a woman is hired — pay gaps, glass ceilings, job ads that, as recently as a few years ago, openly asked for "beautiful girls" alongside the required skills. Jordan's gender problem is further upstream: with female labor force participation sitting near 14 percent, one of the lowest rates measured anywhere in the world, the dominant story isn't workplace friction, it's women who never got a workplace to begin with.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Recognize that women make up over 43% of China's workforce — broad participation is not the issue here. | Assume broad participation means pay parity; the gender pay gap runs close to 13%, and "equal work, unequal pay" remains widespread. |
| Expect strong representation at mid-management and supervisory levels (nearly 42% of supervisory roles). | Expect that to translate evenly to top leadership — board-level representation, while improving, still lags. |
| Watch for marriage- and age-related social pressure shaping women's career decisions around their late twenties. | Assume this pressure is purely personal; it visibly shapes hiring and promotion dynamics too. |
| Take job-ad language seriously as a signal — openly gendered postings have been well documented at major firms. | Assume this is historical; nearly one in five 2018-era civil service postings specified "men only" or "men preferred," and enforcement mechanisms remain limited. |

#### 🇯🇴 Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that the core barrier is entry, not advancement — participation sits near 14%, against an overall labor force rate around 33%. | Assume the barrier is skill or ambition; Jordan has one of the region's most educated female populations. |
| Recognize that family control over where and when women work is a real, named dynamic, not a stereotype. | Assume a woman's own preference, rather than family negotiation, determines her job choice or hours. |
| Expect strong individual advocacy and visible progress in specific sectors like tech. | Expect that progress to be evenly distributed; many women still describe working "ten times harder" to be seen as equally capable. |

---

**China**

China's gender dynamics at work present a genuine paradox: broad inclusion at the base, persistent inequity further up. Women accounted for 320 million workers in 2023 — 43.3% of total employment — and hold meaningful shares of mid-level authority, with 41.9% of supervisory roles and 37.7% of board seats, according to the 2024 China Women's Workplace Status Report. But the same report puts the average monthly gender pay gap at 12.9% (women earning roughly RMB 8,958 against men's RMB 10,289), and flags "equal work, unequal pay" as still widespread.

The more pointed problem shows up in hiring itself. A Human Rights Watch analysis of more than 36,000 job postings found major tech firms running openly gendered recruitment: Alibaba ads promising "beautiful girls" as coworkers, a Tencent recruitment post quoting an employee who joined partly because HR staff "were very pretty," a Huawei posting from 2013 stating flatly that "beautiful girls are needed." Nearly one in five Chinese civil-service postings in the 2018 recruitment cycle specified "men only" or "men preferred" — and the laws meant to prohibit this lack a clear legal definition of gender discrimination, leaving enforcement thin. Layered on top of workplace dynamics is intense social pressure around marriage timing: one Shanghai-educated department manager, Fanggu, described resigning a hard-won management role after turning 25 under family pressure to marry, after noticing male colleagues grow distant and female colleagues grow resentful of her promotion; a 29-year-old Beijing product manager, Limeng, with a British master's degree, described roughly 200 unsuccessful blind dates navigating a dating market where, in her words, "men are still thinking in the old ways" about age.

**Jordan**

Jordan's challenge is structural and upstream of almost everything a typical "workplace culture" article would normally cover. Female labor force participation measured 13.8% in a recent World Bank assessment — against an overall labor force participation rate of 33% in the same period — making it "one of the lowest globally," despite Jordan having one of the region's more educated female populations. The World Bank's own breakdown names specific, addressable barriers: gaps in targeted education-to-employment pathways, caregiving duties, inadequate transportation access, and broader social norms, rather than any single dominant cause.

The texture behind the statistic, reported through named interviews, is less about capability than control. Romouz Sadeq, a Jordanian business owner, put it directly: "Families want women to work, but they want to decide where and when they work. It is a control many families are not ready to give up" — and noted that by defaulting to keeping women from traveling for work, families inadvertently hand men better qualifications, contacts, and opportunities over time. In Jordan's tech sector specifically, where individual progress has been more visible, Yara Alkhader, CTO of a Jordanian startup, described having to "work ten times harder to prove yourself," and noted that after-work networking — where men build the relationships that drive promotion — is a space women often miss entirely because of family obligations at home. Hanan Khader, who runs a coding academy, recalled a male subordinate initially dismissing her offer to help with a technical problem, only to watch her solve it in thirty minutes.

**The Reckoning**

Hofstede's power distance scores land at an identical 80 for China and for the broader Arab World cluster that Jordan is grouped under in the original study (Jordan wasn't individually surveyed), which initially suggests more similarity than the data on the ground reveals. What actually diverges is where hierarchy gets applied: in China, hierarchical, often paternalistic workplace structures still permit — even while constraining — broad female participation and real, if partial, advancement. In Jordan, the same hierarchical instinct operates one level earlier, inside the family, determining whether a woman enters formal employment at all before workplace dynamics ever become the relevant variable.

That makes the two countries' problems genuinely different categories, not just different degrees of the same issue. China's is a workplace-equity problem layered on top of near-universal participation. Jordan's is a participation problem that workplace policy alone — no matter how progressive an individual employer's hiring practices are — can't fully solve, because the decisive negotiation often happens at the kitchen table, not the interview.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *CSMonitor (named interview) — Fatemah Hussein, a Jordanian factory worker, described the modern double burden plainly: "Our mothers and grandmothers kept a home and raised children. But we have two careers: full-time jobs at home and outside" — adding, of the broader shift toward women working, "we are in the 21st century, and we are not going back."*

> *Middle East Eye (named interview) — Yara Alkhader, CTO of a Jordanian tech startup, described programming as something she found genuinely fulfilling — "it's like composing music" — while noting that advancement required working "ten times harder to prove yourself," partly because the after-work socializing that builds men's internal networks is largely closed off to women managing family obligations.*

> *CNN, citing Human Rights Watch research (named research findings) — Internal recruitment materials at some of China's largest tech employers explicitly promised "beautiful girls" as co-workers or stated preferences for male applicants outright, with one Huawei job-fair post in 2013 stating simply: "beautiful girls are needed" — language HRW found alongside a near-total lack of legal enforcement against it.*

### Conclusion

If you're building a team in China, the equity work is granular and ongoing — pay audits, promotion tracking, scrutinizing your own job-ad language against a documented industry pattern of casual sexism that regulators haven't meaningfully stopped. If you're building a team in Jordan, the real work happens before the job posting even goes up: the limiting factor isn't usually the candidate pool's competence, it's whether the candidates you want were ever permitted to apply.

The honest version: in China, ask what a woman is being paid relative to the man next to her. In Jordan, ask who else in her life gets a vote on whether she takes the job at all — and don't be surprised if the honest answer isn't her employer.
