# Lunch Is Not Lunch: A Working Guide to Getting Fed and Getting Ahead in Accra and Libreville

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇦 Gabon

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Accra, the office is a small nation-state with its own cabinet, and lunch is the parliament where it governs itself. In Libreville, the office is a quieter embassy, where the real business happens over coffee that nobody admits is a meeting. Both cities will tell you, cheerfully, that work-life balance is excellent here. Neither city means the same thing by it, and only one of them will actually invite you to the party.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every person in the office individually each morning, even the ones you saw five minutes ago in the stairwell | Walk past a colleague's desk in silence; it reads as a diagnosable grievance |
| Accept the food someone offers you at their desk, even a small bite | Smell or visibly inspect food before eating it — treat it as an insult to the cook |
| Budget real time for meetings to start late ("Ghana Man Time") and bring something to do while you wait | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming the first one ends on time |
| Join the WhatsApp office group chat; this is where real planning for team lunches and events happens | Rely on the official calendar invite alone — it is a formality, not a commitment device |
| Let older or more senior colleagues speak first, and defer visibly in meetings | Interrupt a senior colleague's point, even to agree with it loudly |
| Show up to the office "outdooring" or naming ceremony if invited; declining without a real excuse is noted | Treat a colleague's invitation to a family event as optional small talk |

#### 🇬🇦 Gabon

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open every meeting and most emails with a few lines of small talk before the actual subject | Launch straight into the agenda item — it reads as cold, almost hostile |
| Use "je vais essayer" (I will try) as the polite decline it is, and listen for it from others | Take a soft "I will try" as a real commitment and build your week around it |
| Shake hands with everyone in a room individually on arrival, and again on leaving | Give a single group wave and assume it covers the greeting obligation |
| Treat the tea or coffee break as actual networking time, not a pause from work | Skip the break to "save time" — you lose more goodwill than minutes |
| Dress business-formal by default and watch what the room actually wears before downgrading | Show up in startup-casual on day one at a ministry or a legacy employer |
| Build a social life outside the office deliberately, often through expatriate or InterNations circles | Assume the office will organically produce your social calendar — it mostly won't |

Ghana's working week is, on paper, unremarkable: the Labour Act caps normal hours at eight a day and forty a week, with a mandatory unpaid break of at least thirty minutes after five continuous hours on the job, and at least twenty-four consecutive hours of weekly rest (Playroll, citing Ghanaian labour statute). What the paperwork omits is that the real structuring unit of the Ghanaian office day is not the hour but the greeting. Colleagues who skip the individual morning round of hellos are not merely rude; they are, in the local framing, suspected of carrying a grudge. The office, as one long-running feature on workplace life in Ghana put it, functions as "a mini Ghana" of regional personalities who bring football debates, newspapers, and occasionally actual visitors into the building during work hours (Jobberman Ghana, "Office Buzz"). Lunch itself is less a scheduled event than a running negotiation: who brought waakye today, who is owed jollof from last week, who is quietly not invited to the fufu run because they complained about the smell of someone's banku the previous Tuesday.

The country's Gallup-measured relationship with stress complicates the postcard image of easy-going office warmth. Ghana ranked fourth among Sub-Saharan African countries for daily workplace stress in Gallup's 2023 State of the Global Workplace findings, in a region where 46 percent of workers report feeling a lot of stress on a given day (Gallup, via CitiNewsroom reporting, 2024). The sociable surface and the stressed interior are not actually in tension — Gallup's own analysis finds that engagement, not location or hours, is the dominant driver of workplace stress, and an office that invests heavily in belonging rituals is, in effect, managing stress the expensive, informal way, through relationship rather than policy. The famous unpunctuality — "Ghana Man Time," openly debated in the local press as a drag on investor confidence (GhanaWeb) — is best read not as laziness but as a sign that, in this economy of belonging, showing up exactly on time to a thing nobody else takes seriously signals that you don't understand the room.

Gabon runs on a different clock entirely, set partly by its oil-and-civil-service economy and partly by its formality. Hofstede Insights data place Gabon, like much of Francophone Central Africa, high on power distance, meaning hierarchy is explicit, decisions flow from the top, and a junior employee contradicting a senior one in a meeting is a genuine social event, not just an awkward one (Hofstede Insights country comparison). Safeguard Global's workplace guide to Gabon describes a culture where direct refusal is avoided in favor of hedges like "I will try," where email carries the formal weight and messaging apps carry the operational chatter, and where tea or lunch breaks function as the primary venue for the informal coordination that in flatter cultures would happen openly in meetings. One long-serving Foreign Service household's real post report on Libreville — filed through TalesMag's post-report archive — described the capital bluntly as "a slow-moving post with not a lot going on outside of work," where most expatriate socializing collapses onto a single compound pool and bar, because the city offers few other reliable third spaces.

That isolation compounds for anyone outside the diplomatic or multinational bubble. On the expat.com forum, a member who had relocated from a border town to Port-Gentil described Gabonese acquaintances as initially "very closed," friendly chiefly when a service was wanted, and newcomers as routinely labeled "strangers" and discussed behind their backs even in mixed company — a cultural-misread trap for anyone expecting the office small talk to translate quickly into real friendship. His own fix, after two years, was blunt: build your social circle deliberately among other foreigners, bring small gifts to build rapport locally, and treat work itself — with its built-in structure and routine — as the easier, more legible half of expatriate life in Gabon. A separate practical guide to relocating there notes that InterNations meet-ups, women's clubs, and sports associations are where Libreville's thin expatriate social fabric actually gets woven, because the office, courteous as it is, was never going to do it for you.

### The Reckoning

Put the two offices side by side and the irony sharpens: Ghana's workplace is loud, inclusive, and permeable — anyone can wander into anyone's cubicle with lunch, a grievance, or a cousin visiting from Kumasi — yet it is also, by Gallup's own measurement, one of the more stressful working environments on the continent. Gabon's workplace is formal, hierarchical, and comparatively closed, with hedged refusals and handshake rituals standing in for the warmth Accra hands out for free, yet the stress complaint there is loneliness, not pressure — the office works fine; it's everything after six p.m. that's the problem.

The practical consequence for a newly arrived employee is almost the inverse of what the brochures imply. In Accra, your calendar discipline will matter less than your willingness to be interrupted, fed, and occasionally delayed; the social cost of being aloof is higher than the social cost of being late. In Libreville, your patience with formality and small talk will matter more than your instinct to "just get things done," and you will need to build an actual life outside the office, deliberately and early, because the office was never going to hand you one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *expat.com — a foreign resident of Port-Gentil described his early months as isolating: locals were friendly mainly when a favor was wanted, newcomers were openly called "strangers," and even small gestures like bringing gifts made a visible difference in how quickly he was accepted.*

> *TalesMag Foreign Service post report, Libreville — a diplomatic household candidly called the city "a slow-moving post," noting that most expatriate socializing happened at a single compound pool and bar rather than anywhere the city itself offered.*

> *ExpatExchange.com, Ghana community board — a longtime resident warned that colleagues and acquaintances in Accra will often "tell you what you want to hear, not the real situation," making it hard to know where you actually stand until a decision has already quietly been made elsewhere.*

> *jarniascyril.com expat guide to Gabon — the advice given to newcomers was to treat InterNations meet-ups, sports clubs, and expatriate social groups as a deliberate project from week one, because waiting for an organic social circle to form through the office alone rarely works.*

> *expat.com, Ghana etiquette thread — a contributor's most-repeated practical tip had nothing to do with meetings or deadlines: never visibly smell or inspect food offered to you at a colleague's desk, since the gesture itself, not the food, is what's being judged.*

### Conclusion

The single difference worth planning your first month around is this: Ghana's office will absorb you whether you're ready or not, and the discipline required is social stamina — showing up for the outdooring, tolerating the late start, eating what's offered without flinching. Gabon's office will remain correct and a little distant no matter how long you stay, and the discipline required is initiative — you build the dinner party, the sports league, the InterNations Tuesday, because nobody is going to build it for you. Neither approach is more "efficient" than the other; they're just solving for different kinds of loneliness.

If a friend asked me which one to take the posting in, I'd tell them honestly: take Accra if you want your calendar colonized by other people's lives, and take Libreville if you want your evenings to yourself — just don't expect either office to warn you which one you signed up for.
