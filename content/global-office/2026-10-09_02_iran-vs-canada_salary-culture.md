**From Tehran to Toronto: Why Nobody Told You About the Credential Gap**

**🇮🇷 Iran · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Tehran, your salary is a number the government negotiates for you once a year, in a windowless room, against inflation it mostly loses to. In Toronto, your salary is a number you are expected to negotiate for yourself, against a recruiter who has already decided what you're worth and is prepared to rescind the offer if you disagree. Statistics Canada put average weekly earnings at $1,307.86 in July 2025, up 3.3% year over year — a tidy, official figure that assumes you are being paid for the job you actually trained for. For a significant share of Iranian arrivals, that assumption is the first thing Canada quietly revokes.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the minimum wage and mandatory allowances (housing, transport, family) to be reset annually around Nowruz, in late March | Treat the posted minimum wage as a benchmark for skilled roles; it is a floor, set by a tripartite labor council, not a market signal |
| Budget for the legally mandated Eid bonus, equal to two months' basic salary before Nowruz | Expect it to be a negotiable extra; it is a statutory entitlement with a fixed cap |
| Ask for your payslip in writing — gross pay, deductions, allowances, and net pay must be itemized | Assume a quoted salary is take-home; persistent inflation means nominal figures move fast and real purchasing power is the real conversation |
| Expect sector to matter enormously — oil, gas, tech, and finance pay well above public healthcare or manufacturing | Assume credentials alone set pay; location (especially Tehran versus the provinces) shifts the number substantially |
| Plan around annual salary reviews as standard practice, not a special favor | Expect pay transparency between colleagues; informal secrecy around exact figures remains the norm |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your credentials assessed (through a body like World Education Services) before you need the result, not after a job offer depends on it | Assume a foreign professional license transfers; Canada recognizes medical and many technical credentials only from specific jurisdictions |
| Negotiate the written offer — ranges posted in some provinces are a starting point, not the ceiling | Accept a lower "starting" role as a guaranteed bridge back to your old level; many newcomers describe it as a trap, not a stepping stone |
| Price in the real cost of licensing exams, bridging courses, and the 12–18 months they typically take | Expect your years of foreign supervisory or specialist experience to count at face value on a Canadian resume |
| Ask directly whether a role prioritizes citizens and permanent residents before investing months in bridging programs | Rely on recruiter-promised gross figures; take-home pay after tax, pension, and provincial deductions can run 40–45% lower |
| Treat English or French fluency as a pre-arrival project, not something you'll pick up on the job | Assume today's hiring conditions match the stories you heard two or three years ago; several newcomers report conditions tightening recently |

### Iran

Iranian salary culture is a negotiation with the state as much as with any employer. A tripartite Supreme Labor Council — government, employers, and labor representatives — sets a new minimum wage each year, timed to the Iranian New Year in late March, with mandatory add-ons for housing, transport, and dependents layered on top (rivermate.com). For 1404 (from March 2025), that floor sat at roughly 104 million rials a month before allowances. Above the floor, pay is sharply sector-driven: oil, gas, petrochemicals, tech, and finance pull well ahead of public-sector healthcare and traditional manufacturing, and Tehran pays a real premium over the provinces.

What doesn't appear on any official schedule is persistent, high inflation, which does more to redefine a salary year over year than any negotiation does. The legally required Eid bonus — two months' basic pay, capped and paid before Nowruz — functions less as a reward than as a partial inflation hedge built into the calendar. Pay transparency, meanwhile, is informal at best: colleagues rarely compare exact figures, and a quoted number tells you less about your real position than it would in a market with public salary bands.

### Canada

Canada's salary culture looks, on paper, like a negotiation between equals: post a range, discuss it, sign a contract. For skilled immigrants, the more consequential negotiation happens earlier and is rarely winnable through charm — it's the one with a credentialing body. A pediatric specialist interviewed by IranWire put it plainly: Canada recognizes medical credentials "only from specific countries," which has kept him from practicing medicine at all (iranwire.com). A nurse in the same report spent over a year and a half on registration and additional coursework before she could work in her field — and even then landed in dementia long-term care rather than the acute-care shortage she'd been told was desperate for staff (iranwire.com).

The arithmetic compounds from there. Recruiters often quote gross figures; take-home pay after tax, CPP, and provincial deductions can land 40 to 45% lower, a gap more than one newcomer has discovered only after signing (iranwire.com). On forums like Blind, the complaints run in the opposite direction too — Canadian-born tech workers describing stagnant mid-six-figure salaries, rescinded offers after pushback, and a widening sense that Toronto's cost of living has outrun its pay scale regardless of where you were born (teamblind.com). The country isn't short on income; it's short on a clean mapping between what you were paid to do abroad and what you're credentialed to do here.

### The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights' numbers hint at why these two systems talk past each other: Canada scores 80 on individualism against Iran's 41, meaning the Canadian system expects you to advocate for your own number, while the Iranian system expects the state and your employer to arrive at it collectively, with you as a junior party to the conversation. Flip to power distance and the gap narrows but doesn't close — Iran's 58 against Canada's 39 — so the Iranian instinct to defer to an authority that sets your pay collides, in Canada, with a system that interprets silence as acceptance rather than respect.

The sharper irony is who ends up disadvantaged by which system. In Iran, the state's heavy hand protects workers from being wildly underpaid relative to a published floor, even as inflation erodes it. In Canada, the absence of that hand is sold as opportunity — right up until a credentialing body, not an employer, turns out to be the real gatekeeper on your salary, and no amount of individual negotiating skill gets you past it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *IranWire — Mina, a nurse in Montreal, recalled being told by an immigration lawyer that she and her husband would earn roughly $4,000 a month each; their actual take-home pay came to less than half that once taxes, licensing fees, and pension contributions were deducted.*

> *Iran Times — An engineer who had supervised other engineers in Iran described arriving in Canada willing to start as a drafter, only to find the reset went further than he expected: "Right now I'm starting at less than zero."*

> *Blind — A commenter in a Toronto salary thread said banks have stopped paying tech individual contributors more than roughly 120,000 CAD at any level, and that workers rehired after a layoff can face pay cuts of up to 40%.*

> *IranWire — Sahar, a registered nurse with a PhD who now works in a dementia care facility rather than acute care, said flatly: "Contrary to what they made us believe, Canada is not facing a massive nursing shortage" in the way recruiters describe.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't Iran's salary versus Canada's salary — it's Iran's predictable floor versus Canada's unpredictable ceiling, and which kind of uncertainty you can tolerate. If you're moving with a credential that travels cleanly — software engineering, skilled trades, most business roles — Canada's negotiation culture rewards confidence in a way Iran's collectively bargained system never will. If your credential is medical, legal, or otherwise jurisdiction-locked, budget eighteen months and a second exam before you budget a raise. The thing I'd actually tell a friend: ask the credentialing body for a real timeline before you ask the employer for a real number, because in Canada, the credentialing body is the one that actually decides your salary.
