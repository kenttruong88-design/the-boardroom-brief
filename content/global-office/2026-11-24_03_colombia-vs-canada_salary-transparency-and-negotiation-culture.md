# Colombia Hides the Number, Canada Prints It, and Neither Will Say It Out Loud

🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Canada has spent the past three years passing laws requiring employers to say what a job pays. Colombia, by most accounts, would prefer to discuss it after the second interview. The two countries have arrived at opposite conclusions about the same awkward question, and the answer determines whether you walk into a negotiation holding a map or a blindfold.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive with a researched number, since the posting probably will not give one | Assume a missing salary means the employer has no budget |
| Ask whether there is room to negotiate before accepting | Accept an offer on the spot if you are not satisfied |
| Offer alternatives such as bonuses, flexible work or training if pay is capped | Treat base salary as the only lever |
| Ask how the package is structured, including statutory benefits | Compare a gross figure with a net one from home |
| State your target plainly and without hedging | Bury the number under apologies |
| Check market data on posted roles before the interview | Expect the person beside you to earn what you do |

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the posted range closely, since in several provinces it is mandatory | Ignore the range and ask for something outside it |
| Benchmark against external market data before you reply | Accept the first figure because it sounds polite |
| Negotiate on the offer, not at the first screening call | Open with a number before the employer does |
| Ask about non-salary items such as vacation and flexibility | Assume the range is a promise of the top end |
| Research and rehearse, because most candidates do not | Treat silence as agreement |
| Know whether your province requires posted ranges | Assume the rule is national |

## Colombia: The Number Is a Conversation

According to El Colombiano, writing in April 2026 and citing a Pandapé market report, many Colombian companies leave salaries out of job postings to preserve room to negotiate and to stop pay from becoming the main filter for applicants. Recruiters give a short list of reasons: tailoring offers to each candidate, avoiding resentment among current staff who earn less than new hires, and keeping pay scales from rivals. The same article admits it has no Colombian figure for how common the practice is, which is itself a form of transparency.

The cost is starting to show. Michael Page's Andrés Delgado told El Colombiano that younger candidates read a missing salary as something being hidden, and the paper cites LinkedIn data suggesting postings with visible pay attract up to 35 percent more quality applications. Elempleo.com reported in February 2026 that salary secrecy is giving way to a culture of clarity, pointing to Ley 1496 de 2011 on equal pay and stronger oversight by the Superintendencia de Sociedades. Large companies are expected to show that work of equal value is not paid differently by gender, and the paper lists a labour-equity seal as a way to demonstrate it. Posting ranges is encouraged. It is not, on the evidence I could find, compulsory.

There is a darker footnote. El Colombiano also describes what it calls a surveillance wage, in which software estimates the minimum a candidate would accept using work history, how quickly past offers were taken and signs of financial vulnerability. The paper, citing a report from the Washington Center for Equitable Growth, says the practice is spreading in the United States across health, logistics and retail, and warns that workers doing the same job may end up with different pay without knowing it. The article does not say it is widespread in Colombia. It is, however, the logical end of a market in which the employer holds the number and the candidate does not.

Elempleo.com's advice to candidates is therefore unglamorous: research average pay at a company before applying and negotiate on data. In a system without posted ranges, the research is the transparency, and the candidate has to supply it personally.

Negotiation itself is treated as ordinary commerce. Portafolio, citing Hays, advises candidates to name a target without hedging, to ask whether there is room to move, and to decline to accept on the spot. Adecco's María Camila León told the paper that scarce skills give candidates more capacity to bargain. Hofstede Insights scores Colombia at 67 for power distance, 29 for individualism and 80 for uncertainty avoidance, which is a tidy summary of a country where hierarchy is expected, the group matters and a fixed rulebook is comforting. A published range would be a rulebook. Employers appear to prefer a conversation they can steer.

## Canada: The Range Is Public, the Haggling Is Not

Canada has taken the opposite route by statute. According to HR Reporter, pay information has been required in British Columbia's public postings since November 2023, and Ontario followed on 1 January 2026 for employers with 25 or more staff. Ontario's posted range may not exceed 50,000 dollars, and roles above 200,000 dollars are exempt. Prince Edward Island and New Brunswick have similar rules. Compliance is a work in progress. The same publication reports that about 64 percent of roughly 700 British Columbian employers due to file pay reports by November 2025 had done so, according to a provincial bulletin.

Employers appear to be going further than the law. A Robert Half survey of more than 2,850 hiring managers and workers, as reported by HR Reporter, found that about 70 percent of Canadian employers take transparency steps beyond what is required, and 96 percent include or plan to include ranges in job descriptions. Some 55 percent said they will pay above the planned range to land top talent. The range, in other words, is a floor with ambitions.

The same survey found that 93 percent of employers use or plan to use external market data to benchmark pay and 64 percent review salaries for parity. Employers, in short, have done their homework. Candidates are advised to do the same, since the person across the table now arrives with a spreadsheet and a legal obligation.

The candidates are less ambitious. In Robert Half's 2020 survey, only 36 percent of Canadian professionals had tried to negotiate their last offer, against 54 percent of Americans. Of those who did not, 59 percent were satisfied and 19 percent wanted more but felt uncomfortable asking. Forty percent of men negotiated, against 31 percent of women. Hofstede Insights gives Canada 39 for power distance, 72 for individualism and 48 for uncertainty avoidance: flat, individualist and relaxed about ambiguity, which should make asking easy. The data suggests that politeness has other plans.
 Even among Canadians earning more than 100,000 dollars, only 41 percent negotiated, compared with 64 percent of their American counterparts, according to the same survey as reported by Yahoo Finance.

## The Reckoning

On transparency, Canada wins on paper and Colombia loses on principle, though neither verdict is complete. A Canadian candidate in Ontario or British Columbia can read the range before applying, and many employers say they will exceed it. A Colombian candidate is more likely to find the number only when it is said aloud, and must arrive with market data of their own. The Colombian system rewards the prepared negotiator. The Canadian one rewards the prepared reader, and then largely leaves the negotiating to chance.

On negotiation, the countries swap places. Colombian advice assumes you will bargain, and treats the absence of a posted figure as an invitation. Canadian surveys suggest most professionals will not, even when the range is printed in front of them and the employer has budgeted above it. The practical result is that Colombia offers more room and less information, while Canada offers more information and a population too courteous to use it. Transparency, as the Canadian data shows, is a necessary condition for fair pay and a very poor substitute for asking.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Colombia forum) — A newcomer asked how salary and benefits are usually negotiated, what the expat tax position is and whether exchange rates bite. The thread produced little practical reply, which says something about how informally the subject is handled.*

> *El Colombiano (local press) — A recruiter at Michael Page says younger candidates now skip postings with no salary, reading the omission as something being hidden from them.*

> *Yahoo Finance Canada — A Robert Half executive warns that any successful pay conversation takes research and practice beforehand, advice aimed at a workforce that mostly does neither.*

> *HR Reporter (survey respondents) — Roughly one in five Canadians who did not negotiate say they wanted more money and simply could not bring themselves to ask.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Colombia, treat the missing salary as an opening bid. Arrive with market data, a clear number and a list of alternatives, and ask whether the offer can move. Make sure you understand how the package is built before comparing it with anything from home. If you are moving to Canada, read the posted range, check which province you are in, and then do the thing most Canadians do not, which is to ask for more.

Both systems reward preparation and punish assumption. One gives you the number and expects you to stay quiet. The other withholds it and expects you to speak. Choose according to which silence you find easier to break.
