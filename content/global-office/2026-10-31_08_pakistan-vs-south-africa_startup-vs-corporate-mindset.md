**Karachi Will Make You a Co-Founder by Friday. Johannesburg Will Make You Fill Out a Form by Friday.**

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇿🇦 South Africa**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Pakistan, startup funding collapsed by roughly ninety percent between 2022 and 2024 and the ecosystem is still calling itself vibrant. In South Africa, the official unemployment rate is 33.2 percent and the corporate sector still shuts its doors for six weeks every December. Neither fact is on the recruitment brochure, and both are the actual job.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether a startup's equity promises are in a signed term sheet, not a founder's enthusiasm | Don't assume a "Series A" means the runway is longer than six months — ask directly |
| Clarify if the employer is family-owned, and who outside the family can actually get promoted | Don't treat a C-level title at a twelve-person startup as proof of real authority or headcount |
| Expect shortened, re-timed office hours during Ramadan and plan deadlines around it | Don't schedule hard deliverables for the week before Eid without checking local calendars first |
| Build your network through referrals and WhatsApp groups before you need a job | Don't rely on job boards alone — most hiring here still runs on who vouches for you |
| In corporate roles, use seniority-appropriate titles and formality with managers | Don't bring startup-style flat-hierarchy habits into a conglomerate on day one |
| Confirm payroll history and bank-transfer reliability before accepting a smaller startup's offer | Don't assume a funded-sounding startup pays on time — ask current employees, not investors |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about the employer's B-BBEE scorecard level and what it means for your own promotion track | Don't assume your home-market salary benchmark applies here — compare against local tiers, not global SaaS pay |
| Budget employer-negotiated allowances for schooling, healthcare and security into any offer | Don't treat the 33.2 percent unemployment rate as background noise when you negotiate — it shapes leverage |
| Plan onboarding and projects around the real December–January shutdown, not through it | Don't schedule anything client-facing in the last two weeks of December |
| Ask which JSE-listed holding company actually sits above a "scrappy" startup subsidiary | Don't assume "startup" means Silicon Valley speed — late hiring and slower payment cycles are common complaints |
| Learn that code-switching between English, Afrikaans and other languages in meetings is normal, not a test | Don't ask blunt questions about crime as small talk — ask your employer directly about commute and office-area safety |
| Compare average startup pay (around R334,000, per Adzuna data) against corporate scale before you jump | Don't assume a corporate title transfers automatic authority — decision cycles are genuinely slower here |

Pakistan's corporate sector is, structurally, a handful of family dynasties wearing suits. The Nishat, Dawood and Crescent groups still anchor much of the formal economy, and *The Express Tribune* has argued plainly that concentrated family control breeds nepotism and weak meritocracy even where governance looks modern on paper. Against that backdrop, the startup scene that exploded after 2018 looked like liberation: flat hierarchies, informal dress, decisions made at the speed of a WhatsApp message instead of a memo chain. Then the money left. Funding fell by close to ninety percent between 2022 and 2024, according to tracking by ProPakistani and Nukta, before limping back to roughly $36.6 million across all of 2025 — a rounding error next to what the sector raised at its peak.

There is a third lane in Pakistan that neither "startup" nor "corporate" quite describes: exporting labour itself, remotely. Pakistan's IT and IT-enabled-services exports hit a record $4.6 billion in the 2024-25 fiscal year, per the State Bank's own figures reported by Outsource Accelerator, built on everything from formal outsourcing firms to freelancers billing Western clients in dollars from a bedroom in Lahore. It's the one segment of the economy that sidesteps both the family-conglomerate ceiling and the startup funding drought, because the customer and the paycheck both sit offshore. For a foreign hire, it matters mainly as a signal: if your prospective employer's revenue comes from export invoicing rather than local sales, the internal culture tends to borrow more from "corporate" discipline than "startup" chaos, whatever the ping-pong table in reception suggests.

The cautionary tale is Airlift, which raised $85 million and folded a year later. Reporting by *Rest of World* quotes a former warehouse manager with prior corporate experience describing the operation bluntly: "it felt like they were doing it all for valuation," he said, adding, "it didn't make sense." Other ex-employees remember the opposite feeling on arrival — one recalled an office "lit up like it was designed for some Gen Z-er," with no assigned seating. Both things were true at once, which is the Pakistani startup experience in miniature: real cultural looseness, riding on financial assumptions that evaporated fast. Gallup's 2025 workplace data captures the hangover regardless of which sector you pick — only 4 percent of Pakistani employees report being engaged at work, and just 13 percent say they're thriving in life overall.

South Africa's divide runs along a different axis entirely. Stats SA put the unemployment rate at 33.2 percent in the second quarter of 2025, so the startup-versus-corporate debate is a conversation available only to the formally employed minority. That minority then splits into what locals describe, half-jokingly, as two economies: one running on private schooling, private healthcare and private security, the other on public services stretched thin — a split a Blind commenter on a South Africa compensation thread laid out candidly, noting that a comfortable package there starts around $70,000 and depends heavily on non-salary benefits. Startup pay sits well below corporate benchmarks, averaging around R334,000 according to Adzuna data reported by BusinessTech, which is lower than it was in 2017. What startups sell instead is visibility and speed; corporates sell B-BBEE-anchored structure, JSE-listed stability, and — reliably, almost comically — an entire shutdown of normal business between mid-December and late January, something an ExpatExchange member relocating to Johannesburg flagged as the defining feature of the corporate calendar there.

**The Reckoning**

Here is the twist the brochure definitely leaves out: Pakistan's startups, which perform informality, often collapse into the same personality-driven decision-making as the family conglomerates they claim to disrupt, because there's no regulatory scaffolding forcing otherwise. South Africa's startups, which perform disruption, operate inside a far more individualist culture by Hofstede's measure — scoring 65 on individualism against Pakistan's 14 — yet still answer to B-BBEE scorecards and JSE-listed parents that make "scrappy" a matter of marketing rather than org chart.

The thriving numbers tell a similarly inverted story. Pakistan's 13 percent thriving rate reflects economic constraint pressing on everyone, founder and employee alike. South Africa's 26 percent looks healthier only because it's measured among the roughly two-thirds of the working-age population that has a job at all; Gallup's own South Africa data shows only 45 percent think it's currently a good time to find one. In both countries, "startup or corporate" is a question only the already-employed get to ask.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a commenter on a South Africa compensation thread warned that take-home pay means little without factoring in private-school fees and security costs, since the formal-economy lifestyle runs on benefits as much as salary.*

> *Rest of World — a former Airlift employee recalled his first day: the office was "lit up like it was designed for some Gen Z-er," and he was simply told to sit wherever he liked.*

> *Rest of World — a former warehouse manager with prior experience at a large Pakistani corporation said the startup's growth felt engineered purely "for valuation," adding flatly that "it didn't make sense."*

> *Dawn — a founder quoted in its startup coverage summed up the expected pace as working "25/8," while a reader comment on the piece noted employees often absorb the burnout that founders treat as a badge of honor.*

> *ExpatExchange forum — a member who relocated to Johannesburg for corporate work described the city's defining rhythm as a long, genuine wind-down each December and January, rather than the always-on grind newcomers expect.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't "fast-moving startup" versus "slow-moving corporate" in either country — it's "who actually controls the money when things go wrong." In Pakistan, that means finding out whether you're joining a founder's genuine company or a family dynasty's side project wearing a startup's clothes, because the equity promises and the flat hierarchy both depend on which one it is. In South Africa, it means understanding that you're choosing your tier of a two-speed economy before you're choosing your employer's size, and that a corporate job's bureaucracy buys you a B-BBEE-anchored structure that most startups there can't yet offer.

If you take one thing on the plane: ask about the vesting schedule in Karachi and the scorecard rating in Johannesburg, and ask both before you ask about the ping-pong table — because nobody puts the real answer in the deck, and you will otherwise find it out the hard way, over a drink you end up buying yourself. And if neither lane appeals, remember there's a third option sitting quietly in both labour markets: exporting your own labour directly, on your own invoice, to a client who never has to know what the local shutdown calendar or the local funding winter looks like at all.
