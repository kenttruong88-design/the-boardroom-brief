## Nana, Togbe, and the 87 Percent: Whose Workplace Rules Actually Hold in Accra and Muscat

**🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇴🇲 Oman**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Seven in ten young Ghanaians now hold secondary or post-secondary education, more than any generation before them, and 27% of them are still unemployed and looking, against 20% of workers twice their age (Afrobarometer). In Oman, a government-driven quota policy has spent over a decade trying to push young nationals into private-sector jobs — and 87% of Omani job seekers, including 76% of degree holders, still say they'd rather wait for a government post, even when private employers offer 25% more pay (Al Bawaba). Two very different generational standoffs, same basic shape: a younger cohort that did everything the system asked of it, arriving to find the system isn't holding up its end.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use proper honorific titles ("Nana" in Akan, "Togbe" in Ewe) and physical gestures of respect when addressing older or senior colleagues | Assume respect norms are purely ceremonial; getting them visibly wrong reads as a real insult, not a minor faux pas |
| Use indirect language and proverbs when raising a sensitive issue with a senior colleague, rather than direct criticism | Address conflict with an elder or senior figure head-on; it can be read as disrespectful regardless of how valid the point is |
| Recognize that younger Ghanaian colleagues are more educated than any prior generation but also facing sharply higher unemployment | Assume youth frustration with the system reflects entitlement rather than a genuinely worse market than their parents faced |
| Expect some real generational friction over communication style — younger workers are documented as more willing to voice dissatisfaction directly, including toward authority | Assume all generational tension here is really about "laziness" on either side; the data points to a structural mismatch, not attitude |
| Notice and respect the weight these hierarchical norms carry even in modern, Westernized offices | Treat a hierarchical greeting ritual as something you can skip once you're "familiar enough" with a colleague |

#### 🇴🇲 Oman

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the Omanization quota system before assuming hiring is purely merit-based — sector-specific national-hire percentages shape who gets hired where | Assume a private-sector role is open to you regardless of nationality; several occupations are 100% reserved for Omani nationals |
| Recognize that stability and social status, not just salary, drive the strong preference among young Omanis for government jobs | Assume a 25% private-sector pay premium alone will pull talented young nationals away from public-sector security |
| Expect real workplace formality and calm — one common expat account describes Omani offices as notably free of visible conflict or raised voices | Assume that calm surface means no hierarchical pressure exists; abusive or intimidating management does surface in specific accounts |
| Take youth unemployment frustration seriously as a real, named political and economic issue, not background noise | Dismiss visible frustration among young Omani jobseekers as unrelated to how foreign hires are perceived in the same market |
| Budget for an early start to the working day as a genuine cultural adjustment, not a personal quirk of one employer | Expect Omanization percentages to be static; specific sector quotas and enforcement penalties have shifted materially in recent years |

Ghana's generational gap is fundamentally a credentials-versus-opportunity mismatch. Young Ghanaians are dramatically better educated than their parents' generation, yet face a 27% job-seeking rate versus 20% among 36-to-55-year-olds, and only 14% of them approve of the government's job-creation record (Afrobarometer). Layered on top of that economic gap is a cultural one: formal respect for elders — specific honorific titles, physical gestures, indirect language when raising a grievance — remains a real, enforced workplace and social norm (Modernghana), even as one commentator specifically flagged a generational shift where "many young people believe insults and disrespect are the most effective ways to voice their dissatisfaction" with traditional and political leaders. Ghana's Hofstede profile (Power Distance 80, Individualism just 15) explains why this shift registers as more than generational irritability — it's a visible crack in a system that has, for generations, run on hierarchical deference as a genuine social operating system, not just etiquette.

Oman's generational gap is a policy-versus-preference mismatch, engineered from the top down. Omanization sets minimum national-hire percentages ranging from 25% in construction to 90% in banking and finance, reserves specific roles (HR, reception, security, commercial driving) exclusively for Omani nationals, and backs the policy with real financial penalties — OMR 500 a month per unfilled Omani position under a 2023 Royal Decree (Hyring). Yet the policy's own target demographic resists it: 87% of Omani job seekers still prefer government work, a preference that holds even against a 25% private-sector salary premium, driven by job stability, social status, and a near-universal belief (92% of respondents) that "the government has a responsibility to provide jobs" (Al Bawaba). Named young Omanis have been blunt about the resulting frustration: 25-year-old graduate Nawaf Al Omar described two and a half years of rejected applications and asked directly, "why do foreign workers get preference over us?" (The National), while 24-year-old Hamood Al Nabhani warned starkly that continued joblessness alongside expatriate hiring risked "an undesirable clash between the two communities."

**The Reckoning:** Ghana's older generation built and defended a hierarchical respect system that the younger generation is now visibly testing, precisely because the economic bargain underneath it (defer to elders, inherit their stability eventually) no longer reliably pays out. Oman's younger generation isn't rejecting hierarchy at all — if anything, they're clinging to the most hierarchical, secure option available (government employment) even as policy actively tries to push them elsewhere. One generational conflict is about whether the old deference rules still deserve to hold; the other is about which institution — state or private employer — young people trust enough to defer to in the first place.

The sharper irony: Ghana's youth crisis is unfolding in a comparatively low-hierarchy labor market by regional standards, where young people are pushing back against social hierarchy directly. Oman's youth crisis is unfolding in a heavily state-engineered labor market that is actively trying to reduce a different kind of hierarchy (nationality-based sector protection) and finding its own young citizens don't want that particular door opened — they'd rather wait for the more hierarchical, more secure option on the other side.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Modernghana (personal essay) — Dr. Stephen Gyesaw recounted a specific cross-cultural misread: after a Nigerian colleague bowed in greeting and he responded without reciprocating the gesture, she told a colleague his response felt disrespectful of her values — a formative lesson, he wrote, in "the profound importance of gestures of respect within different cultures," even between two West African contexts.*

> *Expat.com Oman forum — An advisor from Pakistan described the general office atmosphere in strikingly calm terms: "people here, local as well as expats, are very respectful to each other," estimating roughly 80% of workers maintain real work-life balance, with the toughest personal adjustment being simply "the early morning start of a work day."*

> *Expat.com Oman forum (Muscat) — A different, sharper account described a specific manager as abusive and "very racist," using recorded intimidation to discourage HR complaints — a direct contradiction of the "no raised voices" reputation, and a reminder that hierarchy can protect bad actors as easily as it protects order.*

> *The National News — Nawaf Al Omar, a 25-year-old airport-management graduate two and a half years into a fruitless job search, put the generational frustration in the plainest possible terms: "I am getting really frustrated... I feel angry," asking directly why employers seem to prefer cheaper foreign labor over Omani nationals.*

> *The National News — Hamood Al Nabhani, 24, framed the stakes beyond his own job search: "this is not a healthy situation when you have thousands of Omanis jobless while hundreds of thousands of expatriates have jobs," warning that the mismatch risked real community tension if it continued unaddressed.*

### Conclusion

Neither country's younger generation is behaving irrationally, whatever their elders might assume — Ghana's youth are pushing back against a deference system whose old payoff no longer arrives on schedule, and Oman's youth are holding tightly to exactly the kind of institutional security their own government is trying to talk them out of. If you're moving into either workplace, the honest move is to ask who, specifically, feels let down by the current arrangement, and by what promise — because that answer, more than any org chart, is what's actually driving the room.
