# Hungary Brings the Cake, Austria Brings a Calendar: A Guide to Office Social Life

🇭🇺 Hungary · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two offices, a few hundred kilometres apart, run on opposite theories of what a colleague is. In Budapest, a colleague is someone who arrives at your desk with a cake and expects a handshake. In Vienna, a colleague is someone with whom you share a kitchen at agreed hours and a surname until further notice. Eurostat puts the average Hungarian working week at 37.4 hours and the Austrian one at 34.0, which gives Austrians less time in the office and, by most accounts, less inclination to decorate it. For anyone weighing a transfer to either city, the social rituals will matter more than the commute.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Boldog névnapot" and shake hands when a colleague sets out cake on their name day | Treat the occasion as optional, or wait for a calendar invitation that will not come |
| Address colleagues and superiors by title and surname until a relationship is established | Switch to first names on day one because the office looks casual |
| Eat a proper lunch, ideally a canteen meal, between noon and 2 p.m. | Bring a sandwich or salad as your standard lunch and expect it to pass without remark |
| Keep coffee and cigarette breaks to two or three short ones a day | Disappear for long breaks in your first weeks |
| Join team-building outings and the restaurant meal that ends them | Skip the dinner after a paintball afternoon and expect no one to notice |
| Bring something small, such as flowers or good chocolate, when working closely with a celebrant | Arrive empty-handed and say nothing |

### Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "Sie" with your boss and with anyone you do not know, and ask early whether the company uses "du" | Assume that a friendly tone means first names are available |
| Use "Herr" or "Frau" with the surname, and the correct academic title, until invited to stop | Drop the "Mag." or "Dr." because it looks fussy |
| Treat the Kaffeepause as a fixed appointment and take part | Eat at your desk through every break |
| Attend the Christmas party and after-work drinks, and expect colleagues to loosen up | Mistake office reserve in March for the permanent state of affairs |
| Keep small talk to skiing, food and family, and let the host raise business at dinner | Open with a hard sell, or turn a lunch invitation into a negotiation |
| Respect finishing time and do not expect calls or replies long after it | Treat evening messages to colleagues as a sign of commitment |

## Hungary: Cake as Infrastructure

Hungarian office life has a calendar that the Austrian one lacks, and it is printed on a name-day list. According to Hungary Unlocked, the person whose name day it is usually brings the treats to work: a cake, pastries or a box of sweets set out for colleagues to come by, shake hands and say the greeting. Name days are not public holidays, so offices keep normal hours and the ceremony fits around them. The arrangement has an elegant logic. The celebrant pays, everyone attends, and the newcomer who accepts a slice and says the words is understood to have joined the group.

The rest of the working day is more conventional. Expat.com's guide to Budapest describes relations among colleagues as relaxed, with information and jokes shared freely, while communication with superiors tends to be more formal. Bosses may dress casually, the guide notes, but they remain bosses. Lunch lasts between 30 minutes and an hour, usually starting around noon, and business centres often have canteens. The guide is blunt that sandwiches or salads "won't be enough for most." Cigarette and coffee breaks are tolerated when infrequent. Team-building is growing in popularity, through paintball, adventure parks and escape rooms, and it tends to end with two or three courses and a few drinks. The guide was last updated in 2019, so details may have shifted.

Hierarchy and courtesy sit behind all this. Cultural Atlas says Hungarians are often formal and follow the organisation's hierarchy, with decisions and ideas typically coming from the top. People address each other by title and surname, and advanced degrees are worth mentioning. Relationship-building is described as central: meetings open with small talk and questions about family, and trust grows through repeated contact. Expat Arrivals adds that the standard week is about 40 hours, with roughly 21 days of annual leave, and recommends socialising over coffee or lunch. Multinationals, it observes, tend to look more like the United States or Western Europe, so the cake may arrive in a more international form.

One first-person account in Verge Magazine, from a foreigner working in an international Budapest office, sketches the physical setting: open-plan desks, casual dress, a suit a rarity, and English as the business language with Hungarian audible daily. The same author counted at least five weeks in a single year that stretched to six days, because the government assigns Saturday workdays to compensate for midweek holidays. That is a social ritual of a different kind, collective and compulsory, and no cake is provided.

## Austria: Reserve, Scheduled

Austria's version of sociability is real but rationed. Expat.com's guide to Vienna says offices usually start between 8 and 9 a.m., full-time work is 38.5 hours a week, and the day includes a one-hour lunch break. Coffee breaks are a regular fixture, and Viennese colleagues may take several. You address your boss as "Sie". Many Viennese companies have adopted an informal "du" policy in recent years, so the guide advises asking early which applies. Business etiquette is described as generally conservative, and colleagues as often reserved.

Then comes the exception. The same guide says after-work drinks and Christmas parties are celebrated enthusiastically, and that even reserved colleagues may relax noticeably, usually over a Spritzer. Workaround, in a blog post on Austrian office culture, calls the Kaffeepause sacred and says some teams gather in the office kitchen at set times. It adds that meetings may open with five to ten minutes of small talk about skiing, food or family, treated as relationship-building rather than waste. Cultural Atlas takes a stricter view of the boundary: work and private life are strictly separated, business relationships tend to stay formal, and many Austrians do not feel they need a personal relationship before doing business. Friendships can develop over the long term, it says, as long as rapport does not affect business.

The numbers suit the temperament. The OECD reports that Austrians work 1,699 hours a year, against an OECD average of 1,765, and that 9 per cent of employees work very long hours. Eurostat's 34.0-hour average is below the EU figure of 35.9. One French professional who spent about eight years in a Vienna research role wrote that managers were approachable by first name, that Friday afternoons were typically free and that presenteeism was discouraged. Formality of address and informality of hierarchy, it seems, can coexist, which is the kind of paradox Austria handles without comment.

The route into the social life runs through the office all the same. Expat.com's guide to meeting people in Vienna calls work the easiest way to make new friends in the city, because you spend so much time together, and suggests inviting colleagues over or joining them for a drink after work. It also concedes that Viennese can seem grumpy and unapproachable, while adding that most will still help a newcomer and are used to foreigners. The practical reading is that the invitation is genuine but arrives later than a Hungarian one, and that it is best accepted promptly when it does.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two offices handle closeness in opposite ways. Hungary makes it public and sponsored: the cake, the handshake, the long lunch and the dinner after the outing are all occasions on which the group sees you turn up. Austria makes it bounded and scheduled: the Kaffeepause, the end-of-year party and the after-work drink are all occasions with a start, a finish and a drink in hand, and the rest of the time you are left to work. The Hungarian newcomer risks omission, since a missed name day is noticed. The Austrian newcomer risks overreach, since a first name used too early is also noticed.

Hofstede's comparison tool, the single such measure this guide will cite, puts Austria at 11 for power distance against Hungary's 46, and at 63 for indulgence against Hungary's 31. Austria scores 70 for uncertainty avoidance and Hungary 82. Read cautiously, since these are national averages and not office policies, the pattern fits the anecdotes: Austrian rules are fixed but the hierarchy is shallow, while Hungary is more formal about rank and more ritual about hospitality. Hungarians work longer on average, 37.4 hours against 34.0, and have the cake to show for it. Austrians work less, and keep their enthusiasm for December.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a Hungary-based member says colleagues drank before, during and after work, that a pub near the office was full at eight in the morning in winter, and that one coworker hid vodka in a coffee cup. They do not drink and advise caution around driving and machinery.*

> *Expat.com forum — another resident of Hungary describes an after-work life built around neighbours, restaurants, a chess club and theatre rather than drinking, and adds that visiting neighbours can carry political undertones.*

> *Expat.com forum — an Austrian-born contributor says lunch subsidies of up to 8 euros a day, a transit pass worth about 1,000 euros and 14 salaries a year are common, but depend on the employer and are open to negotiation.*

> *Welcome to the Jungle — a French professional in Vienna says managers were approachable by first name, ideas counted regardless of seniority, and people could take up to two days off sick without a doctor's note.*

> *Expat.com forum — a longtime resident says Hungarian pay is low relative to hours, that job adverts often omit salaries, and that newcomers must negotiate and know local living costs.*

## Conclusion

If your idea of belonging is to be fed and greeted, Hungary will oblige, provided you learn the names and the dates and stand up when someone sets out a cake. If your idea of belonging is to be left alone competently and then invited to a party, Austria will oblige, provided you learn the titles and the pronouns and do not mistake February for December. In both cases, ask in the first week what the form of address is, who has a name day, and which Friday the drinks fall on.

Neither office is cold. Each simply hides its warmth in a different drawer, and the new arrival's real task is to find the drawer before the cake runs out.
