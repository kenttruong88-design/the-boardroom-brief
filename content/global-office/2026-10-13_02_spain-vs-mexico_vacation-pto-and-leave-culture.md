**The Country That Shuts Down in August vs. The Country That Just Tripled Its Vacation Law**

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇲🇽 Mexico

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Spain, you will spend your first August unable to find a plumber, a gynaecologist, or your own boss, because the entire country has gone to the beach. In Mexico, you will spend your first year earning twelve vacation days — up from a legally mandated six only a few years ago — while working some of the longest hours in the OECD. Both countries will tell you they know how to live. Only one of them is lying to the labour statistics.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your big personal errands, repairs, and medical visits for any month except August | Try to get a contract signed, a project greenlit, or a tradesperson booked in August — the whole country is at the beach |
| Request vacation weeks ahead and expect it to be granted without much drama — 22+ working days is the legal floor, not a perk | Treat your 22–30 days as negotiable or something to feel guilty about using |
| Embrace the 2–4pm lull as real, if shrinking, downtime in many sectors outside Madrid's international firms | Assume every Spanish office still closes for a three-hour lunch — most urban corporate jobs now run closer to a standard 9-to-6 |
| Dress down — Spanish offices run more casual than their formal reputation suggests | Overdress for your first week; you'll likely be the most buttoned-up person in the room |
| Let dinner plans start at 9 or 10pm without panicking | Expect restaurants to be open, or colleagues to be free, before 9pm |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract's exact vacation accrual — it legally climbs with tenure, starting at just 12 days in year one | Assume US-style "unlimited PTO" norms or 20+ days from day one |
| Expect real warmth around the 2–4pm family lunch block in many companies and smaller cities | Schedule back-to-back meetings straight through the early afternoon without checking local norms first |
| Build slack into your calendar — plans shifting or starting late is normal, not a sign of disrespect | Read a late start or a rescheduled meeting as someone blowing you off |
| Expect a culture of real hustle — Mexican employees post some of the longest average hours in the OECD | Assume the laid-back lunch culture means a laid-back workload overall |
| Learn the Christmas bonus (*aguinaldo*) is legally mandatory, not optional generosity | Treat it as a nice surprise rather than budgeting for it as guaranteed pay |

Spain's vacation culture is, refreshingly, not a culture at all — it is written into labour law as 30 calendar days (22–23 working days) of statutory minimum annual leave, with most employers offering 25 or more via collective agreement, according to Spanish labour-law trackers. The real institution, though, is August itself. ThinkSpain and The Local's long-running expat guides describe it bluntly: "Spain is closed." Tradespeople, doctors, and lawyers alike vanish to the coast, and newcomers learn fast that nothing involving bureaucracy, repairs, or business development should be attempted that month. One Madrid-based intern's account captured the day-to-day texture underneath the stereotype: lunch around 2pm, dinner around 9pm, restaurants shuttered from roughly 4 to 7pm, and nightlife that barely starts before midnight — a schedule, not a vibe.

Mexico's story is the inverse, and far less well known outside the country. The legally mandated vacation minimum was just six days a year until a 2023 labor reform doubled it to twelve, scaling up two additional days per year of tenure after that — a genuinely recent, genuinely large policy shift that most newcomers have no idea happened. Meanwhile, Mexican employees logged an average of 2,124 hours worked in 2021, the highest figure among all OECD member countries (versus 1,767 in the United States, for comparison) according to OECD data reported by the Guadalajara Reporter. The Christmas bonus, or *aguinaldo* — a minimum 15 days' extra pay by law — is one of the few guaranteed financial cushions built into an otherwise lean leave system.

Underneath both systems sits a real cultural throughline: Hofstede Insights scores Mexico at 97 on Indulgence against Spain's comparatively more restrained 44, meaning Mexican culture, on the data, leans harder into enjoying life in the moment — even though its labour law, on paper, offers far less formal time off to do it in. The gratification is daily and social (the 2–4pm family lunch block, the late dinner, the unhurried pace Amy Jones, a five-year Mérida expat interviewed by International Living, described as forcing you to "slow down, to enjoy life, and, most importantly, to live for today"), not annual and legislated.

**The Reckoning**

Here is the real trap for a transferee comparing these two on paper: Spain looks like the "more time off" country, and it is — but its leisure is structural and collective, everyone off in August together, while Mexico's leisure is informal and daily, layered on top of some of the hardest-working annual-hours statistics in the world. Read "Mexico, laid-back culture" and expect European-style time off, and you'll be badly under-prepared for the workload; read "Spain, hardworking economy" and you'll be equally blindsided when your entire client base vanishes for four weeks straight.

The honest read: Spain protects your time off by law and by ritual at once. Mexico protects your lunch and your dignity, via the aguinaldo, while asking far more of your working hours — a trade most relocating professionals don't discover until after they've signed.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local (Spain) — Multiple editions of The Local's long-running expat guide to Spain describe August as a genuine national shutdown rather than a quiet month: tradespeople, doctors, and lawyers are all, as one piece puts it, "at the beach," and newcomers are warned not to expect to get anything bureaucratic or business-related done until September.*

> *International Living — Amy Jones, a Mérida-based expat of five years, said the pace of life in Mexico "forces you to slow down, to enjoy life, and, most importantly, to live for today," adding that in her experience, "life isn't only about making money" the way it often is back home. Fellow expat Linda Laino, in San Miguel de Allende, offered a blunter adjustment tip: plans fall through constantly, so budget for it — "things go wrong, people don't show up."*

> *ExpatExchange — A contributor based in Comitán, Chiapas, described the midday family window as "the slow paced luxury of family time from 2 to 4 on each day," and noted that restaurant culture actively discourages rushing: staff don't hurry diners out after the meal is served.*

> *Absolute Internship (Madrid program blog) — An intern on a Spanish placement admitted their group over-dressed for their first week of work before realizing most offices ran business casual, and found themselves hunting for dinner at 7pm only to discover the entire neighbourhood had closed until 9.*

### Conclusion

If someone's choosing between these two postings based on which country "respects time off" more, the honest answer is: it depends which kind of time off you mean. Spain legislates it and nationalizes it — your 25 days are sacred and so, unofficially, is everyone else's August. Mexico protects the texture of daily life — the lunch, the warmth, the guaranteed bonus — while quietly asking you to log some of the longest hours on the planet to earn it. Neither is the easy option; they're just lenient about different things. Priya's actual advice, over that drink: ask for the real number of vacation days in writing before you ask about anything else, because in one of these countries, the brochure's version of "relaxed" and the law's version of "relaxed" are the same thing, and in the other, they are not even close.
