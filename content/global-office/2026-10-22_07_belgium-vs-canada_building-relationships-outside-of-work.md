**Two Flags, Two Theories of Friendship: Belgium Makes You Wait, Canada Makes You Guess**

🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ninety-three percent of Canadians say they have someone to count on in a time of need, two points above the OECD average; ninety percent of Belgians say the same, one point below it. On paper, this is a rounding error. In practice, it is the difference between a country where people will eventually let you in, and one that lets you in immediately — to the porch, never the house. Relocating for work means re-learning how adults make friends, a skill most of us retired around age twelve. Belgium and Canada each present their own version of this test, and neither grades on a curve.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn at least conversational French or Dutch — the commune-run clubs (dance, sport, music) that locals actually use run in those languages, not English | Expect a work colleague's "we should get a drink" to become a drink within the next calendar month |
| Join something hyper-specific and recurring — a running club, a boules league, a book group — rather than general "expat meetup" events | Mistake politeness or quietness in a meeting for disengagement; Belgians often withhold visible enthusiasm even when on board |
| Accept that the first eighteen months are mostly logistics, not friendship, and plan your social life accordingly | Take it personally when a Belgian friend group that's known each other since primary school is slow to expand |
| Give real advance notice for any gathering — a week, not a day — Belgian calendars fill early | Assume all of Belgium behaves like Brussels; Flanders and Wallonia have distinct social rhythms and language expectations |
| Show sustained, specific interest in one person's life over many small interactions, not one big one | Confuse the international-bubble social scene (fellow expats, EU-adjacent networking) for integration into Belgian life |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat "we should hang out sometime" as a social nicety, not a commitment, unless a date is actually proposed | Mistake friendliness for friendship — warmth at the coffee machine does not imply an invitation is coming |
| Seek out hobby- or identity-based communities (sports leagues, faith groups, cultural associations) over generic networking events | Expect over-the-fence neighbourliness; many Canadians, especially in cities, keep a deliberate social distance from people nearby |
| Be the one to propose the specific plan, with a time and place — Canadians will rarely initiate first | Interpret a lack of follow-up after a pleasant conversation as rejection; it's often just inertia, not avoidance |
| Expect the first real social opening to come through structured activity, not spontaneous encounters | Assume a big city (Toronto, Vancouver) will be easier than a small one; several surveys rank major Canadian cities among the harder places to build a new circle |
| Lean on a newcomer identity or accent as a conversation starter — Canadians frequently respond well to it | Give up after one failed meetup group; attendance is unreliable and most new arrivals cycle through two or three communities before one sticks |

Belgium's reputation for reserve is not folklore; it shows up in the numbers. The OECD's Better Life Index places Belgium's share of people with someone to rely on slightly under the organisation's average, a modest but real gap for a wealthy Western European country (OECD Better Life Index). Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimension data, which rates Belgium relatively high on individualism alongside a notably high uncertainty-avoidance score, helps explain the pattern on the ground: people are self-reliant and risk-averse about letting new people into established routines, which is a different thing from being unfriendly. Brussels Times, reporting on the OECD figures and a 2024 SD Worx work-life balance survey, notes that Belgians rate their overall life satisfaction slightly above the OECD average even as their social-connections score lags — contentment and connection are not the same ledger here (Brussels Times).

The practical explanation, repeated almost verbatim across expat accounts, is proximity: many Belgians never left their hometown's orbit, keep the same friends from school, and simply have less structural need to expand their circle. VRT NWS, republishing an account from a long-term expat, describes Belgians as "a more schedule-loving people" than most newcomers have encountered, with social calendars booked weeks out and little of the home-country habit of same-day plans (VRT NWS). This is not coldness so much as a packed itinerary that newcomers aren't yet on. The fix, per multiple accounts, is patience plus specificity: joining a narrow, recurring activity rather than a broad "meet people" event, and treating the first year as reconnaissance rather than payoff.

Canada inverts the problem. Its social-connections number looks better than Belgium's by a healthy margin — Statistics Canada and the OECD both show high reported levels of someone-to-rely-on — yet "friendly" and "close" turn out to be entirely separate currencies. Hofstede's data puts Canada among the more individualist cultures in the comparison, which tracks with a widely discussed piece in Global Voices arguing that Canadian niceness can function as a well-mannered wall: pleasant, agreeable, and largely impenetrable, with little of the "over-the-fence" casual intimacy newcomers might expect from the stereotype (Global Voices). The warmth is real and also, functionally, a boundary.

What that means for a new arrival is a longer runway between "everyone here is so nice" and an actual standing dinner invitation. The gap is filled, according to the available accounts, by structure rather than chance: sports leagues, hobby groups, professional associations, and faith or cultural communities do the work that casual sociability doesn't. Canada's sheer geography compounds this — a posting in a dense urban core and a posting in a commuter suburb are functionally different social experiments, with several newcomer accounts rating the latter considerably harder.

### The Reckoning

Here is the inversion that should worry anyone packing for either country: Belgium looks closed on paper and opens slowly but genuinely once it does, while Canada looks open on paper and stays pleasantly, permanently ajar. A Belgian friendship, once earned, tends to be loyal and low-maintenance — check-ins might happen only once or twice a year without anyone taking offence. A Canadian acquaintance, by contrast, can stay warm and frequent for years without ever converting into the kind of relationship you'd call at 2 a.m. Both are legitimate social contracts. Neither is explained on the relocation company's welcome call.

The irony compounds at the institutional level. Canada, the Anglophone, immigration-friendly, famously diverse option, statistically out-scores famously communitarian-sounding Belgium on the OECD's own social-support metric — and yet it is Canadians, not Belgians, who get accused by their own commentators of mistaking manners for intimacy. Belgium, meanwhile, scores as the more closed-off culture by the numbers, while delivering what several long-term expats describe as deeper, more durable friendships once admitted. The brochure promise — warm people, easy connections — turns out to be roughly inverted from the lived reality on both sides of the Atlantic.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/expats — One newcomer to Belgium described the culture as "too closed off and introverted," despite genuine effort to connect; the most useful reply in the thread wasn't reassurance but a structural explanation: many Europeans keep the friend group they built before age fifteen for life, and newcomers are competing with two decades of history, not a personality trait.*

> *r/askTO — A Toronto transplant who arrived right before the pandemic described years of near-total social isolation despite a stable job, and found traction only through hyper-specific communities — a tabletop gaming group, a themed Discord server — rather than general "meet new people" events, which consistently underdelivered.*

> *Becoming Brussels (Substack) — A Brussels-based writer described staying inside the international "expat bubble" for years "almost by default, not by choice," since the foreign community there is large enough to fully substitute for local life; the breakthrough came only through a specific shared interest (Belgian politics) that forced sustained contact with actual Belgians rather than other transients.*

> *Expat.com forums — A newcomer to suburban Canada, a year and a half in, reported meeting only clusters of people who had known each other "since pre-k" and found them closed to outsiders by default; the most repeated advice across the thread was to live in a city center rather than a suburb, where social circles form around newer, less entrenched groups.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report — Accounts of Brussels split sharply by approach: those who stayed inside English-language expat circles reported only "nods on the street" from neighbours, while one respondent who learned French specifically to join inexpensive commune-run dance and sports clubs described those groups as the real entry point into Belgian social life.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't friendliness versus coldness; it's two different payment schedules for the same debt. Belgium charges a long, slow interest rate — expect a year or more of being merely tolerated before anything resembling closeness — but the principal, once it matures, tends to be stable and loyal. Canada offers an attractive low introductory rate — everyone is pleasant from week one — with a repayment date on real intimacy that can stretch out indefinitely if you wait for it to come to you instead of structuring it yourself. If your personality needs visible momentum to stay motivated, Canada's warm plateau will frustrate you more than Belgium's cool slope. If you need to feel welcomed before you'll invest, Belgium will test your patience first.

Either way, the mechanism is the same and it is not romantic: join the narrow, recurring, structured thing — the league, the language class, the commune club, the Discord server with actual rules — because neither culture is going to organise your social life for you out of sheer hospitality. If a friend asked me over a drink which one to pick, I'd say: Belgium if you're willing to wait for the real thing, Canada if you can live, indefinitely, on the extremely pleasant version of almost.
