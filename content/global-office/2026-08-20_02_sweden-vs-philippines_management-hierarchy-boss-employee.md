# The Boss Who Won't Act Like One, and the Boss You're Not Allowed to Question

**🇸🇪 Sweden · 🇵🇭 Philippines**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Sweden scores near the bottom of Hofstede Insights' global power distance ranking; the Philippines scores 94, among the highest measured anywhere. In Stockholm, calling your manager by their first name and pushing back on their plan in an open meeting is Tuesday. In Manila, the same move, delivered the same way, can read as a serious breach — one that costs you more socially than professionally. Anyone assuming "hierarchy" means roughly the same thing everywhere is in for two very different first weeks.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings even as the newest or most junior person in the room | Wait to be invited to share your opinion — that invitation may never come explicitly |
| Treat your manager's proposal as a starting point for discussion, not a final decision | Address your boss with excessive formality; first names and flat address are standard |
| Push decisions toward consensus — expect meetings to run longer than you're used to | Expect quick top-down calls; even urgent matters often go through group discussion |
| Take "lagom" seriously: moderate opinions delivered calmly land better than passionate ones | Raise your voice or dramatize disagreement — it reads as a loss of composure, not conviction |
| Trust that silence from your manager after a proposal doesn't mean rejection | Chase your manager for constant approval; autonomy is assumed once a task is assigned |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use respectful terms like "po" and address seniors by title, even if your company is informal in other ways | Publicly question or correct your manager's decision in front of the team |
| Understand that your manager may show concern for your life outside work — that's a relationship, not overreach | Mistake friendliness from a superior for permission to skip the hierarchy on formal matters |
| Deliver disagreement privately, one-on-one, and frame it as a question rather than a correction | Assume a "yes" in a meeting means full agreement — indirect discomfort can look like a yes |
| Recognize that loyalty shown to you (mentorship, flexibility) creates a mutual obligation, or utang na loob | Treat a boss's investment in your career as purely transactional; reciprocity is expected |
| Build rapport through informal chats before and after meetings — it's where trust actually forms | Skip the small talk and go straight to business; it can read as curt or cold |

### Sweden: Management by Absence

Swedish management style is built to make the manager as invisible as possible. Commisceo Global's guide notes Swedish managers act more like coaches than commanders, pushing decision authority down to whoever is closest to the actual work. The Hofstede data underpinning this is stark: Sweden sits among the lowest-power-distance cultures on record, alongside Denmark and Israel, meaning displays of status or authority are actively unwelcome rather than merely unnecessary.

This flatness produces its own kind of stress for a new arrival. Without visible hierarchy to read, it's genuinely hard to know who has authority over what — decisions emerge from long consensus-building discussions (a process sometimes exported internationally as "the Swedish fika-and-meeting model"), and a manager's silence after you propose an idea is not disapproval, it's delegation. The OECD reports Swedes work an average of 1,644 hours a year against an OECD average of 1,776, a number that tracks with a culture that trusts employees to manage their own time without checking in.

### Philippines: Management as Relationship

Filipino corporate culture runs on a completely different operating system. Manila Recruitment's analysis of Filipino management style describes it as paternalistic: bosses are expected to care about employees' whole lives, not just their output, and that care is repaid through what's called utang na loob — a debt of gratitude that shows up at work as loyalty, extra effort, and long tenure. Great Place to Work Philippines has documented active efforts within Philippine companies to dismantle some of this hierarchy in the name of psychological safety, which tells you two things at once: the hierarchy is real, and there's growing local awareness that it can suppress honest feedback.

The practical mechanism is pakikisama — smooth interpersonal relations — which means direct, public criticism of a superior is essentially off the table. Disagreement gets expressed indirectly, often through a trusted intermediary or in a private one-on-one, and reading a Filipino "yes" accurately requires paying attention to tone and context, not just the word itself.

### The Reckoning

The paradox here is that both systems are, in their own way, designed to protect the employee — they just protect different things. Sweden's flatness protects your right to speak; Philippine hierarchy protects your relationship with your boss, which functions as a form of job security and mentorship investment that a flatter system doesn't guarantee. A Swedish manager who moves to Manila and starts soliciting blunt, public pushback from junior staff won't get more honest feedback — they'll get uncomfortable silence, because they've inadvertently asked people to violate a social contract. Conversely, a Filipino manager transferred to Stockholm who waits for staff to defer to their authority will find that authority evaporates the moment they try to use it.

Neither model is more "efficient" in the abstract; each is efficient for the trust structure it was built inside. What changes when you move between them isn't your job description — it's your entire theory of how influence gets exercised.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A professional who relocated to Stockholm from a more hierarchical work culture wrote that the hardest adjustment wasn't the language, it was training herself to stay calm and matter-of-fact in disagreements, since anything that read as emotional intensity got quietly discounted in meetings regardless of how good the underlying point was.*

> *r/askphilippines — One returning balikbayan who'd worked abroad for a decade described re-entering a Manila office and having to relearn that a direct "I disagree" in a meeting, something totally normal at their old job, made their new manager visibly uncomfortable and cost them informal goodwill for weeks.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An engineer who transferred to a Swedish tech company's R&D office noted the biggest surprise wasn't the flat structure itself but how long decisions took to actually land, since nothing moved until enough quiet consensus had built up across the team.*

> *Internations Stockholm — A manager who relocated from a more top-down corporate culture said the thing nobody warned her about was that not being challenged by her Swedish team didn't mean things were going well — it meant she hadn't yet earned enough trust for anyone to bother pushing back.*

> *r/expats — Someone managing a distributed team that included Manila-based staff wrote that the single most useful thing they learned was to solicit disagreement privately over a one-on-one message rather than asking for it in a group call, since almost nobody would volunteer it in front of colleagues, even when asked directly.*

### Conclusion

If you're the one moving, the practical test is this: in Sweden, ask yourself whether you're comfortable exercising authority nobody's going to hand you outright — you'll need to earn it through consensus, not title. In the Philippines, ask whether you're comfortable being trusted with authority you didn't have to earn through consensus, but that comes bundled with an obligation to actually look out for your people, not just manage them.

The honest version I'd tell a friend: neither system is broken, but importing your old one into the new office will make you look either weirdly passive or needlessly aggressive to the people who now report to you — so figure out fast which currency you're now trading in, trust or consensus, and spend accordingly.
