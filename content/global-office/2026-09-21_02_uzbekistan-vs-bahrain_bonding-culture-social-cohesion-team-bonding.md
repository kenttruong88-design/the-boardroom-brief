# The Tea Is Mandatory: Bonding at Work in Uzbekistan and Bahrain

**🇺🇿 Uzbekistan · 🇧🇭 Bahrain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In both Tashkent and Manama, the fastest way to torpedo a working relationship is to decline the tea. Not skip it accidentally — actively decline it, visibly, in front of others. Western newcomers tend to read the ritual as a pleasant preamble to the actual business, something to get through politely before the real agenda starts. In both countries, they have this backwards: the tea is the agenda, and the business that follows is the formality.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇿 Uzbekistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to a colleague's home if one comes — it's a genuine marker of trust extended from the mahalla (neighborhood community) tradition into the workplace, not a formality to dodge | Don't rush the tea-drinking portion of any meeting; sitting and talking before business is the relationship-building step, not a delay of it |
| Learn the basic etiquette of communal eating — sharing from a shared plate at a work lunch is normal and expected, not a hygiene faux pas to work around | Don't decline food or drink offered at a colleague's desk or home without a genuinely good reason; declining reads as rejecting the relationship, not just the snack |
| Defer visibly to older or more senior colleagues in group settings — the aksakal (elder) tradition carries directly into how seniority is treated at work | Don't assume a private, individualistic approach to networking works here; introductions happen through existing relationships and communal events far more than cold outreach |
| Participate in office life-event customs (weddings, births, family gatherings) when invited — these aren't optional extracurriculars, they're where real trust is built | Don't treat team-building as something HR schedules twice a year; the expectation is closer to ongoing, low-key social integration |

#### 🇧🇭 Bahrain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to tea or coffee before any meeting gets down to business — refusing it, even for time pressure, reads as a rebuff | Don't try to fast-forward small talk into the agenda; Bahraini colleagues typically build trust through repeated informal contact before formal collaboration deepens |
| If invited to a majlis (especially during Ramadan evenings), go — it's one of the clearest windows into how business relationships actually form outside the office | Don't assume relationships built at work stay confined to work; managers are expected to know and care about staff on a more personal level than many Western cultures consider standard |
| Join InterNations or a comparable expat network early — Bahrain's compact expat scene means these communities meaningfully accelerate both social and professional integration | Don't mistake Bahrain's cosmopolitan, English-fluent surface for an absence of local relationship norms underneath — hospitality customs still govern how trust is actually built |
| Invest in one-on-one relationship-building with your direct manager specifically — personal rapport with a boss carries outsized weight in how work actually gets assigned and rewarded | Don't treat "quick coffee" invitations as optional networking extras; in a small market, missing them has a real cost to how included you're perceived to be |

### Uzbekistan: The Mahalla Logic, Imported to the Office

Uzbekistan's workplace bonding culture cannot really be separated from the mahalla — the neighborhood-based social structure that has organized Uzbek communal life for generations, and that, per the Embassy of Uzbekistan's own description, exists "to spend good and bad days together." Mahalla dwellers visit each other's homes to share tea and whatever food is available regardless of means, overseen informally by aksakals, respected elders whose authority rests on age and accumulated wisdom rather than title. That logic doesn't stop at the office door. A manager is, in practice, expected to function a little like an aksakal: approachable, generationally respected, invested in staff beyond their output.

The practical effect for a foreign hire is that professional trust in Uzbekistan is built the same way personal trust is — slowly, through repeated informal contact, shared meals, and visible deference to seniority — rather than through discrete, calendared "team-building" events. A newcomer who treats bonding as a checkbox (one happy hour, done) will find colleagues professionally correct but never quite let in. The ones who get invited to a colleague's home for a family occasion have, without necessarily realizing it, been quietly promoted from coworker to something closer to community member.

### Bahrain: Cosmopolitan Surface, Gulf-Traditional Core

Bahrain markets itself, accurately, as the most accessible and cosmopolitan of the Gulf states — English is the default business language, the expat population is enormous relative to the citizen population, and per Commisceo Global's business guide, the workplace blends "traditional values and modern influences." What that blend obscures is how much of actual bonding still runs through distinctly Gulf-traditional channels: the tea ritual as relationship infrastructure, the majlis as a genuine site of professional networking (Gulf News notes Ramadan-evening majlises are specifically used "to consolidate social bonds between various segments of society"), and a managerial style in which personal rapport with subordinates isn't a nice-to-have but a stated expectation.

Because Bahrain's expat community is so large and so well-organized — InterNations runs regular after-work events in Manama specifically because demand for structured social integration is high — a foreign hire can accidentally build an entire social life inside the expat bubble without ever really bonding with Bahraini colleagues. That's a comfortable trap, and a costly one professionally, since so much of how assignments and opportunities flow in Bahraini workplaces depends on the personal-rapport layer that the expat-network shortcut quietly lets you skip.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures reject the idea that bonding is separable from doing business, which puts them on the same side of a line most Western newcomers don't expect. But the mechanisms diverge in a telling way: Uzbekistan's bonding culture is communal and slow-building, rooted in neighborhood and family structures that predate any given job; Bahrain's is more explicitly relational and manager-mediated, built through the specific two-person relationship between you and whoever you report to. Get close to your Uzbek team as a group, and you're in. Get close to your Bahraini manager as an individual, and you're in — the group cohesion tends to follow from that vertical relationship rather than the other way around.

The other irony: Bahrain's large, easy-to-access expat infrastructure is simultaneously the fastest route into a comfortable social life and the easiest way to never actually integrate with local colleagues, while Uzbekistan, with almost no organized expat-network scaffolding to lean on, forces a slower but arguably deeper integration by default, simply because there's no shortcut available.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Responding to "Is Bahrain good for expats?", one contributor described Bahrain's workplace small talk as more extensive than they expected coming from a stricter Western corporate culture, noting that bonding with local colleagues "can lead to heart-to-heart relationships" that go well past typical office friendliness — a warmth they hadn't budgeted time for in their first few weeks.*

> *Quora — An Indian expat answering "What is life like for an Indian expat in Bahrain?" pointed to the availability of Indian cinemas, restaurants, and a temple as easing the adjustment, but noted this made it easy to build a full social circle entirely within the Indian expat community without ever really extending it to Bahraini coworkers — something they only recognized as a gap after a few years in.*

> *Internations Bahrain (via secondary reporting) — Members of the Manama InterNations chapter described the monthly after-work events as the mechanism that got them a close-knit social circle almost immediately after arrival, crediting the network specifically with helping them find housing and friends faster than they expected in a Gulf posting.*

> *Expat Arrivals, Bahrain guide — The guide's contributors flagged that newcomers often underestimate how much unstructured, informal contact (repeated coffees, casual check-ins) is expected before a working relationship is considered established, compared to more transactional norms elsewhere in the Gulf.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway for either posting is the same, even if the choreography differs: budget real time for the parts of the week that don't show up on a calendar invite. In Uzbekistan, that means saying yes to the home visits and the long tea before business. In Bahrain, it means saying yes to the coffee with your manager and, ideally, at least occasionally stepping outside the expat-network comfort zone to build the same rapport with Bahraini colleagues. Skip either, and you'll still get your work done — you just won't ever quite understand why everyone else seems to be getting it done a little more easily than you are.
