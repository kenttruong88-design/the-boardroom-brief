**The Meeting That Never Ends: A Practical Guide to Working in America vs. the United Kingdom**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇬🇧 UK**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An American opens a meeting by naming the decision before lunch. A Brit opens the same meeting by apologising for the weather, then spends forty minutes agreeing to disagree without anyone quite saying so. Both leave the room believing the meeting went well. According to the OECD, the average American worker logs 1,799 hours a year against the average Briton's 1,524 — which raises the obvious question of what, exactly, is filling that extra ten weeks. The answer, this guide will suggest, is meetings that could have been emails, and emails that should have been meetings.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you want in the first thirty seconds — Americans reward people who get to the point | Assume silence in a meeting means agreement; it usually means someone is drafting a rebuttal |
| Use your calendar as a weapon: book 25-minute meetings, not 30, and end on time | Bring up a disagreement privately after the meeting instead of in it — that reads as going behind someone's back |
| Say "great job" out loud, often, even for ordinary work | Undersell your own contributions in a review — modesty is read as low output, not humility |
| Treat your manager as a resource you can interrupt, not an authority you wait on | Expect two weeks' notice on a schedule change to be treated as generous; 48 hours is normal |
| Bring data to back up an opinion — a strong claim with no numbers gets picked apart fast | Assume a "no" is final; Americans routinely reopen decisions if you push with a good case |

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the subtext: "interesting" often means "no," and "with respect" often means "you're wrong" | Take "I'll bear that in mind" as a commitment — it usually means the opposite |
| Deliver criticism privately, wrapped in a compliment, never in the open meeting | Publicly correct a colleague's mistake in front of the team — even gently, it damages the relationship |
| Let trust build over months before expecting candour — colleagues need to like you, not just rate you | Expect an instant, enthusiastic "yes" to a new idea; British consensus takes longer and looks like foot-dragging |
| Match the register: fewer exclamation points, less overt enthusiasm in emails | Skip the small talk before business — jumping straight to the agenda reads as brusque |
| Use humour and self-deprecation to build rapport — "banter" is a genuine trust signal | Mistake politeness for weak opinions; British colleagues disagree constantly, just not out loud |

### The United States: Loud Rooms, Fast Decisions

American meeting culture runs on a theory that speaking is thinking. Silence unsettles a room; a strong opinion, even a wrong one, is preferred to no opinion at all. Workforce.com's analysis of the two cultures notes that American workplaces are comparatively "egalitarian and individualistic," with employees expected to exercise their own judgment rather than wait for sign-off — a habit that produces meetings where junior staff argue with senior staff as a matter of course, not rebellion. Feedback, when it comes, comes in the room: Workforce.com describes American managers correcting mistakes openly, on the theory that public correction prevents recurrence, a practice that would read as a small workplace crisis in London.

The flip side of all that talking is that American decision-making, once made, moves fast. Consultant Will Critchlow, who has spent years managing teams across both countries, observes that Americans "quickly align behind leadership choices" once a call is made — the debate is loud beforehand and largely over afterward. Combined with Hofstede Insights' consistent finding that the US scores among the most individualistic societies measured (a dimension usually cited alongside the UK's own high score, since the two nations sit closer together here than almost anywhere else on the cultural map), the practical result is a workplace that prizes speed and personal ownership over process. The cost, as any American office veteran will admit privately, is a meeting culture where everyone has an opinion and the calendar fills accordingly.

### The United Kingdom: Quiet Rooms, Slow Consensus

British workplace communication runs on what Critchlow calls a "higher-context" style — more is implied than said, and reading between the lines is a basic job skill, not an eccentricity. Commisceo Global's breakdown of British business communication catalogues the mechanism: direct information travels plainly, but anything that risks offence gets wrapped, softened, and delivered as a suggestion rather than an instruction. "I really like Section A, but I wondered if you might make Section B a bit stronger" is a British manager telling you Section B doesn't work.

This produces a meeting culture that looks, to an outsider, indecisive. It is not; it is consensus-building conducted almost entirely off the record. Concerns surface in the corridor after the meeting, not during it — open confrontation is treated as a minor social failure, something that "rocks the boat" in Commisceo's phrasing. Workforce.com's research backs this with a structural note: British employees more often "receive clear directions and approval from their superiors" than their American counterparts, a hierarchy that shows up as reluctance to freelance an opinion without an invitation. None of this means British colleagues lack opinions. It means the opinions arrive privately, later, and usually prefaced with an apology.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that the country with the more hierarchical meeting culture works fewer hours, and the country that prizes speed produces meetings that run long precisely because everyone insists on being heard. An American reading a British "that's interesting" as genuine interest will spend months chasing a project that was quietly killed in the first five minutes. A British colleague reading an American's enthusiastic "love this, let's do it Monday" as a firm commitment will be blindsided when the plan changes by Wednesday — Americans revisit decisions far more casually than the confident tone in the room suggested.

The two countries also diverge sharply on what a meeting is for. In the US, the meeting is where the decision happens. In the UK, the meeting is where the decision is announced, having already been made in a smaller, quieter conversation beforehand — which is why an American who insists on "just discussing it live" in a British office often finds the room strangely resistant to being persuaded on the spot.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *A Guide to British vs American Work (Substack) — An American manager recalled saying "let's table this for now" twelve minutes into her first UK meeting, expecting the topic dropped. Her British colleagues took the word literally in its British sense and launched into a lengthy debate on the very item she'd meant to shelve.*

> *willcritchlow.com — A consultant who has managed teams on both sides of the Atlantic wrote that the hardest adjustment wasn't the accent or the vocabulary, but the trust timeline: Americans extend professional trust based on delivery, while British colleagues withhold real candour until they've decided, often months in, that they actually like you.*

> *Hacker News (Moving to London for a Tech Job thread) — An engineer who relocated from a Bay Area role to a London one described a roughly 25-30% base salary cut on paper, offset by cheaper healthcare and considerably more annual leave — the kind of trade a spreadsheet doesn't fully capture until you've lived both systems.*

> *Experts for Expats — An advisory piece for American transplants warned that UK workplace culture treats visible self-promotion with suspicion, where American offices reward it: the practical advice given was to let finished work speak for itself rather than narrating your own contributions in meetings, a habit that takes most Americans the better part of a year to unlearn.*

### Conclusion

If there is one adjustment that matters more than any other, it's this: an American moving to the UK needs to relearn how to hear a "no," and a Brit moving to the US needs to relearn how to say one. Neither country is being dishonest — they've simply built entire professional grammars around different assumptions about what a meeting is for, and mistaking one grammar for the other is the single most common way an otherwise capable transplant ends up mysteriously unpopular in their first six months. The honest advice, over a drink: in America, talk more than feels natural. In Britain, talk less than feels polite. You'll still get it wrong sometimes, but at least you'll get it wrong for the right reasons.
