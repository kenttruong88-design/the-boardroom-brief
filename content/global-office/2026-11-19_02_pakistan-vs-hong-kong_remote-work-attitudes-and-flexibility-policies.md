# Pakistan Is Told to Work from Anywhere, Hong Kong Is Told to Come In: A Remote Work Reckoning

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Hong Kong, 91 percent of companies were pushing staff to spend more time in the office in Morgan McKinley's 2024 survey, against a global average of 56 percent. In Pakistan, 80.8 percent of the employed work informally, which is a form of flexibility that nobody has ever described as a perk. One country is arguing about how many days to commute, and the other is arguing about whether the router will survive the afternoon.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer in writing what the remote policy is, since no federal policy exists | Assume a verbal promise of flexibility will survive a change of manager |
| Budget for a backup internet connection, a UPS or generator, and a coworking space | Schedule a client call on the assumption that the power and the connection will both hold |
| Agree measurable outputs and deadlines up front, as managers tend to trust what they can count | Switch your camera off and hope that silence reads as productivity |
| Take specialist advice on how foreign income is taxed, since official guidance is thin | Leave foreign earnings undeclared on the theory that nobody is looking |
| Keep contracts with overseas clients in writing, with payment terms and a named jurisdiction | Rely on a handshake with a client in a time zone that cannot be sued |

### Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get hybrid terms in the offer letter, since there is no statutory right to request flexible work | Assume the pandemic arrangement survived at your new employer |
| Ask exactly how many office days are expected and whether they are fixed | Plan a flat in Sai Kung on the strength of a casual conversation |
| Take the commute seriously, as most employers still prize in-person collaboration | Treat the office as optional in the first year, when you have not yet earned the goodwill |
| Clear any work from another jurisdiction with HR first, given tax and immigration consequences | Work from your home country for a month and tell no one |
| Confirm data and security rules before using a personal laptop | Forward client files to a personal email account |

## Pakistan

Remote work in Pakistan is, at first glance, an export success story and, at second glance, an engineering problem. A Pakistan Labour Force Survey for 2024-25, reported by The News, found 80.8 percent of the employed in informal work and only 19.2 percent in formal jobs, which means the typical Pakistani worker has never been offered a hybrid policy because there is no contract to attach it to. The same reporting suggests that platform work remains largely physical, with 97.1 percent of platform workers doing ride-hailing, courier and delivery jobs and only 2.9 percent doing fully digital remote work, even as commentators cite more than two million freelancers behind the country's digital exports. A Dawn analysis cited a study finding that just 13.47 percent of jobs in Pakistan are telecommutable, so the remote debate is conducted about a small slice of the workforce.

The formal side is thinly regulated. According to the International Bar Association, Pakistan has no clear legal definition of freelance or gig work, so remote workers serving American and European clients may not know whether they count as freelancers, the self-employed or employees, with consequences for social security, taxes and disputes. A PIDE policy brief based on interviews with Islamabad officials found no standardised federal work-from-home policy, only directives issued during crises, along with mistrust that surfaced as missed meetings and cameras left off. Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at 70 on uncertainty avoidance against Hong Kong's 29, and the supervisory reflex in the brief, which is to check the file and distrust the screen, is consistent with that number.

Infrastructure is the other half of the story. Geo News reported in August 2024 that internet slowdowns had cut speeds by 30 to 40 percent by one executive's estimate, while the industry body P@SHA warned that the cumulative economic damage could reach $300 million. The better employers adapt. Dawn reported that Careem went remote-first, with staff allowed to work from anywhere in their hiring country, and abroad for up to 60 days a year, while the software firm Integry pays for a coworking space if staff prefer not to work at home. These are real examples, though they are also the sort of employer that reads blog posts about work-life balance.

Pay matters here too. The Labour Force Survey reported by The News put average monthly wages for paid employees at Rs39,042 in 2024-25, up 62 percent from 2020-21 in nominal terms, though high inflation limited the real gain. A foreign contract paid in dollars therefore looks very good from Karachi, and one reason the freelancer's working day is so much more exposed to a throttled connection than the employee's.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong has the opposite problem, which is abundant bandwidth and a persistent belief that work happens where one can be seen doing it. According to Clyde & Co, employees have no statutory right to request flexible or hybrid working, which is left to employer policy and contract, and Census and Statistics Department figures indicate that about 290,000 employees, or 10.6 percent of those in the non-government sector, have flexible work arrangements. Employers cite in-person collaboration, client service, cybersecurity, data protection and cross-border compliance. Hofstede Insights rates Hong Kong at 68 on power distance, which has historically been a polite way of saying the boss is in the building.

The employees, unsurprisingly, want something else. A 2026 Morgan McKinley survey cited by Clyde & Co found that 70 percent of Hong Kong professionals rank working from home as their top benefit and 64 percent say flexibility affects whether they accept a job. The firm's earlier survey, reported by Bloomberg in 2024, found that 80 percent of Hong Kong employees prefer to stay hybrid or remote, with 40 percent willing to forgo a pay rise for it, even as nine in ten local companies pushed for more office time. A PwC survey of 1,043 employees, cited by the South China Morning Post, found that nine in ten Hong Kong employees preferred remote work but only 45 percent had that choice.

The practical consequence for an incoming expat is that flexibility is a negotiated benefit rather than an entitlement, and borders add complications. Clyde & Co notes that Hong Kong taxes only income derived in the territory, that remote work from elsewhere can raise tax and immigration questions, and that employment income is typically apportioned by days actually worked in each jurisdiction. The digital nomad fantasy, in short, comes with a form.

The same SCMP feature quoted a freelance marketer who works remotely from abroad, earning more than HK$30,000 a month on four or five hours a day, which is the sort of testimonial that makes salaried colleagues stare at the ceiling. It also cited Randstad's finding that two in five Hongkongers aged 18 to 25 had quit a job over insufficient flexibility in location or hours, so the employer's calculation is no longer free. The resulting equilibrium, for the moment, is a compromise in which the office is mandatory on certain days and negotiable on others.

## The Reckoning

The two countries offer opposite versions of the same trade. Hong Kong has dependable infrastructure, a clear legal framework and employers who largely say no to remote work, while Pakistan has employers who may well say yes and a power and connectivity environment that can say no for them. A worker in Hong Kong negotiates a hybrid arrangement against a manager's preference. A worker in Pakistan negotiates it against a grid, a policy gap and, for freelancers, a payment gateway.

For attitudes, the data suggests that neither side has quite caught up with its workforce. Hong Kong employees are asking for flexibility in survey after survey and are mostly being answered with a calendar of office days. Pakistan's formal employers are experimenting, the best of them with notable success, but the system around them has not yet decided what a remote worker is. An expat in either place should assume the written policy and the lived practice differ, and that the person to ask is whoever last tried to use it.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Dawn Business — an anonymous employee at a well-regarded Pakistani tech company said remote work had stretched unofficial hours to as many as twelve a day and hurt their social life. Senior managers were unsatisfied even after targets were met, and the employee was looking to switch jobs.*

> *Geo News — a Pakistani freelancer of ten years said his team, which serves Amazon clients over remote desktop, lost about 30 percent of business within a week of the slowdown. Tasks that once took 15 to 20 minutes took six or seven hours, and work moved to India and Bangladesh.*

> *PIDE policy brief — an Islamabad public sector official summed up remote work in the civil service by saying that the work had vanished while the from home part remained, a line that explains the monitoring problem better than a table of KPIs.*

> *South China Morning Post — a Hong Kong senior manager at a multinational services firm said she sometimes works from home but would rather come to the office to keep a boundary between work and private life, which the pandemic had blurred.*

> *Hacker News — a Pakistan-based consultant with more than twenty years of experience advertised on the monthly freelancer thread as a remote worker in the GMT+5 time zone seeking freelance or fractional roles. The listing is an advertisement rather than a complaint, and it quietly states the time zone as the product.*

## Conclusion

If you want a remote career from Pakistan, the real constraints are power, connectivity and clients, so bring redundancy, a written contract and a good accountant. If you want an office job in Hong Kong, the real constraints are the office days written into the offer letter, so negotiate them before you sign and not after you land.

Neither country has solved flexible work, but only one of them has the broadband to make the argument interesting.
