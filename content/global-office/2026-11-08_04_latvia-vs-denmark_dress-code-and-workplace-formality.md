# The Blazer You Packed Is Doing Nothing For You in Copenhagen, and Everything For You in Riga

🇱🇻 Latvia · 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Latvian HR manager and a Danish one were, in a thought experiment it is useful to actually run, asked to describe their ideal new hire's first-day outfit. The Latvian said "pressed, dark, and obviously trying." The Dane said "whatever," and meant it as the highest compliment available. Both countries are small, cold, EU-integrated, and allergic to small talk about money — and yet one of them will silently judge your shoes and the other will silently judge you for caring what anyone thinks about your shoes. This is the part the relocation packet leaves as a footnote, and it is the part that actually determines whether your first team meeting goes well.

## Do's and Don'ts: Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a structured blazer or suit to any client-facing meeting, even internal ones early on | Show up in sneakers and a hoodie to a first meeting with management — it reads as not taking the job seriously |
| Keep colors conservative — navy, grey, black — especially in Riga's financial and legal sectors | Over-accessorize; understated is read as competent, flashy is read as unserious |
| Dress up gradually as trust builds, rather than relaxing too fast | Assume startup culture means no dress code — Latvian tech firms still skew smarter than their Western European counterparts |
| Iron your shirt. Genuinely. It is noticed. | Mistake Baltic reserve for coldness — it's formality, not hostility |
| Match your formality to your counterpart's seniority, not your own comfort | Wear beachwear-adjacent clothing even in a casual Friday context; "casual" in Riga still means slacks |

## Do's and Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear clean, well-fitting casual clothes — jeans and a good sweater are standard even for managers | Wear a suit to a normal meeting unless you're client-facing in banking or law — it can read as compensating |
| Let your competence, not your outfit, do the talking | Comment on a colleague's expensive watch or bag — conspicuous status symbols sit badly with Janteloven norms |
| Save genuine formality for client pitches, interviews, and press events | Assume "casual" means low-effort — Danes dress deliberately minimalist, not sloppily |
| Default to muted Scandinavian palettes: black, grey, navy, the occasional statement coat | Over-dress for internal meetings; it can unintentionally signal distrust of the team |
| Ask what colleagues are wearing before a big event if unsure — directness is welcomed | Expect a dress code memo; Danish workplaces assume adults will calibrate themselves |

## Latvia: The Visible Hierarchy

Latvia's post-Soviet corporate culture never fully shed the idea that appearance signals seriousness, and two decades of EU membership plus heavy Nordic and German investment hardened that instinct rather than loosening it, since those investors also skew formal in the Baltics' eyes. Business etiquette guides aimed at incoming staff, including the widely cited overview at businessculture.org, describe Latvian offices as places where dress still functions as a proxy for competence, particularly in Riga's banking, legal, and consulting sectors, where a visiting EU auditor and a local associate are expected to be visually indistinguishable in formality.

Talesmag's real post reports from Riga — written by diplomatic spouses and staff rather than marketing copy — repeatedly note that Latvians read sloppiness as disrespect, not as relaxed confidence, a detail that trips up expats arriving from more casual Western markets who assume "small EU country" means "informal EU country." It does not. The OECD's broader country profile for Latvia notes a labor market still working through a generational handoff between Soviet-era managers and EU-trained younger staff, and dress code is one of the few places where that generational line is visibly drawn: older managers dress up, and newer staff dress up to be taken seriously by them.

None of this means Latvian offices are humorless. It means the humor arrives after the blazer, not instead of it.

Job interviews amplify all of this considerably. Recruitment guidance circulated by Latvian business schools and staffing agencies routinely tells candidates to overdress rather than risk reading as careless, on the logic that a slightly-too-formal candidate is forgivable while a slightly-too-casual one is not. The same logic persists once hired: a new employee's first few weeks are, in effect, an extended interview, and the wardrobe stays formal until the office has independently decided the person is competent enough to be trusted with their own judgment about what to wear.

## Denmark: Formality as a Red Flag

Denmark inverts the logic entirely, and the inversion is not an accident of climate — it is downstream of Janteloven, the informal Scandinavian social code against standing out or signaling superiority, which jobindex.dk and multiple Danish career sites cite directly when explaining why a tailored suit in a non-client-facing meeting can make colleagues uneasy rather than impressed. The assumption in a Danish office, per How to Live in Denmark's business etiquette coverage, is that everyone present is a competent adult who does not need a uniform to prove it, and dressing visibly "above" the room reads as either insecurity or an implicit claim to be more important than your colleagues — both socially costly in a flat, consensus-driven workplace.

The OECD and Danish labor ministry data on workplace structure consistently show Denmark near the top of European flat-hierarchy measures, and the dress code is simply the most visible expression of that flatness: if title doesn't buy you deference, neither does tailoring. Hofstede Insights' comparative data situates Denmark among the most consensus-oriented, status-averse cultures measured globally, a single-digit score on the masculinity dimension that tracks closely with how little workplace status is performed through appearance at all. The one place this cracks is genuinely external-facing work — investment banking, corporate law, EU affairs — where a suit reappears, deployed deliberately, like a costume for a specific scene rather than a daily uniform.

This has a practical side effect that catches foreign managers off guard: dressing up to seem more authoritative in a Danish office can backfire into the opposite of the intended effect. The-intl.com's reporting on Danish dress-code norms describes new foreign hires who kept wearing suits well past their first month being quietly perceived as less approachable, not more credible — the outfit read as a wall rather than a credential. The correction, when it comes, tends to arrive socially rather than through any formal policy, which is itself a very Danish way of handling it.

## The Reckoning

Put a Latvian associate and a Danish one in the same room and the Latvian will, within the first ninety seconds, have made a reasonably accurate guess about the Dane's seniority based on body language and vocabulary alone, because the outfit gives nothing away — and the Dane will have made an equally confident, equally wrong guess about the Latvian's personality based on the suit, assuming someone dressed that carefully must be either anxious or self-important. Neither read is really about clothing. Both are about what each culture has decided clothing is for: in Latvia, a visible signal of respect for the occasion and the people in the room; in Denmark, a visible signal that you don't think you need to signal anything, which is itself a very carefully calibrated signal.

The practical upshot for anyone relocating is that "formality" is not a single sliding scale you can split the difference on. Overdressing in Copenhagen doesn't read as neutral-safe, the way it might in Riga — it reads as a specific social error. Underdressing in Riga doesn't read as relaxed-confident, the way it might in Denmark — it reads as not having done the homework. You cannot average the two approaches into a photogenic beige middle ground; you have to actually pack two different wardrobes, and more importantly, two different theories of what your clothes are supposed to be doing for you.

There is also a time dimension worth planning around. In Latvia, formality is a starting condition you earn your way out of gradually, as colleagues come to know you; the trajectory runs from dressed-up to relaxed. In Denmark, informality is the starting condition, and any shift toward formality has to be justified by an external occasion — a client, a pitch, a press appearance — rather than earned through tenure. Arriving with the wrong trajectory in mind is a more common mistake than arriving with the wrong clothes.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Riga — paraphrased experience: A diplomatic spouse recalls being quietly pulled aside after wearing jeans to a semi-formal work reception, not out of malice but because colleagues genuinely worried she hadn't understood the occasion's importance.*

> *Expat Exchange, Culture Shock in Latvia thread — paraphrased experience: A contributor describes Latvian colleagues as warm once trust is built, but notes the "proving period" is visibly marked by how carefully newcomers dress before anyone relaxes around them.*

> *Reddit r/Denmark — paraphrased experience: A commenter who moved from a more hierarchical European office recalls being told, gently, that their suit was "making people nervous," and that dressing down actually made them more approachable to junior colleagues.*

> *How to Live in Denmark, business etiquette series — paraphrased experience: A contributor notes that foreign managers who keep dressing formally after the first month are often quietly assumed to be job-hunting elsewhere, since nobody confident in their current role bothers.*

## Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for a posting, the dress code is a reasonably reliable proxy for everything else: Latvia will ask you to earn informality over time, Denmark will hand it to you on day one and judge you for not using it correctly. Pack the blazer for Riga and wear it without irony. Pack the blazer for Copenhagen too, but leave it in the closet unless someone's paying you specifically to wear it — and even then, don't enjoy it too visibly.
