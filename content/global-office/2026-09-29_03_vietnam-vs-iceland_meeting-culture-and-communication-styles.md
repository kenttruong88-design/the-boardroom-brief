# In Vietnam, the Meeting Is Where Nothing Gets Decided. In Iceland, the Meeting Is Where Everything Does.

**🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇮🇸 Iceland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

One long-time foreign resident of Vietnam describes still "putting his foot in it at least a couple times a month" after years of trying to read what his Vietnamese colleagues and family aren't saying out loud. A British transplant to Iceland describes a different, opposite discomfort: genuine stomach-level anxiety at how bluntly Icelanders make requests, absent even a word for "please." Between a culture where meaning lives between the lines and one where it's stated flatly on them, the two countries offer a near-perfect natural experiment in what "good communication" even means — and neither definition is wrong, they're just incompatible with each other by design.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build the personal relationship before pushing business — trust precedes the deal, not the reverse | Don't expect a meeting to end in a decision; most calls are made afterward, at the top, then handed down |
| Read silence, hedging, and "let me get back to you" as real signals, not stalling | Don't publicly criticize or visibly lose your temper — it costs the other person face and damages the relationship |
| Expect meetings to run unhurried; rushing the agenda reads as disrespect for the relationship | Don't mistake politeness for agreement — a gentle "we'll consider it" can be a soft no |
| Frame new ideas gently and let consensus build slowly among stakeholders | Don't push for a fast, contract-locked commitment; Vietnamese business culture weighs one's word over paperwork |
| Show up in person for anything that matters — virtual-only relationship-building is treated as insufficient | Don't assume a junior colleague's quiet agreement in a meeting reflects real buy-in |

#### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you mean plainly — directness is read as honesty and accountability, not rudeness | Don't soften every request with elaborate politeness; over-qualifying can read as evasive |
| Use first names with everyone, including senior leadership — the hierarchy is genuinely flat | Don't expect to need a translator for tone; Icelandic professional register runs through word choice, not formal pronouns |
| Expect direct access to leadership — CEOs routinely deal straight with lower-level managers | Don't assume flat structure means no accountability; punctuality and follow-through are taken seriously |
| Bring your point to the meeting itself — that's where real discussion and decisions happen | Don't be alarmed by brief, unpadded replies; a short answer isn't a cold one |
| Adjust your expectations around small pleasantries — thanking a bus driver, over-apologizing, and similar habits aren't the local norm | Don't read the absence of "please" as impoliteness; the language itself doesn't have a direct equivalent |

### Vietnam: Reading the Room Is the Whole Job

Vietnamese workplace communication runs on what researchers call high-context signaling — meaning is inferred from relationship, tone, and circumstance rather than stated outright, and getting this wrong is the single most common expat misstep documented across business guides and personal accounts alike. Commisceo Global's Vietnam management guide is blunt about the mechanics: decisions are overwhelmingly top-down, "most decisions are made at the top and then given to the employees to implement," and managers are expected to act paternalistically, protecting the reputation of anyone who raises an idea rather than critiquing it head-on in the room.

Face-saving isn't a soft cultural nicety here — it's operational. Public criticism or visible anger, per the same guide, actively jeopardizes working relationships, which is why disagreement tends to surface as gentle qualification rather than direct pushback. Le Tran Law's employee-relations analysis adds a structural note: Vietnam's labor culture still carries the imprint of a risk-averse, consensus-building state-enterprise tradition, meaning organizational change moves slowly and requires demonstrated group benefit before anyone signs on. One long-term foreign resident's own account captures the emotional cost of adapting to this from a low-context starting point: even a neutral factual comment can land as an accusation, and true fluency in reading the unsaid takes years, not months.

Meetings themselves are consequently less a decision-making venue than a relationship-maintenance one — the real decisions often happen elsewhere, afterward, among people who weren't all in the room.

### Iceland: The Meeting Is the Decision

Iceland runs close to the opposite model. BusinessCulture.org describes Icelandic professional communication as "relatively direct," built around "straight talking, punctuality, accountability and honesty" as explicit cultural values rather than aspirational ones. Formal address exists in the language — *Herra*, *Frú*, *Ungfrú* — but per the same source is "very rarely used," even in schools; first names dominate business interaction top to bottom, phone directories included.

The structural flatness compounds the directness. Icelandic companies are described as "streamlined," with CEOs maintaining direct contact with managers well below them on any org chart — a genuinely classless approach to internal communication that would be unusual even in famously flat Nordic peers. One British long-term resident's account captures how disorienting this can feel from the outside: the Icelandic language has no dedicated word for "please," and requests that land as simple directness to a local can register as rudeness to a visitor from a more heavily hedged politeness culture — he describes it as an intellectual understanding that never quite catches up with the gut-level discomfort.

The upshot for meetings specifically: because hierarchy carries little weight and bluntness is the professional register, the meeting itself tends to be where things actually get decided — junior voices are heard, disagreement is stated rather than implied, and there's comparatively little of the "real conversation happens afterward" pattern that defines high-context cultures.

### The Reckoning

Put a Vietnamese team and an Icelandic team on the same video call and the miscommunication writes itself: the Icelandic side reads Vietnamese hedging as lack of commitment or unclear thinking; the Vietnamese side reads Icelandic bluntness as disrespectful or even aggressive. Neither read is correct, and neither is really about the words being used — it's about where each culture has decided meaning should live. Hofstede's power-distance dimension makes the structural gap legible in a single number: Vietnam scores among the higher end globally for hierarchy-acceptance, while Iceland scores among the lowest, a gap wide enough that an org chart which functions perfectly in Reykjavik would be read as chaotic or disrespectful in Hanoi, and vice versa.

The practical translation for anyone managing across both: in Vietnam, invest the unglamorous time in relationship-building before you need anything, because that's where your actual leverage lives. In Iceland, say the actual thing in the actual meeting, because no one is coming afterward with the real answer — the meeting was the real answer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Substack, "An Expat's Thoughts on Life" (jdpearce)* — A long-term Western resident of Vietnam, married into a Vietnamese family, described repeatedly misreading high-context signals even after years of practice — a passing comment about a missing item during a visit was read as an accusation rather than an observation. He puts his foot in it "at least a couple times a month," he wrote, and recommends anyone relocating study high-context versus low-context communication seriously before arriving, since it's a more fundamental adjustment than language itself.

> *Medium — Matt McKenna, "Four Things I'm Still Not Used to About Iceland"* — A British long-term resident described genuine physical discomfort at Icelandic directness: the language "has no word for 'please,'" and requests delivered without a politeness marker still trigger what he calls "a weird feeling in my stomach," years into living there — despite fully understanding, intellectually, that no rudeness is intended.

> *Commisceo Global Vietnam management guide (practitioner-sourced business etiquette account)* — Foreign managers are warned explicitly that rushing a Vietnamese meeting to a fast conclusion is counterproductive: "meetings should be unhurried," and attempting to force pace "risks losing trust" — patience isn't a virtue here so much as a precondition for the deal happening at all.

> *BusinessCulture.org Iceland communication guide* — The source notes a detail that surprises most newcomers more than any single stat: Icelandic phone directories list people by first name, not surname, reflecting a business culture where "Icelanders often call each other by their first name even when doing business" — a small fact that tells you more about the country's flat hierarchy than an org chart ever could.

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Vietnam, the practical skill to build before you land isn't language fluency so much as patience with ambiguity — learn to treat a soft "we'll consider it" as data, not stalling, and invest in the relationship long before you need anything from it. If you're heading to Iceland, the skill is closer to the opposite: say the direct thing, in the meeting, without three layers of hedging, and don't mistake bluntness for a personal slight. The honest version I'd give a friend over a drink: in Hanoi, the real meeting happens after the meeting; in Reykjavik, there is no after — so don't leave the room without saying what you actually meant.
