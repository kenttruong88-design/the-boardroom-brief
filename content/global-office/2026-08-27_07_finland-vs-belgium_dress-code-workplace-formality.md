# How to Dress for a Meeting in a Country That Doesn't Believe in Meetings, and One That Believes in Them Deeply

🇫🇮 Finland · 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Helsinki, a chief financial officer can show up to a board presentation in a fleece and nobody blinks, because the fleece is Finnish-made, technically excellent, and utterly without ego. In Brussels, arrive at a similar meeting in anything less than a pressed jacket and a colleague will quietly wonder whether you have accepted the seriousness of the occasion, or of Belgium. Both countries are wealthy, orderly, and unglamorous by design. Only one of them thinks a well-cut suit is doing something useful.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear clean, practical layers — quality outerwear reads as competence | Overdress for a first meeting; it can be read as trying too hard |
| Save the suit for client-facing finance, law, or government roles | Assume casual dress means casual expectations of the work itself |
| Accept a sauna invitation if a senior colleague extends one | Decline the sauna outright — it's a genuine extension of the meeting |
| Keep jewelry, cologne, and flourish minimal | Wear anything flashy meant to signal status or seniority |
| Dress for the weather first, the meeting second | Expect written guidance — most offices have no formal dress policy at all |

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark suit to any first meeting, regardless of your role | Show up in what you'd wear to a Helsinki client call |
| Invest in tailoring — fit is noticed more than brand | Treat "business casual" as an invitation to denim |
| Match formality to the more conservative counterpart in the room | Assume a relaxed Flemish tech office sets the norm for Wallonia, or vice versa |
| Keep client-facing attire polished even on internal-only days | Loosen up simply because no one is visiting the office that day |
| Ask what "smart casual" means locally before testing it | Read a friendly office as a license to under-dress at client sites |

---

Finnish workplace formality is best understood as an absence rather than a rule: there usually isn't one. Business dress "is usually flexible, so people can dress in a way that makes them feel good," notes a Finland-focused business etiquette guide, and the wider culture backs that up — Finland scores just 33 on Hofstede Insights' Power Distance index, among the lowest in Europe, reflecting a society where hierarchy is treated as a convenience rather than a costume ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/belgium,finland/)). That egalitarianism shows up in the data too: Statistics Finland found 35 percent of wage earners worked remotely in 2023, and access to flexible hours runs as high as 85 percent among state employees. When nobody is reliably in the building, and nobody outranks anybody by much, a dress code becomes hard to enforce and pointless to write down.

What Finland does police, quietly, is competence-signaling. Overdressing reads as arrogance, not effort — a genuinely counterintuitive penalty for anyone arriving from a suit-and-tie economy. And the famous Finnish aversion to small talk in the office pairs with an equally famous willingness to keep negotiating in a sauna, towel and all, which is either the most honest form of business casual on the continent or a stress test disguised as hospitality (MyHelsinki; Planet Depos).

Belgium runs on the opposite assumption: that formality is itself a form of respect, and skipping it costs you credibility before you've said anything. Belgium's Power Distance score sits at 65, roughly double Finland's, meaning hierarchy is expected and superiors are often genuinely harder to reach ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/belgium,finland/)). Dark suits and ties remain the default for client-facing and senior roles, and even Belgium's tech and creative sectors — where guides concede dress "may be more relaxed" — advise erring formal for a first meeting (World Business Culture; ClickUp). Statbel puts 2024 telework rates at 33 percent of employees, roughly the EU norm and well below Finland's, so more people are in the office, dressed, more often ([Statbel](https://www.eurostat.org)).

Belgium also complicates itself internally. Flemish workplaces trend toward flatter, more consultative management, while Walloon offices lean into stricter hierarchy and title-consciousness, meaning the "correct" formality in Antwerp and Liège isn't identical (HousingAnywhere). A newcomer who calibrates to one region and transfers unchanged to the other will misjudge the room.

**The Reckoning.** The two countries essentially invert each other's logic. Finland treats visible formality as a mild character flaw; Belgium treats its absence as one. Finland's OECD-adjacent work-life data shows a culture that guards personal time fiercely even as it lets appearance slide, while Belgium's Better Life Index figures — only 4 percent of employees working very long hours, well under the OECD average of 10 percent — show a country that protects the same boundary while insisting you look sharp on the way out the door (OECD Better Life Index). Formality and overwork are not, it turns out, the same axis; Belgium proves you can dress like you never leave and still leave on time.

The practical trap is assuming either country's dress code is really about clothes. In Finland, it's about not claiming status you haven't earned through actual competence. In Belgium, it's about signaling that you take the room, and the people in it, seriously before a word is spoken. Pack the fleece for Helsinki. Pack the tailor's number for Brussels.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *A Quora respondent describing Finnish offices noted that Finns will accept you however you're dressed and judge you little by your appearance, which took longer to internalize than expected after years in more image-conscious workplaces.*

> *A contributor on a diplomatic-spouse forum covering Brussels postings warned that "formal" on a Belgian invitation can mean full black tie, not business attire, and that misreading the difference at a single event was more embarrassing than any workday mistake.*

> *An expat guide to Belgian business culture flagged that the same "business casual" phrase means different things in Flanders and Wallonia, and that new arrivals who copied their Antwerp office's relaxed norms into a Walloon client meeting visibly misjudged the room.*

> *One relocation account from Helsinki described the disorientation of being told there was simply no dress code to learn, and having to instead reverse-engineer the unwritten line between "comfortable" and "unkempt" by watching what senior colleagues actually wore.*

> *An HR-focused Belgian business etiquette write-up advised new hires to dress slightly more formally than they think necessary for the first several weeks, since the cost of underdressing lingers with colleagues far longer than the mild awkwardness of overdressing.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Belgium to Finland, spend the money you'd have spent on suits on better boots instead — nobody is grading your jacket, and everyone will notice if you're unprepared for the weather. If you're moving from Finland to Belgium, buy one properly tailored suit before you land, because the alternative is spending your first month being quietly recalibrated by a room that already decided something about you before your first sentence. Both systems are internally consistent; neither is more "professional" than the other, just optimized for a different kind of trust. Dress for the country you're in, not the one you left — Finland will forgive the wardrobe; Belgium will remember it.
