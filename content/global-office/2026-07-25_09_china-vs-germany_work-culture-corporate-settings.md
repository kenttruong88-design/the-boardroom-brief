**China Made the 72-Hour Workweek Illegal and Kept Working It Anyway; Germany Made "Going Home on Time" a National Identity**

**🇨🇳 China · 🇩🇪 Germany**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The average German worker logs about 1,330 hours a year, fewer than any country the OECD tracks. The average Chinese worker, three years after the country's top court explicitly declared the notorious "996" schedule — 9am to 9pm, six days a week — illegal, still logs roughly 48.5 hours a week, well above the legal 44-hour cap. One country wrote work-life balance into law and largely lives it. The other wrote overwork out of law and is still, gradually and unevenly, catching up to its own ruling.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real, if uneven, change — major firms like Midea and DJI now enforce earlier clock-out times as a compliance signal | Assume 996 is fully gone just because it's illegal; average hours (48.5/week) still exceed the legal 44-hour cap |
| Ask directly about actual office hours during interviews, not just stated policy | Take a job posting's "flexible hours" language at face value — it's sometimes a loophole workaround for overtime rules |
| Watch for rebranded overtime — "big week/small week" schedules are a known way companies sidestep 996 enforcement | Assume enforcement is uniform across the country; local labour bureaus vary in how strictly they apply the rules |
| Recognize public compliance moves (early office closures) as a genuine cultural shift underway, even if incomplete | Expect immediate parity with Western hours; the shift is real but still in progress as of 2026 |
| Note that overtime pay is legally required above the 44-hour threshold | Assume unpaid overtime is simply how things work — it's a labor law violation, even if still common in practice |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Practice Feierabend — leaving at your scheduled time and genuinely disconnecting after hours | Send or expect work messages after hours; it's culturally discouraged and often seen as poor boundary-setting |
| Expect 25-30 days of paid vacation as standard — plan to actually take it | Treat unused vacation days as a badge of dedication; they're seen as an entitlement to use, not hoard |
| Be punctual without exception — lateness reads as a lack of professionalism, not a minor inconvenience | Run over on meeting time or miss a stated deadline without clear communication in advance |
| Bring precise, well-prepared input to meetings — efficiency is prized over improvisation | Expect long, exploratory meetings; German meetings tend to be concise and outcome-focused |
| Separate work and personal time cleanly, even in flexible or remote arrangements | Assume "flexibility" means always-on availability — it more often means structured autonomy within firm boundaries |

### China: The Law Changed Before the Culture Did

China's Supreme People's Court declared the 996 schedule an explicit labor law violation in August 2021, reaffirming a legal cap of 44 hours a week with monthly overtime capped at 36 hours, according to [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/china-work-culture-996-crackdown-70-hour-week-shift-125060400581_1.html). Enforcement genuinely intensified through 2025 and into 2026 — major employers including Tencent, Midea, DJI, and Haier have publicly adopted earlier clock-out times as visible compliance signals, a shift covered in detail by [MSAdvisory](https://msadvisory.com/working-hours-china/).

But the gap between ruling and reality remains wide. Average working hours in China still sit around 48.5 hours a week — above the legal ceiling — with enforcement described as sporadic and unevenly applied across regions and local labour bureaus, and with some companies simply rebranding 996 into euphemisms like "big week/small week" scheduling to route around the letter of the law, according to the same reporting. The practical takeaway for anyone joining a Chinese firm: the legal environment has fundamentally shifted, but the lived culture — the assumption that visible long hours signal commitment — is still catching up to its own legislation.

### Germany: The Boundary Is the Point

German corporate culture runs on a near-opposite premise: efficiency within defined hours, not hours as the measure of commitment. The concept of Feierabend — literally "celebration evening," culturally meaning a clean, guilt-free end to the workday — is deeply embedded, with employees expected to disconnect fully after leaving the office, according to [Amatum's guide to German work-life balance](https://amatum.com/germany-work-culture-balance/). Most German companies offer 25 to 30 days of paid vacation annually, a figure treated as a baseline entitlement to actually use, not a number to quietly forfeit, per [NNRoad](https://nnroad.com/blog/german-work-culture/).

This isn't slack — it's a different theory of productivity. German workplace culture emphasizes precision, punctuality, and disciplined time management specifically so that work gets done within standard hours rather than spilling past them, according to [Edstellar's 2026 guide](https://www.edstellar.com/blog/germany-work-culture). Germany's roughly 1,330 average annual work hours — the lowest among OECD-tracked countries, per [Visual Capitalist](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/annual-working-hours-in-countries-2023/) — isn't read domestically as underwork; it's read as evidence the system is functioning as designed.

### The Reckoning

The two countries offer a genuinely useful natural experiment in what actually changes workplace overwork: law alone, or law plus deep cultural buy-in. China legislated against 996 with real teeth — court rulings, enforcement pressure, and visible corporate compliance moves — and is still watching average hours sit meaningfully above the legal cap years later. Germany didn't need a dramatic legal crackdown on overwork because the cultural default — Feierabend, real vacation use, disconnection after hours — was already doing most of the enforcement work informally, with labor law reinforcing rather than fighting the prevailing norm.

Hofstede Insights scores China at 87 on long-term orientation versus Germany's 83 — both high, both patient — but the two countries channel that patience very differently at the office: China's long-term orientation has historically expressed itself as sustained personal sacrifice for collective or company success, while Germany's expresses itself as sustained structural discipline that protects, rather than consumes, personal time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — A manager who moved from a Chinese tech firm to a German subsidiary described the shift as disorienting in the opposite direction from what they expected — not that Germans worked less hard during the day, but that assuming anyone would still be online after 6pm was treated as a mild social error.*

> *Quora — Someone who transferred within the same multinational from its Shanghai office to its Frankfurt office wrote that vacation days in China were nominally generous on paper but rarely fully used, while in Germany taking the full 30 days was simply expected, and not doing so raised questions from HR.*

> *TeamBlind — An employee asking whether a manager could still enforce a 996-style schedule despite the legal ban received replies describing "big week, small week" rotations as a common workaround still in active use at several major firms as of the last year.*

> *Quora — A German professional who'd worked in both countries said the real difference wasn't hours worked but what silence after 6pm signaled — in Germany it meant a healthy boundary, while in a Chinese office it could be read, rightly or wrongly, as a lack of dedication.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing work ethics across the two countries noted that German colleagues were process-oriented to the point of rigidity, valuing a methodical, structured approach that made hours predictable — a contrast they found calming after years navigating a Chinese office culture that treated schedule volatility as normal.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to China, ask specific, direct questions about actual office hours before accepting an offer — the law has changed, the culture is changing more slowly, and the gap between them is exactly where you don't want to get caught. If you're heading to Germany, take Feierabend at face value: leaving on time isn't a signal of low commitment, it's the entire operating system. The honest comparison, over a drink, is that Germany built a culture that enforces its own labor law by habit, while China is still finding out whether a court ruling can out-argue a workplace norm that took decades to set.
