# 30 Days of Guilt vs. A Whole Month of Shutdown: Germany and Spain Take Vacation Differently

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇪🇸 Spain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Germans take, on average, more vacation days than almost anyone in Europe — around 31 a year by recent Federal Statistical Office figures — and a striking 84% of them still report feeling vacation-deprived, with younger workers apologizing to colleagues for requesting time off. Spain, meanwhile, doesn't so much schedule vacation as let an entire month quietly stop functioning: roughly one in five companies close outright in August, and nobody apologizes for any of it. Germany has more days and more guilt. Spain has fewer legally mandated days and considerably less shame about using every one of them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your full allocation — 20 statutory days minimum, often 28–30 in practice | Assume more generous PTO automatically means less guilt about using it |
| Block out at least one two-week stretch — it's a genuine cultural and practical norm | Apologize excessively for time off, even if younger colleagues around you do |
| Plan vacation requests early — the process is often formal and calendar-driven | Assume unlimited flexibility — most companies track and expect advance notice |
| Fully disconnect while away — checking email on vacation is not admired here | Let a heavier Gen Z guilt culture around handoffs become your default expectation |
| Use the six weeks of fully paid sick leave if you need it — it's a real, separate entitlement | Confuse sick leave with vacation days — they're tracked and treated differently |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around August — treat it as a structural closure, not an inconvenience | Expect responsive service, deliveries, or bureaucracy resolved during peak August weeks |
| Use your full 30 calendar days (23 working days) — it's a strong legal entitlement | Try to carry unused vacation days past December 31 — Spanish law generally won't allow it |
| Expect "jornada intensiva" (shortened summer workdays) in many sectors | Assume every business closes — larger chains and multinational offices usually stay open |
| Ask early which weeks your specific office or team takes off | Schedule critical deadlines or major renovations for August — nothing moves quickly |
| Enjoy the four-day "puente" long weekends that form around midweek public holidays | Expect payment in lieu of unused vacation while still employed — it's generally not permitted |

### Germany: More Days, More Anxiety

Germany's vacation entitlement looks generous by almost any measure: a statutory minimum of 20 days for a five-day work week, but Deel and multiple HR guides note the real-world average sits closer to 28–30 days once company policy is factored in, with the Federal Statistical Office recording an average of 31.0 days actually taken in 2023. And yet a widely cited Expedia-commissioned survey found 84% of German workers report feeling vacation-deprived — a notably higher share than American workers who receive far fewer days to begin with.

The generational split is the real story here: 47% of German Gen Z employees say they feel bad about colleagues covering their workload while they're away, compared to just 16% of baby boomers, and Gen Z employees are roughly three times as likely to feel they need to apologize when requesting time off. A Blind thread on "handling sick leave guilt" captures the same dynamic playing out around time-off requests generally — a culture that legally protects rest more thoroughly than almost anywhere else, while a rising generation of workers increasingly second-guesses using it. Germany didn't design a guilt culture around vacation. It seems to have imported one, even with the legal infrastructure actively working against it.

### Spain: The Month That Simply Stops

Spain's approach runs on a completely different mechanism: rather than individual negotiation over when to take leave, an entire structural period — August — collectively shuts large parts of the country down, no apology, no guilt, no individual negotiation required. How2Spanish and multiple expat guides describe "el gran parón" (the great pause) as shaped by climate, school calendars that run from late June to early September, and deeply embedded work-culture habits, particularly in construction and manufacturing sectors that either close entirely for weeks or switch to shortened "jornada intensiva" workdays.

The legal backbone is genuinely strong: Spain's Workers' Statute guarantees 30 calendar days (23 working days) of paid leave annually — more generous on paper than Germany's statutory floor — plus up to 14 public holidays layered with the uniquely Spanish "puente" practice of bridging a midweek holiday into a four-day weekend. Roughly 18% of Spanish companies close outright in August according to recent estimates, which surprises visitors from the UK or US but registers as entirely unremarkable domestically — a GB News feature on expat cultural adjustment specifically singled out August opening hours as the single strangest adjustment newcomers report, ahead of language or bureaucracy.

### The Reckoning

The paradox worth sitting with: Germany's vacation culture is legally robust and individually negotiated, which theoretically gives workers maximum control — and that same individual negotiation is exactly where the guilt creeps in, disproportionately for younger employees who feel personally responsible for the workload gap their absence creates. Spain's vacation culture removes that individual negotiation almost entirely for a full month a year — nobody personally decides to disappoint colleagues by taking August off, because everyone is taking August off — and that collective, structural quality appears to be exactly what spares it from the guilt spiral visible in German data. More individual flexibility, in this specific case, produced more anxiety. Less individual choice produced less.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A poster in a thread about handling time-off guilt described the specific discomfort of watching younger German colleagues visibly over-prepare handoff documents and apologize repeatedly before a two-week vacation that their contract, and the law, fully entitled them to take without justification.*

> *Quora — Answering whether Spanish offices close in August the way Italian ones do, a respondent confirmed the pattern was real but uneven — large multinational offices generally stayed open, while smaller, family-run, and construction-sector businesses closed outright or ran drastically reduced hours for weeks.*

> *Germany Is The Wurst (expat newsletter) — A longtime foreign resident described the disorienting first experience of a German colleague firmly declining to answer a "quick work question" while on vacation, framing it not as rudeness but as the writer's own imported assumption that availability equals professionalism.*

> *Quora — Someone listing the hardest adjustments to living in Spain as an expat put August closures near the top, describing the specific frustration of needing a repair done in mid-August and being told, without much apparent concern, to simply wait until September.*

> *GB News (expat interview) — An expat living in Spain named the shift in opening hours and August's near-total pause as the single strangest cultural adjustment of the move, more disorienting even than the language barrier, because it required rethinking the assumption that "closed" is ever a temporary, individual inconvenience rather than a shared, seasonal norm.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Germany, use every one of your vacation days without apologizing for it — the law is on your side even when workplace culture quietly isn't. If you're moving to Spain, stop fighting August and start planning around it; nothing you need done urgently that month is actually going to move, and that's by design, not dysfunction. The honest version for a friend: Germany will give you the days and make you feel bad about spending them; Spain will just close the whole country and spare you the decision entirely. I know which one sounds more relaxing, but ask a German Gen Z employee before you assume it's obvious.
