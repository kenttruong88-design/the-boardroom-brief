# Czechia Nods Politely and Does Otherwise; Taiwan Stays Silent and Stays Late

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇹🇼 Taiwan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two meeting rooms, one unspoken agreement: nobody will tell the boss what they actually think. In Czechia, nearly half of employees do not give their managers critical feedback directly, according to a 2026 employee barometer. In Taiwan, the government's own guidance to newcomers advises against criticising colleagues in team meetings at all. The method differs. Czechs tend to agree in the room and quietly diverge afterwards, while the Taiwanese tend to preserve the harmony of the room and put the disagreement somewhere it cannot embarrass anyone. The newcomer's task is to work out which kind of yes is on offer.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time or early; being a few minutes late every day gets noticed | Treat the start time as a rough suggestion |
| Use formal greetings until a colleague invites first names | Open with first names and a hug |
| Get agreements in writing; verbal commitments are not considered binding | Assume a nod in the room settles the matter |
| Present views without forcing them; Czech managers respond poorly to aggressive, fast-talking styles | Run a hard-sell meeting |
| Ask politely whether the group can switch to English if the room has drifted into Czech | Assume the drift is hostile; one commentator puts about 90 percent of cases down to thoughtlessness |
| Leave around 5 pm and eat lunch with colleagues | Stay late to prove dedication or schedule Friday afternoon meetings |

### Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the most senior person first and defer to them in meetings | Treat a hierarchy as a suggestion |
| Praise colleagues publicly when it is earned | Criticise a colleague in a team meeting, even constructively |
| Move a written criticism to an in-person conversation quickly | Assume an email message means what it appears to say |
| Build trust before proposing improvements to a manager | Offer unsolicited improvements in your first weeks |
| Protect face, your own and other people's, in tone and wording | Point out a mistake in front of others |
| Learn when you may leave; ask what is actually expected | Assume the stated hours are the real hours |

## Czechia: Yes, and Then Something Else

The Up Benefity 2026 Employee Barometer, reported by Expats.cz, supplies the main figures. Nearly half of Czech employees do not give their bosses critical feedback directly. Among those who hold back, nearly three in ten say honesty has backfired on them before. Nearly half say they sometimes adjust the truth at work to avoid conflict, about 40 percent say management sometimes filters information to suit its own interests, and only about one in ten trusts management almost completely. The same article links the pattern to a hierarchical tradition, including a communist-era style in which avoiding mistakes counted for more than open feedback.

The practical consequence for a foreign colleague is a meeting that feels agreeable and then produces something different. Expats.cz guidance on dealing with Czech bosses warns to expect indirect answers, with decisions often reached after the meeting through a systematic process, and to make requests in advance rather than appearing unannounced. An older BusinessCulture.org guide, dated 2013 and so worth reading with caution, notes that the host typically prepares and presents the agenda, that verbal commitments are not considered binding and that general agreement should be put in writing. A separate 2005 Expats.cz essay describes foreign managers pleasantly surprised by how fast staff carry out instructions, then finding a month or two later that the work differed from what was agreed.

Czech workers also seem to keep their hours under control. Our World in Data figures, compiled on Wikipedia, put Czech annual hours per worker at 1,788 in 2023, and the guidance for newcomers is that staying late is not read as dedication, since Czechs tend to separate work from home and usually leave around 5 pm. A quieter risk is linguistic. Expats.cz reports that meetings in traditional Czech companies can drift into Czech, leaving foreign staff excluded; a communication consultant quoted there estimates that about 90 percent of the time it is thoughtlessness, not intent. Younger, tech-focused workplaces, it adds, tend to run in English. The same outlet notes that a Journal of World Business study found leaders from the United States and Western Europe were viewed more favourably than equally qualified leaders from Czechia, Poland and Albania, which may explain why a Czech employee greets an imported manager's request for candour with polite scepticism. It also advises against scheduling anything on a Friday afternoon, when many colleagues are already unreachable.

## Taiwan: The Silence Is Doing a Lot of Work

Taiwan's own government is unusually candid. Its Gold Card programme describes Taiwan as a non-confrontational culture, advises avoiding criticism of colleagues in team meetings, since others may read it as an attempt to embarrass them, and says criticism often arrives by text or email, so it is wise to arrange an in-person conversation quickly. It adds that suggesting improvements to a line manager early in a working relationship can come across as arrogance. Seniority carries real weight. Expat Arrivals reports that senior managers in many businesses expect to be consulted on key decisions, and that colleagues may avoid pointing out mistakes to protect face, even when this costs the company.

Academic work on East Asian meetings makes a related point. The Conversation notes that quieter East Asian staff are often read as disengaged when they may be showing respect or preparing a considered answer, and that disagreement can appear as silence or lack of enthusiasm, not as an objection. Fast, overlapping brainstorming, a Western default, can sideline exactly the people who have something useful to say.

The hours are the part the brochures tend to mention in the third paragraph. Our World in Data figures compiled on Wikipedia give Taiwan 2,032 hours per worker in 2023, about 244 more than the Czech figure of 1,788, which works out to roughly six extra weeks of full-time work a year. A decade-long American resident of Taiwan writes that leaving at 5.30 was read as a poor attitude, that unpaid overtime is common and that bosses may expect emails on weekends. The same writer reports six make-up Saturdays in 2023 and says remote work remains rare at traditional companies. The Taiwanese office, in short, runs on harmony, hierarchy and a floor of availability.

## The Reckoning

Compared directly, the Czech office is shorter on hours and long on reservations, while the Taiwanese office is long on hours and long on reservations. A Czech meeting ends with polite agreement and a private doubt; a Taiwanese meeting ends with silence and a private doubt. In both cases the real discussion happens somewhere else, and the newcomer who reads the room too literally ends up with a plan nobody intends to follow. The Czech version is the more tolerant of a direct question, particularly in younger, English-speaking companies. The Taiwanese version is more forgiving of nothing at all.

The difference that matters for a mover is the cost of speaking up. In Czechia, the Up Benefity data suggests it is mostly a matter of trust, and international managers may well expect directness that older workplaces did not reward. In Taiwan, challenging a senior person early can be read as arrogance and damage a relationship that is built on face. Czechia lets you ask for English and go home at five. Taiwan lets you ask for nothing, and then goes on asking you for more.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Foreigners in Taiwan blog — an American who worked in Taiwan for ten years learned the hard way that speaking freely to superiors can end a career at a traditional company, that attitude and attention to detail mattered as much as results, and that his boss expected work to be finished during vacation.*

> *NYCU Global MBA student blog — a Polish MBA student liked the friendliness of Taiwanese colleagues but found the courteous pleasantries and long email exchanges inefficient, and missed the directness and precision he associated with European offices.*

> *Taiwan Scene — an expat woman with almost two decades in Taiwan describes offices as indirect and high-context, where you must read the atmosphere, know whom to approach and judge how to voice criticism, and says problems eased as she moved into more skilled roles and gained control of her schedule.*

> *Expats.cz — a feature on surviving a Czech office reports that colleagues seem reserved at first but warm up, tend to voice irritation openly rather than feign friendliness, and that harassment and sexist humour are tolerated more than in the United States.*

> *Expats.cz employee voices — staff at a Prague employer describe leaders who give quick praise after a difficult call and colleagues who talk through hard situations together, though this is a flattering employer profile, not a neutral survey.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, decide which kind of unspoken rule you handle better. Czechia rewards patience, written follow-up and a willingness to ask for English; it punishes the assumption that a nod in the room is a commitment. Taiwan rewards deference, relationships and judgement about when to speak; it punishes early, public disagreement and the assumption that the contracted hours are the actual ones. In both, confirm in writing and learn who really decides. Ask colleagues you trust, after the meeting and away from the table, what was actually decided. In Prague that conversation happens over lunch, possibly with a beer; in Taipei it happens one-to-one, once enough trust exists to make it possible. Either way, the minutes are the least reliable record in the building.

Prague will let you leave at five having understood nothing. Taipei will keep you until you have understood everything, including what you were never going to be told.
