# "Senhor" and "Oga": Two Ways of Never Questioning the Boss

**🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇳🇬 Nigeria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nigeria scores 80 on Hofstede's power distance index; Portugal scores 63. Both numbers describe societies where the boss's decision is the decision, full stop — the difference is texture, not degree. In Nigeria, the deference has a name, "Oga," and a set of physical gestures (a nod, a slight bow) built around it. In Portugal, the deference is quieter and wrapped in formal address — "Senhor" plus surname — but the underlying instruction to a new arrival is nearly identical: do not mistake your manager's warmth for an invitation to disagree with them in front of others.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors as "Senhor/Senhora" plus surname until told otherwise | Switch to first names quickly, even if colleagues use yours |
| Raise disagreement privately, after the meeting, not during it | Challenge a manager's decision in front of the team |
| Expect a paternalistic streak — managers may ask about your wellbeing genuinely | Mistake personal warmth from a boss for permission to be informal in decisions |
| Trust that experience and tenure, not just title, drive whose opinion carries weight | Assume a flat-sounding conversation means a flat decision-making process |
| Watch for the startup exception — younger companies are visibly flatter | Apply traditional-corporate hierarchy assumptions to every Portuguese employer |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use appropriate titles ("Oga," "Sir," "Ma") consistently, especially early on | Default to first names with a senior colleague or manager |
| Bring concerns to your manager privately, framed respectfully | Publicly question a decision in a group setting, even a good-faith one |
| Recognize that age and seniority carry independent authority, not just title | Assume a younger person in a senior role has the same standing as an older one |
| Expect decisions to move top-down with limited consultation | Expect to be looped into strategic decisions at your level as a norm |
| Read the "hustle" ethos as compatible with hierarchy, not a contradiction of it | Assume entrepreneurial energy at the individual level means a flat org chart |

---

Portugal's hierarchy runs on inherited formality more than raw command. Commisceo Global's management guide describes Portuguese managers as expected to combine "an authoritative approach with a concern for the well-being and dignity of employees" — a genuinely paternalistic model where being above someone in the chain doesn't license poor treatment, but does mean that person's judgment isn't meant to be second-guessed. World Business Culture is more direct: subordinates aren't expected to be consulted on decisions because supervisors are assumed to have earned their position through experience, and inserting your opinion uninvited reads as questioning that earned authority rather than as helpful collaboration. The formality is visible in address itself — "Senhor" or "Senhora" plus surname is standard until a senior colleague explicitly signals otherwise — and in communication style, which leans indirect enough that a direct American-style "I disagree" can land as confrontational even when it's meant constructively.

Nigeria's hierarchy is both older and more explicitly acknowledged as a cultural artifact — Businessday NG's own Hofstede analysis places the country at 80 on power distance, a score the outlet frames candidly as reflecting "the supremacy of hierarchies" as an accepted social fact, not a corporate quirk. The term "Oga" (from Yoruba, roughly "boss" or "master") functions as both an honorific and a genuine descriptor of how decisions actually get made: research cited in workplace studies describes Nigerian managers as functioning like a "benevolent autocrat" — subordinates receive instructions rather than co-author decisions, and academic work on the topic notes that high power distance can make it genuinely difficult for employees to push back even against clearly unreasonable treatment, since challenging a superior carries real social cost. Respect gestures — a slight bow, specific greetings for elders — extend the same logic from family and community life directly into the office, meaning the deference a new arrival sees on the street is not a separate register from what's expected in a Monday morning meeting.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive contrast is where the softness sits. Portugal's hierarchy is wrapped in personal warmth — managers who ask about your family, who take a paternal interest — which can lull a newcomer into thinking the relationship is more negotiable than it is; the warmth is real, but it does not translate into decision-making input. Nigeria's hierarchy is wrapped in more visible formality and title-consciousness, which can make it look colder from the outside, but the "hustle" culture running underneath it — the widely-noted entrepreneurial energy at the individual level — means ambition and hierarchy coexist without contradiction in a way that might confuse someone expecting flat structures to correlate with dynamism. In both countries, the mistake an outsider makes is the same one, just triggered by different signals: reading informality (Portuguese warmth, Nigerian entrepreneurial hustle) as evidence the org chart is softer than it actually is.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response describing workplace cultural traits to consider in Nigeria noted that titles like "Oga" function less as a formality and more as an ongoing, real-time signal of where you stand relative to a superior — getting it wrong, even accidentally, registers immediately.*

> *Quora — One respondent to a question about elder-respect customs in Nigerian culture listed physical gestures — a nod, a slight bow, sometimes more depending on region — as standard workplace behavior toward older colleagues regardless of formal job title, meaning a younger person's authority on paper doesn't override age-based deference in daily interaction.*

> *expatinportugal.substack.com — A newcomer's account of adjusting to Portuguese office life described the private, after-the-fact style of raising disagreement as the hardest habit to build, having come from a culture where speaking up in the room was considered the transparent, honest thing to do.*

> *Expatica Portugal business guide (aggregated contributor commentary) — A recurring observation among expats was that Portuguese managers' indirect communication style could initially read as a lack of feedback altogether, until newcomers learned to listen for softer, qualified phrasing as the actual substance of a critique.*

> *Quora — A thread on Nigerian workplace dynamics noted that a foreign boss and a wealthy Nigerian boss are often perceived and treated quite differently by staff, with local hierarchical expectations sometimes applied more intensely toward Nigerian superiors than toward expatriate ones — a nuance that cuts against the assumption that hierarchy applies uniformly regardless of who's on top.*

### Conclusion

The practical move in Portugal is to treat warmth and authority as two separate channels: enjoy the former, don't test the latter — save your disagreement for a private, respectful follow-up conversation, never the room. In Nigeria, the practical move is to treat titles and gestures as functioning information, not just etiquette — get "Oga," "Sir," and "Ma" right early, and understand that age can outrank title in ways your home country's org chart never prepared you for. Neither system is more restrictive than the other, just differently disguised — the honest thing I'd tell a friend headed to either country: figure out fast whether the hierarchy you're seeing is dressed in warmth or dressed in formality, because either way, it's still the hierarchy.
