# Uganda vs. Australia: One Culture Apologizes for Being Early, the Other Apologizes for Nothing

🇺🇬 Uganda · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Kampala, arriving on time to a meeting marks you as either a foreigner or someone with nothing better to do, and the room will wait for the boss regardless of what the invite said. In Sydney, arriving on time marks you as competent, and the meeting will start without the boss if the boss is running late, because nobody there believes hierarchy is a valid excuse for wasting other people's Tuesday. Both countries call this "professionalism." Only one of them means it in minutes.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇬 Uganda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually with a handshake before business begins, even in a group of twelve | Walk in and start talking agenda items cold — it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Address senior colleagues by title and surname until invited to do otherwise | Assume first-name informality is available on day one |
| Build in 30-60 minutes of buffer around any meeting's stated start time | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming each will start on schedule |
| Route disagreement through a senior colleague or a private aside | Contradict a superior in open meeting, even when you're certain you're right |
| Expect consensus-building to take multiple sittings before a decision lands | Push for a same-meeting yes/no on anything consequential |
| Dress formally and take the meeting seriously as a social occasion, not just a work task | Treat the small talk at the start as filler you can skip |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time, or a few minutes early, and expect the meeting to start on the dot | Wander in fifteen minutes late assuming seniority buys you slack |
| Say what you mean plainly, including disagreement, framed around the work not the person | Wrap criticism in so much diplomatic padding that nobody can find it |
| Use first names with everyone, including your manager's manager | Default to titles and surnames — it reads as stiff or try-hard |
| Open with brief small talk, often sport or weekend plans, then get to the point | Launch straight into business with zero warm-up, or drag the small talk out |
| Speak up with your own view even if it differs from the room | Stay silent assuming someone more senior should go first |
| Come with a clear ask and confirm next steps and an owner before the meeting ends | Leave a meeting without agreed actions, assuming it'll sort itself out |

### Uganda: The Meeting as Social Contract

Uganda's workplaces run on a hierarchy that would make a Habsburg blush, and meetings are where that hierarchy gets its liturgy performed. According to the Uganda Bureau of Statistics, the country's labour market remains dominated by informal and own-use employment — the 2021 National Labour Force Survey counted roughly half of working Ugandans in own-use production rather than formal wage jobs — which means the polished conference-room meeting that expats picture is, for most of the working population, a fairly rare and self-consciously formal event, not the ambient wallpaper of daily work it is elsewhere. When it does happen, it opens with handshakes, inquiries after one's family, and a period of relationship-tending that a visiting consultant might clock as delay and a Ugandan colleague would clock as due diligence.

Time, in this context, behaves less like a fixed grid and more like a suggestion with social exceptions. Meetings often lack a written agenda, decisions accrue through consensus rather than a single decisive vote, and — this is the part that trips up newcomers hardest — the more senior the person, the more forgivable their lateness. A junior staffer who strolls in forty minutes late causes a minor scandal; a director who does the same is simply demonstrating that their time is, in fact, worth more. It is not chaos. It is a coherent status system that happens to run on a different clock than the one on your phone.

### Australia: The Meeting as Efficiency Audit

Australia inverts nearly every one of those defaults. Its workplaces score among the most individualistic in Hofstede Insights' country comparisons, and that individualism shows up in meetings as a low tolerance for both excessive formality and excessive hierarchy — junior staff are expected to challenge a bad idea from a senior colleague, not defer to it, and managers who lean on status rather than argument tend to lose the room. The Australian Bureau of Statistics' 2024 Time Use Survey found full-time workers spending close to eight and three-quarter hours a day on employment-related activities, and a workplace culture this allergic to wasted time treats an unstructured meeting as a small moral failing on the part of whoever called it.

That efficiency instinct, though, runs into the same wall every efficient culture eventually hits: Harvard Business Review's research on meeting behavior has repeatedly found that most professionals consider a large share of the meetings they attend unnecessary, and Australian offices are not exempt just because they start on time. What Australia has solved is punctuality and directness — feedback lands as a comment on the work rather than an attack on the person, and nobody assumes silence means agreement. What it has not solved, any more than anywhere else has, is the basic human tendency to schedule a meeting to avoid making a decision by email.

### The Reckoning

Put a Ugandan and an Australian in the same call and the friction shows up almost immediately, and almost never where either side expects it. The Australian will read the Ugandan's opening pleasantries as a stall tactic and start subtly checking the clock; the Ugandan will read the Australian's fast pivot to business as a faint insult, evidence that this counterparty doesn't think the relationship is worth thirty seconds of small talk. Neither is wrong by their own rules. Both are, by the other's rules, being slightly rude without knowing it.

The counterintuitive part is which culture actually decides faster once the topic turns serious. Uganda's consensus-driven meetings look slow — multiple sittings, layers of hierarchy, nothing signed off on the spot — but the resulting agreement tends to be durable, because everyone with the standing to unpick it later was in the room and nodded. Australia's meetings look fast — agenda, discussion, decision, action items assigned before the coffee's cold — but the flat, everyone's-opinion-counts format can produce a "decision" that quietly unravels the moment someone senior who wasn't consulted weighs back in. Speed and durability, it turns out, are not the same metric, and each country has optimized for one at the expense of the other.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a professional who relocated to Kampala for a new job described the disorientation of being told a meeting started at 10am and learning, only after sitting alone in the conference room until nearly 11, that the invite time was aspirational rather than binding.*

> *Expat.com forum — a contributor on the Uganda forum's business etiquette thread noted that skipping the handshake-and-small-talk ritual to "save time" at the start of a meeting was noticed and quietly held against them for weeks afterward.*

> *Quora — a respondent on a thread about culture shocks in Australia described the relief of realizing that blunt feedback in a meeting wasn't a prelude to being fired, just how Australians critique a slide deck, and that colleagues who argued hardest in the room often got along fine at lunch.*

> *LinkedIn Pulse — an expat commentator on Australian work culture wrote about the pleasant surprise of watching a junior hire challenge a director's proposal in an open meeting and the director simply say "fair point," with no visible loss of face on either side.*

> *Quora — a foreigner working in Kampala advised that the single most useful adjustment was mentally reclassifying "the meeting" as beginning at arrival plus an hour, rather than treating the stated start time as real and feeling perpetually aggrieved.*

### Conclusion

Neither approach is more "professional" than the other; they're optimized for different failure modes. Uganda's meeting culture guards against decisions nobody actually agreed to and relationships nobody bothered to build. Australia's guards against wasted hours and unspoken disagreement curdling into resentment. A team working across both would do well to set explicit start-time expectations in writing (so "10am" means the same thing to everyone invited) and to agree, separately, on how much relationship-building precedes the agenda item — because assuming your own country's defaults are simply "how meetings work" is the fastest way to insult someone while running precisely on schedule.
