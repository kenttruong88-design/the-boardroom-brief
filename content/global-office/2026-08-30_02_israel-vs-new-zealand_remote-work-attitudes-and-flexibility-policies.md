# In Israel, Remote Work Gets Interrupted by the Army. In New Zealand, It Doesn't Get Interrupted — It Just Never Fully Existed.

🇮🇱 Israel · 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two small, export-oriented economies, two theories of "flexible work" that share almost nothing. In Israel, 65-70% of companies have spent two years pulling staff back to the office even as a meaningful share of the workforce can vanish for weeks on military reserve duty — a scheduling problem no return-to-office memo addresses. In New Zealand, there is no legal right to work from home at all, yet a third of the country quietly does some work there anyway, protected less by statute than by a national temperament that treats a 5 p.m. finish as civic virtue. Both call this "flexibility." They do not mean the same thing.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the work week to run Sunday–Thursday, with Friday a half-day before Shabbat | Don't schedule anything urgent for Friday afternoon through Saturday |
| Accept that "hybrid" usually means Tuesday–Thursday in the office | Don't assume a fully remote offer is stable — under 10% of tech roles are, and shrinking |
| Build slack into project timelines for reserve duty (miluim) call-ups | Don't treat a colleague's sudden two-week disappearance as a performance issue |
| Expect direct, informal, occasionally blunt communication even on video calls | Don't mistake bluntness for hostility — it isn't |
| Ask about home-office equipment and internet stipends explicitly | Don't assume remote-work terms are legally standardized — there's no comprehensive statute |

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit flexible-work requests formally, in writing, citing the Employment Relations Act | Don't expect an automatic yes — employers can decline for several broad reasons |
| Expect an answer within a month, by law | Don't expect a legal right to work from home — there isn't one |
| Plan for real time-zone isolation on calls with the US or Europe | Don't schedule "quick" syncs with Northern Hemisphere teams without expecting someone's evening ruined |

| Expect public servants to average under a day a week at home | Don't be surprised if "flexible" means shifted hours more often than a location change |

### Israel

Israeli hybrid work is less a lifestyle perk than a running negotiation between a booming, globally plugged-in tech sector and a state that periodically drafts a meaningful share of that sector's engineers. Since mid-2024 the pendulum has swung toward the office: Wix's president announced a full five-day, in-office week that, per CTech, set off a small civil war on LinkedIn between employees calling it regression and executives calling it necessary cohesion. Playtika followed. Industry estimates put Israeli companies now pushing for more office time at 65-70%, with pure remote roles down to single digits of tech postings.

Layered on top is a scheduling problem no HR platform has a field for: reserve duty. Every Israeli who completed military service remains obligated to periodic call-ups, typically two to four weeks a year, sometimes far more, and high-tech workers are disproportionately represented in reserve units. A manager cannot plan a sprint around an engineer who may be unreachable for a month with little notice — putting Israel's return-to-office push and its most disruptive flexibility need in open contradiction. Hofstede data explains the default to physical proximity: Israel scores 81 on uncertainty avoidance, among the highest in the OECD sample, meaning ambiguity about who's in the office and who's been called up is precisely what the culture tolerates least. The fix for uncertainty, apparently, is togetherness — not looser remote policy.

### New Zealand

New Zealand's approach is almost the mirror image: procedurally generous, substantively cautious. Since 2007, Part 6AA of the Employment Relations Act has given every employee, from day one, the right to request a change to hours, days, or place of work, for any reason. Employers must respond within a month, on pain of a fine — but the law grants no right to have the request approved. Employers decline on broad grounds, and increasingly do: Stats NZ's Household Labour Force Survey put the share of employed people doing some work from home at 34% in June 2025, flat since 2022, while the public service — the sector with the most granular data — reported staff averaging just 0.85 days at home per week, and falling.

The cultural backdrop makes this restraint counterintuitive. New Zealand scores 79 on Hofstede's individualism index against Israel's 54, and 75 on indulgence, suggesting a population well disposed toward autonomy and leisure. Yet nearly a third of surveyed businesses told researchers in late 2025 they intend to end fully remote arrangements by 2026. The laid-back reputation describes what happens after 5 p.m., not where you're allowed to be at 10 a.m.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Israel, culturally collectivist and improvisational, treats the office as default and remote work as an exception renegotiated constantly — even though its own defense obligations are the single biggest disruptor of any predictable in-office schedule in either country. New Zealand, culturally individualist and reputedly relaxed, built the more bureaucratic system: a codified right to ask, a statutory deadline to answer, and a workforce that, having asked, mostly gets told to keep commuting.

Neither country offers what a remote-first worker might expect: an entitlement to work from anywhere. Israel doesn't offer it because ambiguity is corporately unbearable. New Zealand doesn't offer it because the law formalizes a conversation, not its outcome. The genuine flexibility, in both places, lives in the gaps policy doesn't reach — the Friday half-day, the after-5 p.m. silence — not in the remote-work clause itself.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American who relocated to Israel for a remote US-based role said the real friction was time zone, not culture: evening hours to match US colleagues meant missing weekday dinners, and once, a work call colliding directly with a wedding.*

> *Quora — Someone researching Israeli tax residency under the Aliyah exemption noted the ten-year break on foreign income sounds generous until you try to determine, mid-relocation, whether remote work performed physically in Israel for a foreign employer still counts as "foreign" — nobody asked gave the same answer twice.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter working a New Zealand-to-Northern-Hemisphere reporting line described a routine 20-minute clarification turning into a lost day, purely because the 10-to-12-hour gap meant every reply landed after the other side had gone home.*

> *TeamBlind — An Israeli tech employee described return-to-office announcements landing differently by team: engineers fully remote for three years got six weeks' notice to relocate near an office, no stipend offered, while newer hires treated the policy as simply how the job had always worked.*

> *Expat.com — A new arrival in Auckland was surprised how rarely "work from home" came up unprompted in interviews; the employers she raised it with treated the request as reasonable but unusual, as if she'd introduced a foreign concept rather than a normal ask.*

## Conclusion

Choosing between them for a remote-friendly career means choosing what kind of unpredictability you can tolerate. Israel offers genuine hybrid infrastructure and a tech sector that still, formally, talks about flexibility — but the rhythm can be scrambled overnight by forces outside HR's control, and the pull back to full-time office presence is accelerating faster than most job postings admit. New Zealand offers a workforce that plainly wants boundaries and a law that makes asking for them free — but the answer, statistically, trends toward no, and the isolation makes any cross-hemisphere arrangement a standing tax on somebody's evening.

Neither government has built what remote workers actually want: a right to the outcome, not just the ask. Israel solved that by mostly not asking. New Zealand solved it by writing down, quite formally, exactly how you're allowed to be told no.
