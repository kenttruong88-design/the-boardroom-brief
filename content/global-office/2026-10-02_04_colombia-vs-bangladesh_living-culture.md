**Estrato 4 vs. Gulshan: How Your Address Sets Your Price in Colombia and Bangladesh**

**🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇧🇩 Bangladesh**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Colombia has, uniquely among nations, assigned every residential address a number from one to six — your *estrato* — which quietly determines what you pay for electricity, water, and gas, regardless of your actual income. Bangladesh has no such system, but it doesn't need one: Dhaka's expat enclave of Gulshan runs on its own private economy entirely, where one newcomer found apartment rents running not in the hundreds of euros she'd budgeted for, but in the thousands, simply because that is the price of living where other foreigners already live. Both countries have solved the same problem — how much should a newcomer pay to exist here — with two completely different, equally opaque answers.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your landlord or employer your *estrato* before signing anything — it silently sets your utility bills | Assume a national inflation figure reflects your actual cost of living — expats report real inflation running well above official 6% stats |
| Enroll in the EPS public health system through your employer; it's mandatory and genuinely functional | Skip supplemental private insurance if you can afford it — EPS wait times for specialists can be long |
| Compare rent across cities before committing to Bogotá — Manizales and Barranquilla run noticeably cheaper for comparable space | Assume "cheap by US standards" means cheap outright — imported goods, electronics, and specialty groceries carry a steep premium |
| Budget separately for imported staples (berries, specialty nuts, electronics) if you keep home-country eating habits | Expect one blanket "Colombian cost of living" — it varies more by estrato and city than almost any other Latin American country |
| Use a local accountant for your first tax year — residency rules affect income tax status quickly | Assume your employer's HR fully explains EPS vs. private coverage — expats routinely report needing to ask twice |

#### 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Dhaka before committing to a lease, if at all possible — the gap between guidebook averages and expat-enclave reality is large | Trust citywide cost-of-living averages for your own budget — Gulshan and Banani run far above the Numbeo city median |
| Let your employer's relocation team choose your neighborhood — most arrange housing in established expat areas for practical reasons | Assume structural safety is uniform — building codes and enforcement vary sharply by area and developer |
| Bring a mattress and pillows if you have specific preferences — local options differ from what many Western arrivals expect | Pack heavy bedding, duvets, or winter clothing — Dhaka's climate makes it dead weight |
| Budget for private health insurance from day one — public healthcare infrastructure is stretched relative to population | Assume monthly averages for "a single person" apply to a family — family cost-of-living estimates run roughly 3.5 times higher |
| Ask specifically about childcare options before arriving — formal, Western-style childcare is concentrated almost entirely in expat-heavy neighborhoods | Expect walkable residential density to resemble a Western suburb — most apartment living is vertical and compact even in wealthier enclaves |

### Colombia: the number on your door

Colombia's *estrato* system, assigning every residence a socioeconomic tier from one (poorest) to six (wealthiest), was designed as a cross-subsidy mechanism: higher-estrato households pay more for utilities so lower-estrato households pay less. For a newly arrived professional, it functions as an invisible second salary negotiation — the same job offer lands very differently depending on whether your apartment sits in a 3 or a 5. Forum testimony on Expat.com bears this out at the ground level: rents for comparable two- and three-bedroom units range from $320 a month in a mid-tier Bogotá neighborhood to $500 in Barranquilla's "estrato 4" zone, with posters noting real inflation feels closer to 10-15 percent despite official figures near 6 percent.

Healthcare runs through the EPS system, a World Bank-studied universal coverage model that is mandatory, employer-linked, and — by regional standards — genuinely functional, though expat guides consistently note that supplemental private insurance smooths over specialist wait times that EPS alone doesn't solve. Overall living costs sit roughly 40 percent below a mid-sized US city, per Numbeo's Bogotá data, but that average flattens real variation: a Bogotá expat spending liberally on imported groceries can blow past a Manizales counterpart living on local staples by a wide margin, in the same country, on the same salary.

### Bangladesh: two economies, one city

Bangladesh's Numbeo data puts Dhaka at roughly 64 percent cheaper than a mid-sized US city overall and 87 percent cheaper on rent specifically — numbers that describe a real city most of its residents actually live in, and describe almost nothing about where a relocating professional will actually be housed. An ExpatExchange account of moving to Dhaka is blunt about the gap: coming from Bucharest, where "a decent apt...will be a few hundred euro," the same standard in Dhaka's expat-concentrated Gulshan district runs "in the thousands" — because Gulshan isn't really priced against the Dhaka median, it's priced against what other multinational employees and their employers are willing to pay.

Welfare infrastructure outside that enclave remains thin by regional comparison; public healthcare capacity is stretched relative to population, and most relocating professionals default to private insurance from day one rather than relying on public systems. Childcare follows the same enclave logic as housing: formal, internationally-recognizable childcare options cluster almost entirely in Gulshan and similar neighborhoods, priced accordingly, while the broader city runs on family and informal-care networks that a foreign arrival typically can't access.

### The Reckoning

Both systems solve the newcomer-pricing problem by geography rather than by income, just inverted: Colombia explicitly, legally stratifies every address so cost tracks neighborhood by design; Bangladesh does it informally, letting Gulshan's density of embassies, multinationals, and expatriates bid up an entire enclave's prices independent of any national policy. The Colombian system is more honest about what it's doing — the number is printed on your utility bill — while the Bangladeshi system requires you to discover the real price by actually moving there, as the Bucharest-to-Dhaka account discovered the hard way.

The counterintuitive twist: Colombia's explicit stratification, designed as redistribution, can feel more predictable to a newcomer than Bangladesh's laissez-faire enclave pricing, precisely because it's written down. A foreign professional in Colombia can look up their estrato before they sign a lease; a foreign professional in Bangladesh generally cannot know their real cost of living until they've already committed to a neighborhood an employer chose for them.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Colombia cost-of-living forum) — One Bogotá-based poster described paying roughly $320 a month for a modest Teusaquillo apartment while another in Manizales paid about $330 for a full three-bedroom, two-bath unit — concrete evidence, from direct comparison, that "Colombia's cost of living" is really several different cost structures depending entirely on which city and estrato you land in.*

> *Expat.com (Colombia cost-of-living forum) — A contributor flagged a recurring frustration: official inflation figures near 6 percent didn't match lived experience, which felt closer to 10-15 percent, especially for imported staples like berries and specialty nuts that many expats add back into their diet rather than fully adapting to local grocery norms.*

> *ExpatExchange (Dhaka relocation report) — An expat who moved from Bucharest to Dhaka's Gulshan district described the rent shock directly, framing it as a lesson for future arrivals: budget expectations formed in a mid-sized European capital simply do not transfer, and the practical advice offered was blunt — visit before you commit to anything.*

> *ExpatExchange (Dhaka relocation report) — The same account flagged a less obvious risk: in neighborhoods with inconsistent building codes, a quiet street of single-story houses can abruptly sprout a six-to-ten-story structure next door, a construction and shading risk that guidebooks rarely mention alongside rent figures.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Colombia, the real negotiation isn't your salary — it's your estrato, and it's worth researching before you pick a neighborhood, not after. If you're moving to Bangladesh, assume the guidebook numbers describe a city you won't actually be living in, and budget from Gulshan prices, not the national average. The line I'd give a friend over a drink: in both countries, the cost of living is really the cost of *which* living — find out which slice of the city your employer is actually putting you in before you believe any number at all.
