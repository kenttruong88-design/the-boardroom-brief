# Bucharest and Sofia Have Both Heard of the Home Office and Remain Unconvinced

🇷🇴 Romania · 🇧🇬 Bulgaria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two of the European Union's most prolific exporters of software engineers have achieved something remarkable, which is to employ those engineers while keeping them out of their own spare rooms. According to Eurostat's 2025 figures, as reported by Euronews, about 1.3 percent of employed people in Romania and 1.4 percent in Bulgaria usually work from home, against an EU average of roughly 9 percent and a Finnish high of 20.5 percent. Both countries are, in other words, a sixteenth of Finland. Anyone moving there for a job should assume the flexibility on the brochure is a negotiating position, not a fixture.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the exact number and the named days of office attendance before signing | Accept "hybrid" as an answer without a number attached |
| Check that the telework arrangement sits in your contract, not only in an internal policy | Assume a Slack announcement can rewrite your terms; a contract generally changes by agreement |
| Plan for two or three office days in Cluj or Bucharest, with Thursday the likely crowd | Rent far from the office on the strength of a verbal promise of remote Fridays |
| Budget for commuting, equipment and home-office running costs when comparing offers | Treat the net salary as the whole picture; the old IT income tax exemption ended on 1 January 2025 |
| Look at EU or US employers hiring remotely if full remote is non-negotiable | Expect local outsourcing firms to offer full remote as a standard option |
| Confirm who pays for the desk, chair and internet | Assume the employer has read the telework rules as carefully as the inspectors may |

### Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the remote workplace to be described in writing, as the 2024 Labour Code amendments expect | Work from a location your employer has never been told about |
| Use the right to request a change of remote workplace for up to 30 working days a year | Assume that "remote" and "abroad" are the same thing |
| Rely on the rule that you need not answer employer messages during rest periods | Answer midnight messages and then wonder why they continue |
| Ask whether your employer uses algorithmic tracking, and request the written explanation | Let a monitoring system make decisions about you without asking how it decides |
| Research Sofia hybrid norms in the IT sector, where office leasing is strongest | Expect a large technology employer to be downsizing its floor space |
| Treat a remote offer from a foreign firm as a separate market from local employment | Count on a US employer agreeing to keep you on its payroll from Bulgaria |

## Romania: A Telework Law and a Hybrid Handshake

Romania's remote-work story is one of enthusiasm defeated by logistics. Back in 2022, a survey by Undelucram.ro, cited by Romania-Insider, found 72 percent of employees wanted to keep working from home, and that 54 percent would refuse a request to come in three to five days a week. A 2023 survey by eJobs Romania was less rosy about the reality, finding 46.1 percent of employees worked exclusively at the office and 60 percent of candidates would decline a job without remote or hybrid options. Both surveys are several years old, and employers have had time to respond. They appear to have responded by asking for more attendance.

The most recent evidence points to a market settling on a compromise nobody loves. A November 2026 survey by Cluj Business Campus, reported by Undelucram.ro, found 44 percent of companies renting space there had left their attendance rules nearly unchanged over two years, with about 17 percent raising on-site days and roughly the same share lowering them. Two or three days a week was the ideal, Thursday the favourite. The survey covers one campus and does not disclose its sample, so it is a mood, not a census. Meanwhile, Adevarul reported in August 2026 that full-remote IT jobs are getting scarce, and that some employees prefer resigning to returning. Some commenters in the r/programare subreddit, it said, regard return-to-office mandates as layoffs conducted by commuting.

There is also the cultural backdrop. Hofstede's dimensions, as reproduced in teaching tables, give Romania a power distance of 90 and uncertainty avoidance of 90, both near the top of any chart. That is a polite way of saying managers like to see people at desks, and rules like to be written down before being ignored. The EBRD's Aksoy told Euronews that countries with stronger norms of in-person supervision tend to have lower remote adoption, which is a general finding, not a Romanian verdict, but it fits uncomfortably well.

The state, to its credit, is watching the paperwork if not the philosophy. CEE Legal Matters wrote in 2025 that Romanian labour inspections were expected to examine remote work compliance more closely, with health and safety, equipment reimbursement and working hours singled out. The same briefing observed that workers increasingly treat remote work as a default rather than a privilege, which is the sort of sentence that explains most return-to-office memos. Undelucram.ro's own advice to candidates is to ask whether attendance rules changed in the past two years, and to remember that refusing telework cannot by itself justify a unilateral change to a contract or a disciplinary measure. It is useful guidance, in the way a fire exit is useful when one is already inside the building.

## Bulgaria: Rules Written Carefully, Offices Leased Enthusiastically

Bulgaria has done the legislative homework. According to KPMG Bulgaria, amendments to the Labour Code that took effect on 2 April 2024 oblige employers to ensure each remote workplace meets health and safety minimums, to explain in writing any algorithm that manages remote workers, and to allow a change of remote workplace for up to 30 working days a year at the employee's written request. Employees need not respond to employer messages during daily or weekly rest periods unless their contract says otherwise. It is a respectable framework, and it applies to a workforce of which only 1.4 percent works from home.

The commercial picture is less accommodating. Cushman & Wakefield | Forton reported, via Fakti.bg, that Sofia leased 61,000 square metres of office space in the third quarter of 2025, with IT companies taking nearly 57 percent of it. The report measures leasing, not attendance, so one should not infer a specific number of office days. One may, however, infer that firms who lease that much space intend to fill it. The same report put Sofia's vacancy rate at 12.4 percent at the end of September, with no new office buildings completed in the quarter and about 200,000 square metres under construction, so the market is hardly pining for emptiness. Bulgaria's cultural profile in the same teaching table, a power distance of 70 and uncertainty avoidance of 85 (both marked as estimates), is a notch gentler than Romania's but points in the same direction. An earlier BTA interview, from 2022, with a Bulgarian human-resources association board member, said hybrid was growing in high tech and public administration, which is a reasonable summary of what Bulgaria has since achieved: hybrid in a building.

For anyone hoping to be employed from abroad, an Expat.com thread from around late 2025 is sobering. A US citizen living in Bulgaria reported that most American remote roles require staying in the United States, and a resident adviser said fully remote jobs exist but attract competition from lower-cost workers worldwide. The advice is old and unglamorous. Sell skills the employer cannot find at home, and expect to be asked where you will be sitting.

## The Reckoning

On paper, Bulgaria is the more orderly place to work remotely. Its Labour Code spells out workplace safety, algorithmic transparency and the right to ignore a late-night message, which gives an employee something to cite. Romania has a telework framework too, but the practical advice from its own press is to check the contract, because internal policy is a changeable thing. Both countries sit at the bottom of the Eurostat table, separated by 0.1 percentage points, which is within the margin of a long lunch.

Euronews offers some general reasons why south-eastern Europe lags, and none of them are flattering or particularly fixable. Remote work is rarer where economies lean on manufacturing, construction, tourism and in-person services, and Eurofound's Jorge Cabrita pointed to poor connectivity as a deterrent elsewhere in the region. Neither point obviously describes the technology sector in Sofia or Cluj, where the jobs are desk-based by nature, which suggests the binding constraint there is management habit rather than the job mix. That is the awkward part for any relocating professional: the work is portable, the law is mostly ready, and the person approving the roster is not.

The difference is in the expectations. Romanian workers have shown, in surveys and in resignation threats, that they want flexibility and are willing to say so. Bulgarian employers, at least in Sofia, have shown a taste for large office leases and a hybrid compromise. Neither country offers the Finnish arrangement of working wherever the laptop lands. If flexibility is a deal-breaker, pick a company, not a country, and ask for the policy in writing before the relocation.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/programare, paraphrased via Adevarul): Full-remote IT roles are rare now, and even outsourcing firms have gone hybrid. One commenter said the offers paying comparable salaries wanted two or three office days in Cluj or Bucharest, where the pay would not cover the rent.*

> *Expat.com (Bulgaria forum, paraphrased experience): An American in Bulgaria lost a US remote contract, then discovered that few American employers will keep a worker abroad. A fellow poster said couples they knew had negotiated remote arrangements after already working for the company.*

> *Expat.com (Sofia forum, paraphrased experience): A poster advised that remote work for clients in wealthier countries usually pays better than local roles, and that living cheaply at home can beat a modest relocation offer abroad.*

> *Blind (Romania tech thread, paraphrased): A commenter in 2022 said remote jobs with EU companies had become easier to find and paid closer to San Francisco levels, and advised avoiding nearshore consulting firms. The thread is old, so treat the pay claims as indicative.*

> *Romania-Insider (first-person, paraphrased): A journalist who moved from Bucharest to a village said it worked only because her role suited remote work and her small team could be trusted without supervision. She still travels in for some meetings and sometimes misses being in the room.*

## Conclusion

The practical conclusion is unromantic. Neither Romania nor Bulgaria is a remote-work destination in the Finnish sense, and a job offer there should be read for what it says about attendance, not what it hints about lifestyle. Bulgaria gives you a stronger statutory toolkit; Romania gives you a sharper sense of what employees expect, and a labour market where some are willing to quit over the point. In both, the person who signs your rota matters more than the law that frames it.

If you want maximum flexibility, ask for the number of office days, the named days and the contract clause before accepting. If a hiring manager cannot answer, you have learned the policy. Both countries will let you work from home, provided you can first prove you are not needed at the office.
