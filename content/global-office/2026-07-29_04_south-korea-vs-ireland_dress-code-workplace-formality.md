**A Suit Is Never Just a Suit: What Your Wardrobe Is Actually Signaling in Seoul and Dublin**

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Dublin, showing up in a full suit and tie to a Tuesday client meeting will get you a few curious glances and maybe a joke about the funeral you're late for; in Seoul, showing up underdressed to the same meeting can quietly cost you credibility before you've said a word. One country dresses for comfort with an undertone of competence. The other dresses for hierarchy, and the wardrobe is doing more organizational work than most job descriptions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a dark suit, white shirt, and conservative tie until your specific office proves otherwise | Show up underdressed to a first meeting — appearance is read as a proxy for seriousness |
| Keep hair, makeup, and grooming polished daily — Korean offices treat overall presentation, not just clothing, as part of the dress code | Get visible tattoos, facial piercings, or unnatural hair colors without checking your industry's norms first |
| Watch what senior colleagues wear and calibrate slightly more formal, not less | Assume startup or tech roles mean casual dress by default — many still expect suits |
| Expect appearance standards to shift by context — a work dinner, a client visit, and an internal meeting can each carry different expectations | Treat one "casual Friday" as evidence the whole week has loosened up |
| Notice generational movement toward business casual at some chaebols and use it as a cue, not a rule | Push a casual dress code onto your own team before confirming leadership has actually endorsed the shift |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to smart casual — a well-fitted blazer with chinos or tailored trousers covers most professional settings | Over-dress dramatically for a casual office; it can read as trying too hard rather than as respect |
| Invest in quality over formality — fit and presentation matter more than how buttoned-up the outfit is | Assume "casual" means sloppy; expectations around quality have risen even as formality has dropped |
| Save the full suit for client-facing finance, legal, or aviation-leasing environments where it's still standard | Wear the same outfit to a scrappy creative agency that you'd wear to a Dublin law firm |
| Use first names and informal rapport-building early — it mirrors the relaxed dress norm | Mistake casualness in dress or address for a lack of professional expectation elsewhere |
| Check specific workplace norms in your first week — dress code varies widely by industry | Assume the whole country dresses the same way; finance and tech can look completely different |

---

**South Korea's** approach to workplace dress treats appearance as inseparable from professional credibility. World Business Culture's country guides describe the standard as a dark suit, white shirt, and dark tie for men, with women expected in similarly formal dresses or suits — and the boundaries extend well past clothing into grooming, with expat guides to Seoul's corporate culture noting that even one missed day of makeup on a team was enough to visibly unsettle colleagues. General workplace norms explicitly discourage visible tattoos, facial piercings, and unconventional hairstyles, treating "excessive individuality" as a professional liability rather than a personal choice. Hofstede Insights data consistently ranks South Korea high on power distance, and the dress code operates as a daily, visible reinforcement of that structure — where you sit in the hierarchy is legible from across the room before anyone speaks. There is movement: some chaebols have edged toward business casual in recent years, but the shift is gradual and unevenly adopted.

**Ireland's** relationship to workplace formality has moved in the opposite direction, and moved recently. The Irish Times and multiple business-etiquette guides describe smart casual as the default across most Irish professional environments now, a marked shift from the more rigid, suit-driven expectations that held a generation ago. That doesn't mean standards have vanished — presentation, fit, and quality expectations have arguably increased even as formality dropped, so "casual" in Dublin still means a well-cut blazer, not the loosest interpretation of business attire. Formality survives in pockets: finance, legal, and Ireland's outsized aircraft-leasing sector still lean toward traditional suiting, while much of tech and creative industry has settled into a relaxed middle ground. Irish workplace culture more broadly is described as informal and relationship-driven, with first names used early and a general emphasis on being approachable — the wardrobe simply follows that broader social register.

**The Reckoning:** The two countries have essentially inverted what a suit signals. In Seoul, formal dress is a baseline expectation that communicates respect for hierarchy and seriousness about the job; deviating from it downward is the risk. In Dublin, formal dress increasingly signals distance or over-formality; deviating from it upward — showing up too buttoned-up — can read as slightly off, not impressive. An employee who dresses "correctly" by South Korean standards on their first day in a Dublin office may come across as stiff or uncertain about the culture, while an employee who dresses by Irish smart-casual standards on their first day in a Seoul office may come across as not taking the job seriously — and both mistakes are entirely about the same instinct to dress well.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech worker who transferred from a US office to a Seoul team described the appearance standard as more exhausting than the actual work in the first month, noting that colleagues clocked small details like a wrinkled shirt in a way no previous manager ever had.*

> *Quora — Someone who spent several years working in Seoul before relocating to Dublin wrote that the strangest adjustment was realizing nobody in their new Irish office was silently evaluating their outfit each morning, and that the mental energy freed up by that alone was noticeable.*

> *Internations — A German professional based in Seoul described being pulled aside by HR after a casual Friday outfit was deemed too informal for a client-facing role, despite the company's official policy technically permitting it — the unwritten rule outranked the written one.*

> *Fodor's Travel Talk Forums — A visitor-turned-resident in Dublin noted that professional Irish colleagues seemed to judge outfits on fit and care rather than formality, observing that a well-tailored casual look earned more respect than an expensive but ill-fitting suit.*

> *Quora — An American who moved to Seoul for a corporate role advised future expats to buy their work wardrobe locally rather than bringing it from home, since Korean-market tailoring and silhouettes read as noticeably more put-together to local colleagues than off-the-rack Western suiting.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Seoul, over-invest in your wardrobe before you start — treat it as onboarding material, not an afterthought, and expect the standard to extend to grooming, not just clothes. If you're heading to Dublin, resist the urge to bring your most formal wardrobe as a show of respect; a well-fitted casual outfit will read as more competent, not less. The through-line in both places is that nobody is really talking about clothes — they're talking about whether you understand the room. My honest advice: pack fewer suits than you think you need for Dublin, and more than you think you need for Seoul, and you'll be right both times.
