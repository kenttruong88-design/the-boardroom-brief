**The Company That Won't Let You Leave, and the One That Won't Ask You to Stay: Job Loyalty in Canada vs. Japan**

**🇨🇦 Canada · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Japan, quitting can be such a fraught social event that a cottage industry of "resignation agents" (*taishoku daiko*) now exists purely to hand in other people's notice for them, because some employers still act as though a trained employee leaving is a kind of betrayal. In Canada, the average worker has simply stopped bothering to test the market: tenure sat at 8.5 years in 2023, and job-switching has cooled well below pre-pandemic levels, according to Statistics Canada. One country enforces loyalty; the other seems to be backing into it by accident.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your resume current even in a job you like | Assume loyalty alone protects you from a layoff |
| Negotiate before you consider leaving — most raises now come from staying, not switching | Job-hop purely on principle in a cooling market |
| Interview occasionally to keep your skills and market rate sharp | Treat a stable tenure as evidence you're being paid fairly |
| Read the slowdown in job-switching as a market signal, not a personal failure | Panic if your peers seem to be moving and you're not |
| Ask directly for growth opportunities if you plan to stay long-term | Wait passively for promotion in a market with less internal churn |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your resignation to be treated as a serious, personal event | Assume a two-weeks-notice email will be received gracefully |
| Research a company's actual turnover before joining if flexibility matters to you | Assume all Japanese firms still offer *shūshin koyō* (lifetime employment) — only 8.8% do |
| Know that switching jobs within 3 years can still read as a red flag to traditional employers | Job-hop as aggressively as you might in North America without checking sector norms |
| Consider whether an international or foreign-owned firm suits your mobility expectations better | Expect Japanese-style annual turnover (under 10% in large firms) if you value fast advancement |
| Use official channels (labour bureaus, or resignation agencies if truly stuck) if an employer won't process your exit | Assume Japanese law doesn't protect your right to quit — it does |

---

Canada's story isn't really about loyalty as a value — it's about a market that has, for now, made loyalty the path of least resistance. Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey puts average job tenure at 8.5 years as of 2023, up slightly from 2022 but below the pandemic-era peak. The job-switching rate that spiked in 2022, when vacancies were "through the roof," has since cooled substantially. Economists watching the trend note this isn't necessarily healthy: mobility is how workers, especially early-career ones, typically gain responsibility and pay, and when switching slows across the board, a kind of traffic jam forms, with fewer openings trickling down to new entrants. Canadian loyalty, in other words, is less a cultural commitment than a symptom of a tighter labour market — which means it could reverse the moment conditions do.

Japan's loyalty is the real thing, or at least it was. *Shūshin koyō* — lifetime employment — emerged as post-war ideology: stay through to retirement, and the company protects you through good times and bad. It's now down to roughly 8.8% of Japanese companies actually offering it, according to labour research, but the cultural residue is enormous. Japan's inter-firm mobility rate has averaged less than half that of OECD peers over the past two decades, and annual turnover for core employees at large firms sits under 10%, compared with over 40% in many U.S. sectors. The system has produced real strangeness at the margins: because some managers, especially older ones raised inside the old hierarchy, take a subordinate's departure almost personally, an entire industry of *taishoku daiko* resignation agents has grown up to submit paperwork on behalf of employees too intimidated to quit in person — even though Japanese law guarantees the right to resign.

The Reckoning: Canada's stability is economic weather, liable to change with the next hiring cycle; Japan's stability is closer to load-bearing cultural infrastructure, reinforced by law, hierarchy, and shame, and it's eroding slowly rather than snapping. Hofstede Insights' long-term orientation dimension puts Japan among the highest-scoring cultures in the world, consistent with a system built around decades-long commitment, while Canada scores in the more short-term, individualist range typical of Anglo economies — meaning a Canadian who stays five years is behaving normally, and a Japanese employee who leaves after two is still, in many rooms, behaving abnormally. The irony worth sitting with: younger Japanese workers are increasingly prioritizing personal well-being over institutional loyalty, converging toward the Canadian mindset just as Canada's own market conditions are nudging workers back toward staying put.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *reddit.com/r/jobs — Responding to a thread asking whether staying loyal to one company is career suicide, one commenter described turning down several better offers over a decade out of loyalty, only to be laid off anyway, and summarized the lesson as: never make the decision out of loyalty alone.*

> *reddit.com/r/jobs — Another commenter in the same thread said they'd job-hopped from $12.50 an hour to a six-figure salary within five years, framing tenure as a financial decision rather than a character trait — though others in the thread warned that leaving before the one-year mark can itself read as a red flag to future employers.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread titled "Job hopping in Japan," posters noted that most Japanese companies still don't welcome candidates who've stayed under three years per job, while one engineer described hopping three times in three years specifically because pay and promotion had stalled — a strategy that worked, but that they knew read badly on paper locally.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about lifetime employment in Japan noted that the stigma once attached to working for a foreign company instead of a traditional Japanese one has faded considerably, making that path a more viable option than it used to be for people who want mobility without fully adopting local job-hopping norms.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Canada from a place with less mobility, the useful mental shift is to stop treating your resume as a monument and start treating it as a living document — the market may be quiet right now, but quiet markets end. If you're moving to Japan from a more fluid system, the shift is to understand that a resignation there isn't just an HR transaction; it can be read as a relational rupture, so plan your exit with the same care you'd plan a difficult conversation. Priya's honest take, over a drink: Canada is currently loyal because it's convenient, and Japan is loyal because it's expensive not to be — neither country has actually settled the argument about which approach serves the worker better.
