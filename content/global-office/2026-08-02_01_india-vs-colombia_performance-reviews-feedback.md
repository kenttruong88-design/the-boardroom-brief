**The Annual Review Will See You Now: A Survival Guide for India and Colombia**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇨🇴 Colombia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In India, a bad performance review can end a career trajectory quietly, over months, through a Performance Improvement Plan that both parties understand is a formality on the way out. In Colombia, a bad performance review is more likely to happen in a closed-door meeting so gentle you might not clock it as bad until the paperwork arrives weeks later. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they value direct feedback. Neither means it the way a Dutch manager means it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect feedback to flow top-down, from a manager who has final say | Assume a 360-degree review process means peers can say what they actually think |
| Read between the lines when a manager says "let's discuss offline" | Take vague feedback at face value — ask specific, private follow-up questions |
| Keep a written record of praise and commitments, since verbal promises shift | Push back on a manager's assessment in front of the team, even if you disagree |
| Treat your manager's opinion of you as a major career input, not just an HR checkbox | Assume staying quiet during review season protects you — silence reads as disengagement |
| Ask directly what "meets expectations" versus "exceeds expectations" actually requires | Expect a low rating to come with a clear explanation unprompted |

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build rapport with your manager well before review season — trust shapes the score | Expect negative feedback delivered bluntly, even when performance genuinely slipped |
| Read tone and context carefully; disapproval is often implied, not stated | Assume a pleasant, warm review conversation means your job is secure |
| Ask for concrete examples if feedback feels vague — it usually is, by design | Criticize a colleague's work in a group setting, even constructively |
| Expect praise to be given publicly and criticism privately, almost without exception | Interpret indirectness as weakness — it is a deliberate way of preserving dignity |
| Use formal titles and a respectful tone with your reviewer, even in casual companies | Skip the small talk at the start of a review meeting — it is doing real work |

Indian corporate appraisal culture inherited its bones from a mix of British administrative hierarchy and a domestic services industry that scaled through rigid, stack-ranked systems. The stack rank is technically retired at most major employers, but its ghost lingers: reviews of Indian performance management practices find companies are only slowly shifting from top-down appraisal toward 360-degree and continuous-feedback models, and cultural friction with the new systems is real, because peer feedback assumes a flatness Indian offices don't structurally have. India's Hofstede power distance score is 77, well above the global average of roughly 56 — a number that shows up in review season as employees who wait to be told rather than volunteer an opinion, and managers who expect that wait.

The upside, reported consistently by people who've worked under Indian management, is clarity once feedback does arrive. On the workplace forum Blind, one thread praised Indian managers for giving actionable, specific direction that outlasted the reporting relationship itself — a contrast to the vague, sandwiched feedback common elsewhere. The downside shows up just as consistently: a viral post covered by Gulf News described an employee placed on a Performance Improvement Plan five months after joining, following a refusal to work during approved paid leave at a company that expected Sunday hours as standard. Feedback in India can be sharp and useful, or it can be an instrument of control — the review cycle doesn't always distinguish between the two.

Colombia's review culture runs on a different fuel entirely: relationship first, content second. Colombia also scores high on power distance — hierarchy is accepted, titles matter, and a manager's assessment carries real institutional weight — but it pairs that hierarchy with a collectivist, high-context communication style that treats direct criticism as a genuine social cost. Cultural guides on Colombian business etiquette are consistent on this point: negative feedback is typically delivered privately to protect the employee's reputation, while praise happens in front of the team to build it. A Bogotá-focused newsletter on Colombian work habits noted that long hours coexist with a real tolerance for informal drift — arriving fifteen minutes late, extended lunches, entire holiday weeks where the office empties out — because relationship maintenance is treated as legitimate work, not a distraction from it.

What this means for a reviewer trained elsewhere: a Colombian "there's room to grow" can be doing more damage-control work than it sounds like, and missing that subtext is a common expat misread. Salaries add their own pressure to how seriously employees take review outcomes — OECD data puts the average wage across member countries near $58,000 annually, while Colombia's national average sits closer to $9,400 and India's closer to $3,000, meaning a review-linked raise or bonus is proportionally a much bigger swing for employees in both countries than it is for their counterparts in wealthier OECD markets.

**The Reckoning.** Both countries share high power distance and a genuine reluctance toward peer-level bluntness, which makes them more alike than either would be to, say, the Netherlands. But the mechanism of politeness differs in a way that trips up managers who transfer between them: India's version tolerates directness once a hierarchy has been established, while Colombia's version treats directness itself as faintly hostile regardless of rank. A manager who is "just being honest" in Mumbai can read as needlessly harsh in Bogotá, delivering the same message with the same intent and getting a very different result. The counterintuitive part is that India, often stereotyped as indirect, actually produces sharper written and verbal feedback once a review is underway — it's the lead-up and the appeal process that stay opaque. Colombia is the reverse: warm and legible right up until the moment the actual verdict needs delivering.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (via Gulf News reporting) — An employee's account of being placed on a Performance Improvement Plan just five months into a new role, after declining to work during approved paid leave, went viral for capturing a workplace where six-day weeks and Sunday availability were treated as unstated defaults rather than exceptions.*

> *TeamBlind — One long-time contributor argued that Indian managers are underrated specifically for feedback quality, describing direction that was concrete enough to act on immediately and useful well after the reporting relationship ended — a rebuttal to the assumption that hierarchical cultures give worse feedback.*

> *TeamBlind — A thread titled "brutally honest feedback for Indians abroad" became a repository of the opposite complaint: that within India, admitting to received criticism can be read as weakness and sometimes gets quietly used against the employee later, breeding a reluctance to acknowledge feedback openly at all.*

> *Quora — Someone answering questions about working in Colombia as a foreigner noted that most expats who try to build a career on a local salary eventually leave, and that the ones who stay long-term are usually supplemented by remote income, a pension, or savings from home — a detail that shapes how seriously a Colombian review cycle should factor into someone's financial planning.*

> *Substack (mejiaj.substack.com) — A Colombia-based writer tracking local work habits observed that extended hours in the office coexist with real tolerance for informal time — fifteen minutes of grace on either end of the day, long lunches, entire weeks around Holy Week and December where output visibly drops — complicating any assumption that face time in Colombia maps cleanly onto how someone will actually be reviewed.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is about timing more than tone: in India, invest in the relationship with your manager continuously, because that relationship is the review system in everything but name, and clarity comes only after the hierarchy is satisfied. In Colombia, invest in the relationship even harder, but don't mistake warmth for a good outcome — ask direct questions to get direct answers, because the system is built to avoid volunteering them. Either way, don't wait for the annual conversation to find out where you stand.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: in India, worry when the feedback gets specific and cold. In Colombia, worry when it gets warm and vague. Neither country is going to tell you which one is happening — that part's on you.
