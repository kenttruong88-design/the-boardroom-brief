# Suits Are Dying in London. In São Paulo, They Never Left.

**🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Only 2.3% of London job postings now mention a casual dress code, the lowest share of any UK region, even as the national suit market has shrunk roughly 8% since 2015 and ties have quietly become optional almost everywhere else. In São Paulo, meanwhile, appearance still functions as a legible signal of rank — a three-piece suit reads "executive," a two-piece reads "office worker," and no one is confused about which is which. The British are relaxing dress code unevenly, sector by sector. Brazil never treated it as optional in the first place.

### United Kingdom: Formality Fragmenting by Postcode and Sector

British workplace dress code hasn't so much relaxed as it's split. According to data cited by [PA Life](https://palife.co.uk/news/workplace-wellbeing/stylists-top-tips-plus-british-workplace-dress-codes/) and [DavidsonMorris](https://www.davidsonmorris.com/office-dress-code/), Northern Ireland, North West England, and the East Midlands post the most casual job listings nationally, while London sits at the opposite extreme — just 2.3% of its postings mention casual dress — a direct reflection of the City's concentration of financial and legal employers where formality remains near-mandatory. Nationally, the suit itself is in structural decline: men's suit sales fell roughly 8% between 2015 and 2019 and the slide has continued, and once-obligatory items like the necktie are now genuinely optional even in traditionally formal settings.

What replaced the old uniform isn't chaos so much as understatement. British business casual, per multiple UK career guides, means pressed trousers, collared shirts, proper shoes with socks — precision and restraint rather than visible display. The dress code has become sector-legible rather than nationally uniform: what you wear now tells a colleague more about your industry than about the country you're in.

### Brazil: Appearance as a Readable Social Signal

Brazil has resisted this drift toward informality almost entirely in professional contexts, particularly in São Paulo. According to [World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/brazil/business-dress-code/), men are expected to wear conservative dark suits, with the specific cut carrying meaning — three-piece suits historically signal an executive position, two-piece suits signal general office staff. Legal, banking, and accounting roles are described as the strictest of all, to the point that seeing a banker in sneakers would be genuinely notable. Women, meanwhile, dress with more flair than in many comparable Western offices, per the same reporting — Brazilian professional culture tolerates and even rewards expressiveness in women's fashion in a way British corporate culture generally doesn't.

This isn't just aesthetic preference. [The Brazil Business](https://thebrazilbusiness.com/article/dress-code-in-brazil-by-industry) frames appearance as a genuine marker of social class, one Brazilians read quickly and specifically — which raises the stakes of getting dressed for work considerably higher than in the UK, where dress increasingly signals sector more than status. That said, the same reporting notes real movement at the margins: startups and tech firms have begun adopting business casual, and even traditionally strict companies have introduced "Casual Fridays," suggesting Brazil's formality, while still dominant, isn't static either.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part isn't that Brazil is more formal than Britain — it's that Britain's own formality hasn't disappeared so much as fractured by sector and geography, while Brazil's formality remains legible nationally even as it climate-adapts (lighter fabrics, not looser cuts). A visitor assuming "the UK is casual now" will be badly wrong-footed walking into a London law firm; a visitor assuming "Brazil is a hot, relaxed country, dress will be relaxed too" will be equally wrong-footed walking into a São Paulo bank.

Hofstede Insights data offers a partial explanation for why dress reads so differently as a signal in each culture: Brazil's power distance score of 69 reflects a society comfortable with visible hierarchy, of which dress is one legible marker; the UK's much lower score of 35 helps explain why British offices have been able to flatten visible status markers like dress without triggering the same social disruption it might in a higher-hierarchy culture.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing London office norms specified the exact texture of British business casual: pressed trousers with a front crease, proper shoes with socks, never trainers or sandals — a level of precision that surprised them coming from a culture with looser "smart casual" standards.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech worker comparing dress codes across companies noted how starkly a Meta-style office (genuinely no dress code) contrasted with UK finance postings for similar seniority roles, cautioning that switching from tech to finance in London meant relearning the wardrobe from scratch, not just the job.*

> *Expat.com forum — A business traveler preparing for a São Paulo trip asked what to actually pack, and the responses converged on one point: bring the suit regardless of the heat, because turning up underdressed to a client meeting reads as a lack of seriousness, not adaptability to climate.*

> *r/AskUK — A commenter reflecting on the disappearance of ties in most offices noted that seniority used to be visible at a glance and now genuinely isn't — junior staff and directors can be dressed almost identically in some offices, which they found made day-one social navigation harder, not easier.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway for anyone relocating: in the UK, dress for your specific sector and city, not for a national stereotype — a Shoreditch startup and a Canary Wharf bank might as well be different countries. In Brazil, dress for the meeting you're actually attending, not the weather, and assume appearance is being read as information about you whether you intend it or not.

If a friend asked me which approach to trust less: neither, entirely — pack the blazer either way, and let your first week of actual observation tell you what your specific office really expects, because both countries' national reputations are now less reliable guides than they used to be.
