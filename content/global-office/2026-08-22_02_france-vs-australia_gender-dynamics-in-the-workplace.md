# France Mandates Equality by Law; Australia Prefers to Publish a Spreadsheet About It

🇫🇷 France · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

France has spent fifteen years legislating women onto its corporate boards, which now sit close to 44 percent female — comfortably ahead of the global average — while roughly eight in ten French women report having personally witnessed sexism at their own desk. Australia has no such quota, a national pay gap nearly double France's, and a workplace culture that likes to think of itself as easygoing precisely because nobody has yet had to write the inequality into a boardroom bylaw. Neither country has solved the problem. They have simply chosen different ways of not solving it, and this guide is for anyone who needs to know which version of not-quite-equal they are relocating into.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR for your employer's Index de l'Égalité Professionnelle score (public by law) before signing — anything under 75 is a warning sign | Assume a gender-balanced board means a gender-balanced leadership team; the 40 percent quota stops at the boardroom door |
| Take the full 16-week congé maternité — pay and role are legally protected, and a downgraded return is grounds for a claim | Joke about a female boss being "worse than a man" — surveys of French offices list it as one of the most common sexist remarks made about women managers |
| Address colleagues by name and title | Assume "ma jolie" or similar diminutives read as charm rather than condescension — treat them as neither normal nor harmless |
| Expect a formally hierarchical office where the patron's word carries real weight | Expect fathers taking short paternity leave to be an act of disengagement — 28 days is the legal allowance, not a personal statement |
| Ask about your employer's unexplained pay-gap justification (mandatory over 5 percent) | Mistake a strong legal code for an enforced one — reporting mechanisms exist, but colleagues describe complaints going nowhere |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Look up your prospective employer's actual gender pay gap on the WGEA public data portal before you accept | Assume "no worries" informality means no hierarchy problem — the executive pay gap (26.2 percent) is wider than the national average (21.1 percent) |
| Ask specifically about promotion criteria in male-dominated sectors like mining, construction and finance | Take a FIFO mining or trading-floor role assuming the "boys' club" reputation is exaggerated — government inquiries have confirmed real culture problems there |
| Use the Fair Work Act's discrimination protections if you need them — they have teeth | Assume board diversity (38.1 percent women on ASX 200 boards) means C-suite diversity; the number of female ASX 300 CEOs actually fell in 2024 |
| Push for a written flexible-work arrangement if you're relocating with dependents | Assume flexible work is career-neutral — research links it to slower progression when managers don't formally support it |
| Compare notes on pay with peers at your level | Wait for the gap to be handed to you — WGEA's own data shows most people underestimate it until they ask directly |

## 🇫🇷 France: quotas at the top, culture in the middle

On paper, France is well ahead. The Copé-Zimmermann law pushed listed-company boards to roughly 40 percent female representation years ago, and the newer Rixain law extends the principle downward: 30 percent women in senior executive bodies from March 2026, rising to 40 percent by March 2029. Companies with 50 or more staff must publish an annual Gender Equality Index; the national average hit 88.5 out of 100 in 2025, and any unexplained pay gap over 5 percent now requires formal justification to regulators. According to the OECD, France's gender wage gap for full-time workers stood at 12 percent in 2022 — level with the OECD average, and a woman at the median earns roughly 88 cents to a man's euro.

The lived experience lags the legislation. Surveys of French offices cited by TheLocal.fr found roughly 80 percent of women reporting they had witnessed workplace sexism, with half saying they had personally been targeted — patronizing greetings, sexist jokes, and comments dismissing female bosses as harsher than their male counterparts. France's relatively high Hofstede power-distance score (around 68, versus Australia's 36) helps explain why: a culture that defers heavily to hierarchy makes it harder for quota-driven board seats to translate into a genuinely different day-to-day management style, since the men occupying the operational chain of command below board level were mostly promoted under the old rules.

Parental leave is generous by international standards — 16 weeks of maternity leave at close to full pay, and a paternity allowance that was doubled to 28 days in 2021 — but the career cost still lands disproportionately on women. Coverage of return-to-work experiences notes that many mothers go back sooner than they'd like, or leave permanently, because part-time work and childcare remain expensive enough to erode the value of staying, and comparatively few fathers use their full allotment.

## 🇦🇺 Australia: no quota, but nowhere to hide the numbers

Australia has taken the opposite approach: minimal mandated representation, maximal transparency. Since 2023–24, the Workplace Gender Equality Agency has published employer-level gender pay gap data — a level of disclosure most OECD countries don't require. The 2025 scorecard put the national gap at 21.1 percent, down 0.7 points from the year before but still meaning a woman earns 79 cents for every dollar a man earns, a $28,356 annual gap. The spread by industry is stark: about 5.1 percent in female-dominated sectors, rising to 15.6 percent in male-dominated ones, with finance, construction, mining and utilities the worst offenders.

The trend at the top is actively worsening rather than improving. The executive-level pay gap climbed to 26.2 percent in the past year, and female CEOs at ASX 300 companies fell from 26 to 25 in 2024 even as ASX 200 boards edged toward 38.1 percent female directors. Tech tells a similar story in miniature: entry-level women earn about 72 cents to the male dollar, the gap widens with each promotion, and women leave technical roles at roughly twice the rate of men after 40. Reporting on mining and finance culture — including the national Respect@Work inquiry and a Western Australian probe into fly-in-fly-out sites — has documented a genuine "boys' club" problem, not merely a perception one.

## The Reckoning

France's national wage gap (12 percent) is nearly half Australia's (21.1 percent), and its boards reached gender balance roughly a decade before Australia is projected to. But the same French system tolerates an office culture where sexist remarks remain common enough to be considered unremarkable, while Australia's public, employer-by-employer disclosure means a bad number can't be quietly absorbed into a national average — it sits next to the company's name for anyone to find.

The paradox is that the country with weaker formal protections is more honest about its failure, while the country with the stronger legal architecture is more forgiving of bad behavior day to day. Hofstede's dimensions offer a partial explanation: France's high power distance preserves top-down deference that a quota can mandate but not dissolve, while Australia's markedly higher individualism and lower power distance make it culturally easier to call out a bad manager by name — and harder for that manager to hide behind rank.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor working in Australian finance described the pay gap as invisible until she directly compared notes with male peers at her own level; the industry, she said, rewards long hours and visibility in ways that quietly favor colleagues without caregiving responsibilities.*

> *Quora — A foreign worker in a French office described the environment as formally polite but rigidly hierarchical, noting that the "boss" role remained overwhelmingly filled by men from elite grandes écoles backgrounds regardless of how balanced the board looked in the annual report — a classic case of mistaking the board slide for the org chart.*

> *TheLocal.fr — One respondent recounted being greeted daily as "ma jolie" (my pretty one) by a manager who considered it charm rather than condescension, and said colleagues had largely stopped reporting such comments because nothing ever seemed to change as a result.*

> *mylola.com (expat blog) — An American mother who gave birth while working in France wrote that the maternity protections let her focus entirely on recovery and the baby without fear of losing her job or income — a genuine, pleasant surprise compared to what she'd expected from home.*

> *SmartCompany.com.au — A female investment manager described leaving an Australian firm not over pay but because she was permanently treated as "not one of the boys," locked out of the informal deal-flow conversations where real career progression actually happened.*

### Conclusion

If what you want is more women in the room, France delivers — its boards prove quotas work at the level they're aimed at. If what you want is an employer that can't hide its number, Australia delivers that instead, publishing pay gaps by name even as its executive suite quietly gets less equal. Neither offers a shortcut around due diligence on the specific employer, not just the country.

The honest version, over drinks: don't relocate based on the national law, relocate based on the specific office's actual numbers — France will make you go digging in an index nobody reads, and Australia will hand you a spreadsheet the company would rather you didn't. Either way, read it before you sign, because the friendliest interview never mentions the org chart's actual color.
