# Pakistan Built a Freelance Economy Without a Rulebook, Canada Wrote the Rulebook and Wants You Back at Your Desk

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Pakistan's finance adviser announced in 2026 that freelancer export earnings reached 1.76 billion dollars, a record, earned by an estimated 2.5 to 3 million people whose legal status remains, to put it politely, under consideration. Canada has the opposite arrangement: employees whose rights are well documented and whose employers, increasingly, would like to see them in the building. A prospective migrant between the two will find that remote work is celebrated in the economy that has not legislated it and quietly rationed in the one that could.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether the employer has any written work-from-home policy | Assume an ad hoc directive is a permanent entitlement |
| Test your home internet and power backup before promising availability | Treat rural connectivity as equal to Karachi or Lahore |
| Agree measurable outputs with your manager early | Rely on goodwill to prove you are working |
| Clarify how foreign-currency freelance income is taxed | Assume your overseas clients' contract protects you locally |
| Expect messaging apps to double as the office channel | Ignore a message because it arrived outside a ticketing system |
| Budget for the electricity and data a home office consumes | Expect the employer to reimburse them by default |

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the posting for the number of on-site days required | Assume remote means a permanent arrangement |
| Put hybrid terms in your employment contract | Rely on a hallway conversation with your manager |
| Ask whether attendance expectations have changed recently | Take pandemic-era flexibility as the baseline |
| Think about commute time and housing cost together | Sign a lease before knowing your office days |
| Use hybrid days for deep work and office days for meetings | Spend office days on video calls |
| Raise concerns early if a mandate changes your plans | Wait for the mandate to take effect and then resign in a hurry |

## Pakistan: Plenty of Talent, Little Policy

The numbers on the export side are impressive. Arab News reports that freelancer export earnings reached 1.76 billion dollars in the fiscal year ended June 2026, up 78 percent, with per capita earnings rising from about 12 dollars a month in 2021 to nearly 53 dollars a month in 2026. The country was ranked 16th of 193 in a 2026 outsourcing talent index. The same report puts the freelancer population at 2.5 to 3 million, and flags this as an estimate. The talent is real. The framework around it is not.

According to the International Bar Association, Pakistan's legal system lacks a clear definition of freelance or gig work status, leaving remote workers uncertain about whether they are employees or contractors, how foreign-currency income is taxed, and what protections apply when a client abroad stops paying. Freelancers generally lack pension, health insurance and workers' compensation. Employees fare little better on policy. The Pakistan Institute of Development Economics found that there is no standardized work-from-home policy, and that directives have been issued ad hoc in response to crises, first the pandemic and then a 2026 fuel crisis in which up to 50 percent of office staff were told to work from home.

Attitudes follow the policy gap. The same PIDE brief, drawn from 12 interviews in Islamabad and therefore not a national picture, describes hierarchy-minded senior officers who struggle to manage people they cannot see, a so-called yes-man mentality, and communication run through WhatsApp. A Dawn reader letter in July 2025 calls remote work a distant dream for most of the workforce and says workplace culture still values physical presence, often for reasons that are not professional. Infrastructure compounds this: rural connectivity, power cuts and the cost of electricity at home all weigh on the idea. A 2022 column in The News cited a sample suggesting only about 10 percent of the economy could switch to off-site or hybrid work, and noted that most firms returned to full on-site work by August 2020, within weeks of trying a hybrid arrangement.

The commute supplies the practical argument that the policy has not. A Tribune opinion piece from March 2026 argues that a journey of about eight kilometres in Karachi can take an hour, and that some employees spend thousands of rupees a month on ride-hailing, which makes the case for flexible start times and remote days in IT, finance and design. The piece is an argument and not a study, and it names no national data, but anyone who has queued at a Karachi junction will recognise its arithmetic. Firms, for the most part, have not yet been persuaded that a late arrival and a poor hire are different problems.

## Canada: Plenty of Policy, Shrinking Appetite

Statistics Canada's figures chart the rise and retreat of remote work with some precision. About 7.1 percent of workers mostly worked from home in May 2016, roughly 41 percent did in April 2020, and by November 2023 the figure had settled at 19.7 percent. Data for May 2025, reported by BNN Bloomberg, show 17.4 percent of employed people mostly working from home, down from 18.7 percent a year earlier, while 77.6 percent worked only outside the home and 5.1 percent were hybrid. Commuter numbers rose for the fourth consecutive year, and Ontario announced that its public servants would be back in the office five days a week by January 2026.

Workers, meanwhile, have not changed their minds as quickly as employers. In an Angus Reid Institute survey of 1,622 Canadian adults from February 2023, 51 percent of employed respondents preferred more time at home and 49 percent preferred more time in the office. Among those who work from home at least some of the time, 31 percent said they would consider switching jobs if ordered back, and 21 percent said they would likely resign shortly afterwards. The same survey found that 84 percent rated connection to colleagues positively on-site, against 50 percent at home, while 81 percent rated work-life balance positively at home, against 54 percent on-site. Canada, in other words, has data supporting both sides of the argument and employers have chosen the side that comes with a lease.

Statistics Canada also asked employees who usually work from home what they wanted. In August 2023, about 24 percent said they would prefer to work more hours from home, about 65 percent would keep their current arrangement, and about 12 percent would prefer fewer hours at home. That is not a revolt. It is a settled majority that has found a compromise, which is precisely the kind of workforce that discovers its compromise has been withdrawn by memorandum. The hybrid share itself was 11.7 percent of employees in November 2023, up from 3.6 percent in January 2022, while exclusive home working fell from 24.3 percent to 12.6 percent over the same period.

Hofstede Insights scores Canada at 39 on power distance and 72 on individualism, a profile consistent with managers who expect to be questioned and employees who expect to negotiate their arrangements. Pakistan's side of that comparison is better documented by the PIDE brief, whose interviewees describe deference and senior officers accustomed to commanding, which is a recognisably different management temperature.

## The Reckoning

The honest comparison is between a framework without a culture and a culture without a framework. Canada has legal clarity, regulated employment status and a measurable workforce, but a steady drift toward the office that its own surveys suggest workers resent. Pakistan has demonstrated, by earning 1.76 billion dollars in freelancer exports, that people can deliver from a laptop, yet it has not written down what that work is, who protects it or how it is taxed, and its ordinary offices still tend to equate attendance with commitment.

For a mover, the useful question is which kind of flexibility you need. A freelancer or contractor serving foreign clients will find Pakistan's model workable but undefended, and Canada's tax and employment rules will feel stricter but clearer. An employee seeking a hybrid arrangement will find Canada able to write it into a contract, with the risk that the contract is later renegotiated by a return-to-office memo. In Pakistan, the same arrangement may depend on a manager's temperament and the reliability of the local power grid.

> *Blind — A US-based engineer wanted to spend three to six months a year in Pakistan to care for ageing parents. Replies suggested asking HR first, checking tax implications and policy on equipment abroad, and warned against using a VPN to hide location.*

> *Dawn reader letter — A writer argued that for most Pakistanis remote work is a distant dream, citing unreliable rural internet, shared household devices, restrictions on women's access to technology and management that equates presence with performance.*

> *The News International — A younger Lahore worker described fuel savings in the spring of 2020 and fast adaptation to new software, but also longer hours, higher summer power bills, costlier mobile data and HR notices announcing no raises or Eid bonuses.*

> *Dawn opinion — A writer who had worked remotely in earlier years and taught on Zoom noted the isolation of remote work, and argued that rising fuel costs and household electricity bills make stipends a fair request.*

## Conclusion

Neither country has solved remote work. Pakistan has the talent and the foreign earnings but offers little in the way of definitions, protections or consistent employer policy, so flexibility is something you negotiate and then hope survives. Canada offers contracts, regulation and measurable norms, but the direction of travel since 2020 is toward more days in the building, and a hybrid promise is only as durable as the next executive memo.

Before moving, ask for the attendance policy in writing, and ask what happens when the policy changes. Ask about internet, power and tax as seriously as you would ask about salary. It is also worth asking who gets to define flexibility, since in Pakistan the answer is often a manager and in Canada it is increasingly a head office. Flexibility, like the office kettle, is available to all and fought over by those who know where the good one is.
