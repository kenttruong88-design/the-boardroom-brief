# Suits in Budapest, Thongs in Sydney: Hungary and Australia Can't Agree on a Dress Code

🇭🇺 Hungary · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Budapest, a new hire who shows up to a client meeting in sneakers is making a statement, and not the one they intended. In Sydney, a new hire who shows up in a full suit is making an equally loud one, usually translated by colleagues as "job interview" or "funeral." Hungary scores 46 on Hofstede's power-distance index to Australia's 36, a ten-point gap that sounds trivial until you realize it is the difference between a workplace where the org chart dresses itself every morning and one where almost nobody can tell who the boss is by looking, right up until the invoice needs signing.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇺 Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark, conservative suit to first meetings with clients or senior leadership | Assume the relaxed day-to-day dress code applies to your first week on the job |
| Address colleagues and superiors with formal titles and surnames until invited to switch | Start using first names unprompted, even if your new boss seems easygoing |
| Keep shoes polished and clothing pressed; tidiness still reads as seriousness | Show up in shorts, sandals, or visible gym wear, even in a Budapest August |
| Dress up noticeably for Monday planning meetings and anything involving senior staff | Treat "smart casual" as a licence for jeans every single day at a multinational HQ |
| Save your most casual outfit for the "Friday wear" many offices now explicitly allow | Wear heavy cologne, perfume, or flashy jewelry to a first client meeting |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask what the office actually wears, or watch your manager, before day one | Default to a full suit and tie unless you're in law, finance, or client-facing banking |
| Treat a collared shirt and clean trousers as the safe, all-purpose baseline | Wear a tie unprompted; in most offices it reads as overdressed, not respectful |
| Dress more formally for the interview than you ever will for the actual job | Assume relaxed clothing means relaxed deadlines; the standards didn't get casual too |
| Save thongs (flip-flops) and boardshorts for the beach, not the office | Comment on a colleague's outfit; directness has limits even in a casual culture |
| Expect pockets of real formality — insurance, legal, banking — inside the casual norm | Show up scruffy for a client pitch because the internal dress code is relaxed |

### 🇭🇺 Hungary

Hungarian workplaces run on a quiet, well-understood hierarchy, and clothing is one of the ways that hierarchy announces itself. Hofstede's power-distance score of 46 for Hungary sits meaningfully above Australia's 36, and in practice this means title and seniority carry more daily weight: ClickUp's business-etiquette guide to the country notes that Hungarian business culture expects formal titles and surnames until you're invited to drop them, that decisions move top-down, and that attire runs "formal and conservative" — dark suits for men, tailored suits or conservative dresses for women — particularly in traditional industries and client-facing settings.

Day-to-day office life softens that formality considerably. Guidance aimed at interns and relocating staff describes most Hungarian offices as smart casual rather than suited, with management at smaller firms often dressing informally too; the bigger the multinational, generally, the more formal the baseline. What doesn't soften is the register with superiors: relationships with peers are casual and joke-filled, but even a relaxed boss is, as one guide to the Budapest work culture puts it, "still your boss," and that distinction tends to show up in what people choose to wear around them.

One structural reason dress norms still matter so much: Hungarians spend more time in the office than most of their EU peers. A September 2025 survey from GKI Economic Research, citing the Hungarian Central Statistical Office (KSH), found that only 29% of Hungarian employees have any opportunity to work from home, and that the country's remote-work rate is roughly half the EU average. When most of the workforce is physically present most of the time, in-person dress and decorum simply get more airtime than they would in an economy where half the meetings happen in a kitchen.

### 🇦🇺 Australia

Australia's workplace culture starts from the opposite premise: formality is the exception that has to justify itself. With a Hofstede individualism score of 90 — one of the highest in the world — and a comparatively low power-distance score of 36, Australian offices tend to flatten hierarchy on purpose, and dress code follows suit, or rather, doesn't. The Australian Bureau of Statistics' August 2025 Working Arrangements release found 36% of employed Australians usually worked from home, with 59% of managers and professionals doing so, and roughly one in five employees working on a casual basis with no guaranteed hours — a workforce for whom "what to wear to the office" is often a part-time question at best.

Indeed Hiring Lab's 2025 analysis of Australian job postings adds useful texture: mentions of relaxed dress standards such as "smart casual" or "dress for your day" have climbed steadily since the pandemic, are about 3.2 times more common in highly remote occupations than in medium-remote ones, and are, oddly, most prevalent in insurance job ads — 11.6% of postings, well ahead of legal (3.6%) and accounting (2.9%). The ACT posted the highest regional share of casual-dress mentions at roughly 0.9%.

None of this means Australian workplaces lack standards; it means the standards are unwritten and situational rather than codified. Law firms, banks, and anything client-facing still skew formal, interviews still call for more polish than the job itself requires, and the lower hierarchy signals mostly affect how people address each other, not whether anyone is paying attention to how they look.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the difference is really about where formality is aimed. Hungary's formality is vertical: it tracks who you're speaking to, with titles, suits, and deference reserved disproportionately for superiors and first encounters, while peer relationships stay warm and informal underneath. Australia's informality is closer to universal by default, but it is conditional on sector and context — the egalitarian baseline holds until a client, a courtroom, or a slipping share price says otherwise, at which point, as more than one Sydney office has recently rediscovered, it can vanish fast.

For someone relocating, that cashes out as a simple rule of thumb. In Hungary, overdressing on day one is the safer mistake; the culture will forgive a suit that turns out to be unnecessary long before it forgives sneakers in front of someone senior. In Australia, overdressing is the more diagnostic mistake — not forbidden, just a quiet signal that you haven't yet worked out who's actually in the room, which, this being Australia, might matter more than what you're wearing to be in it. Either way, the first two weeks are an observation exercise more than a wardrobe decision: watch what the person one rank above you wears on a normal Tuesday, not what the employee handbook claims, and adjust from there.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An employee at Atlassian's Australian offices describes how the company's famously relaxed culture hardened after its stock dropped, shifting toward what they call "US style corporate culture with heavy performance review" — a reminder that Australian informality is a norm, not a guarantee, and can tighten fast once head office gets nervous.*

> *Vergemagazine — Adelina Wong, who worked in Hungary for about a year and a half, found the dress code far more relaxed than she expected going in: she wore jeans through winter and dresses in summer without issue, and says she can count on one hand the number of times she's seen a colleague in a suit the entire time she's been there.*

> *Expat.com forum — A senior American executive who joined an Australian company's board found that wearing a "very conservative business suit always" didn't spare her unsolicited comments on her appearance from board colleagues, and that she had to consciously smile more than she would at home so her directness, as an American, wouldn't be misread.*

> *Tales from a Small Planet (talesmag.com) — A U.S. Embassy family posted to Budapest reported the office dress code simply as "professional dress and Friday wear," varying by section and by whether staff deal with the public — exactly the kind of department-by-department detail they wished someone had spelled out before their first week, instead of leaving them to guess.*

### Conclusion

The practical packing advice is almost comically simple. For Hungary, bring one genuinely good suit, learn who outranks whom before you learn where the coffee machine is, and expect the rules to loosen considerably once you're inside the circle — the warmth is real, it's just sequenced after the formality, not instead of it. For Australia, bring one decent collared shirt, resist the urge to overdress out of politeness, and pay closer attention to the room than to the dress code, because the dress code is deliberately unwritten and the room, not the handbook, is where the real one lives.

Hungary dresses for the hierarchy it has. Australia dresses for the hierarchy it would prefer not to admit to having — right up until the share price gives it away.
