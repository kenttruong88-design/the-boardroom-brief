## The Nod That Isn't a Yes: Meeting Culture Survival Notes for India and Hong Kong

🇮🇳 India · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In India, a head wobble can mean yes, no, maybe, or "I am listening and will do absolutely nothing about this," and foreign managers spend years failing to tell the difference. In Hong Kong, the meeting itself may be unnecessary, because the decision was made over lunch and the calendar invite is mostly theatre. Both cultures will tell you, with total sincerity, that they value directness. Neither does, in quite the way the relocation brochure implies, and the gap between what gets said in a meeting and what actually happens afterward is where most new arrivals lose a quarter of their first year.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address the most senior person in the room first, even if they say nothing for the whole meeting | Assume a "yes" or a nodding head means agreement rather than acknowledgment |
| Follow up any verbal agreement with a written email summary, same day | Take a flat "no" at face value when a hedge was almost certainly intended |
| Build in slack for meetings to start fifteen to thirty minutes late | Schedule a hard 6pm end time if a US or European client is on the call |
| Use "Dr.," "Sir," or full professional titles until invited otherwise | Push back on a senior colleague's idea directly in front of the group |
| Expect the real decision to happen in a smaller, informal huddle after the big meeting | Mistake politeness for consensus — ask privately if you need a real answer |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive with an agenda and stick to the clock — meetings are expected to be efficient | Pad small talk at the start; get to the point within the first few minutes |
| Exchange business cards with both hands and a slight bow if meeting a client | Assume long office hours mean the meeting itself will run long |
| Confirm decisions in writing immediately — things move fast once agreed | Expect a straight "no" — disagreement is often signalled through silence or a change of subject |
| Keep a close eye on your phone for after-hours messages from Mainland or Western counterparts | Plan on leaving exactly at 6pm if your manager or client base is cross-border |
| Learn a few Cantonese pleasantries; effort is noticed even in an English-first office | Confuse Hong Kong's fast pace with Mainland Chinese or Singaporean norms — all three differ |

### India: hierarchy first, clock second

India's meeting culture is best understood as a seating chart that happens to involve conversation. According to Hofstede Insights' country scores (accessed here via a Cyborlink mirror, since Hofstede's own comparison tool returned a robots block), India scores 77 on power distance, among the highest on the index, meaning organisational rank is treated as a structural fact rather than an awkward relic. The practical result: the most senior person speaks last, is contradicted rarely, and can end a meeting by simply not deciding anything, which is itself a decision.

Timekeeping follows a similarly elastic logic. Punctuality is aspirational rather than enforced in most Indian offices, and meetings routinely start behind schedule without anyone treating this as remarkable. What is not elastic, according to ILO-derived labour data reported by The Swaddle and Business Today, is the workweek itself: Indian employees log among the longest hours globally, with time-use surveys showing salaried urban men averaging roughly 53 hours a week, and a large share of that load is now meeting-driven. An Atlassian "Workplace Woes" survey covered by People Matters found 84 percent of Indian knowledge workers believe they would be more productive with fewer meetings, 90 percent struggle to balance meeting load with actual work, and 66 percent say they regularly attend meetings with no stated goal. The meetings are not optional; they are simply not always meant to produce anything.

For anyone managing an India-based team from abroad, the operative skill is reading the room rather than the transcript. A polite "I will try" is a soft no. Silence after a proposal is not buy-in. And the real negotiation often happens in the five minutes after the official meeting ends, in the hallway, which is where you actually want to be standing. A written follow-up is not bureaucratic box-ticking here; it is often the first moment anyone in the chain commits to something on record, which is precisely why it should land the same day, before the verbal version of events has a chance to drift.

### Hong Kong: fast, blunt, and quietly exhausted

Hong Kong presents the inverse puzzle: a culture that prizes speed and efficiency in the room, layered on top of a workweek that refuses to end. The Hong Kong Legislative Council Secretariat's 2019 research brief put the median full-time working week at 44.3 hours and the all-employee average at 42 hours, longer than comparable advanced economies such as the UK, Germany, or Japan, with Singapore as the one regional exception. More strikingly, in six-day-week sectors like security, property, and food service, 44 to 45 percent of workers logged 60-plus hours weekly. The International Labour Organization defines anything past 48 hours as "excessive," which in parts of Hong Kong's service economy is simply Tuesday.

Inside the meeting itself, the culture is closer to its reputation: agendas are kept, time is respected, and small talk is minimal. Hofstede's data (again via the Cyborlink mirror, as Hofstede Insights' own page blocked automated access) puts Hong Kong's individualism score at a low 25 against a comparatively high long-term orientation of 61 — a culture oriented around group and firm loyalty, but pragmatic and fast-moving about the work itself. The catch, often reported by professionals in cross-border finance and consulting roles, is that Hong Kong's efficient in-room culture sits inside a workday structured around other time zones. Calls scheduled for the convenience of London or New York routinely run into the evening, and the official end of the business day is, in practice, a suggestion. Code-switching compounds the pressure: an English-first meeting can pivot into Cantonese the moment the discussion turns sensitive, a courtesy to colleagues that doubles as a signal, to anyone paying attention, that the real conversation has started.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trap: both cultures will insist, in the first week, that they communicate directly — and both are technically telling the truth, just about different things. Hong Kong is direct about the agenda and blunt about disagreement; India is direct about hospitality and deference, and oblique about disagreement. Mistake one for the other and you will either steamroll a Hong Kong colleague who expected you to get to the point faster, or alienate an Indian one by demanding a yes-or-no answer they were never going to give you verbally.

The irony cuts the other way too. India, widely stereotyped as the land of long, meandering meetings, actually runs a tighter data story on meeting fatigue than its reputation suggests — its workers are measurably aware of and frustrated by the problem. Hong Kong, stereotyped as the efficient financial-hub cousin, has the longer official workweek of the two and a service-sector overtime culture that the Legislative Council itself has flagged as a persistent policy problem. Neither place is lazy about work. Both are, in different registers, exhausted by meetings that exist to manage hierarchy or time zones rather than to make decisions.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/developersIndia — a recurring thread describes managers scheduling calls at 11pm or later so that US counterparts don't lose "precious" daytime hours, with the practice passed down from senior staff to new hires until it reads as normal, even as the client side of the call keeps to a standard 9-to-7 day.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a consultant at a Big Four firm in Hong Kong described evenings running past 8pm as routine, driven less by workload than by late calls with European and American teams and by colleagues who arrive closer to 9:30am and take long lunches, pushing the whole day later.*

> *r/managers — a manager described finding meetings with an India-based team hard to follow, citing the stock email phrase "do the needful" as a specific source of friction, and other commenters pointed to high-context communication and layered hierarchy as the likely cause rather than bad faith on either side.*

> *A SingleTrackWorld forum thread on the Indian head movement often described as a "wobble" collected several first-hand accounts from people who had worked with Indian colleagues or travelled in South India, including one who was repeatedly misread as asking a question when a sideways nod was, in fact, a clear yes — and another who noted the gesture's meaning shifts by region, which is exactly the kind of detail a pre-departure briefing rarely covers.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on communication style alone, the honest answer is that India will cost you time learning to read deference, and Hong Kong will cost you evenings learning to read urgency. Neither is worse; they are different taxes. The practical move is to stop treating "directness" as one skill and start treating it as two separate ones — knowing when silence is a no in India, and knowing when a 9pm message in Hong Kong is actually a 9am problem wearing a different time zone's clothes.

If a friend asked me over a drink which one to pick, I'd tell them to go to India if they want to get better at patience, and to Hong Kong if they want to get better at speed — and to budget, either way, for at least one meeting in the first month that leaves them completely unsure whether they got a yes.
