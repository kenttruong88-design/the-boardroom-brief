# Zero Income Tax, Zero Free Lunch: What It Actually Costs to Live in the United Arab Emirates or Australia

**🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The United Arab Emirates will not take a dirham of your salary in income tax, and Australia will cheerfully take up to 45 cents of every marginal dollar you earn. And yet a family relocating to Dubai can expect to spend more on a single year of health insurance and school fees than an Australian family spends on both put together, subsidy included. This is the quiet joke sitting underneath every glossy relocation brochure for these two countries: one has built a tax-free paradise that bills you for everything else, and the other taxes you to the bone and still makes you fight for a childcare spot. Neither system is a scam, exactly. Both are simply honest about where the money goes, once you know where to look.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your tenancy with Ejari (Dubai) or Tawtheeq (Abu Dhabi) immediately — no Ejari, no visa renewal, no DEWA connection | Assume your employer's "accommodation allowance" covers a one-year rent cheque; most landlords still want it paid in one or two instalments |
| Confirm your employer has enrolled you in mandatory health insurance before you need a doctor | Skip travel insurance for family back home — UAE cover rarely extends beyond the emirate it's issued in |
| Budget separately for school fees if you have children — they are rarely bundled into any "cost of living" estimate you're given | Buy property expecting instant residency; you need Dh750,000 equity for an investor visa and a full Dh2 million for the five-year Golden Visa |
| Negotiate rent renewal in writing every single year — the market moves fast in both directions | Pay a security deposit in cash without a receipt referencing the DEWA premises number |
| Keep a buffer for district cooling charges in summer — they can double a utility bill overnight | Expect Numbeo's averages to match a real 2026 lease; asking rents are commonly 15–30% higher |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for Medicare enrolment and the Child Care Subsidy the week you arrive, not the week you need them | Assume a "subsidised" childcare place is an available one — approved-care waitlists in cities often run months |
| Get private hospital cover early if you plan to stay past a year — the Medicare Levy Surcharge punishes high earners who delay | Rely on JobSeeker or any welfare payment to cover market rent — under 1% of listings nationally are affordable to recipients |
| Compare rents in regional centres near your city; commuter towns can be dramatically cheaper than the capital itself | Sign a 12-month lease without checking the bond — it's typically four weeks' rent, lodged with a state authority, not the landlord |
| Shop at Aldi or Asian grocers for staples — mainstream supermarkets mark up imported goods heavily | Underestimate how fast childcare fees are rising — official data show increases running roughly double the general inflation rate |
| Factor private health insurance into your monthly budget even with Medicare, since public cover excludes dental and has long specialist queues | Expect "free" public healthcare to mean no bills — gaps, excess fees, and specialist waits are routine |

The United Arab Emirates runs on a trade: no income tax, no formal unemployment benefit, and a social safety net built almost entirely for citizens, not the roughly nine in ten residents who are foreign nationals. Since January 2025, the Cabinet has extended mandatory employer-funded health insurance across every emirate, closing the gap that previously existed outside Dubai and Abu Dhabi — a genuinely useful reform, and also a tacit admission that without it, an expatriate with a ruptured appendix was on their own. Housing runs on a parallel logic: tenancy contracts must be registered through Ejari or Tawtheeq before a resident visa can be renewed, rents in Dubai rose sharply through 2024 and remain high into 2026, and the property market doubles as an immigration pathway, with a Dh750,000 stake buying a renewable investor visa and Dh2 million buying five years of not having to think about it.

The number nobody quotes up front is the all-in one: cost-of-living trackers cross-checked against actual 2026 Dubai leases put a single professional's monthly budget, rent included, somewhere between AED 16,000 and 24,000 — before school fees, before a car, and well before the district cooling bill that quietly doubles in August. Numbeo's published averages tend to undershoot current asking rents by 15 to 30 percent across popular neighbourhoods, which means anyone budgeting off a generic cost calculator arrives short. Parents feel this hardest: school fees sit entirely outside any "cost of living" estimate a recruiter hands you, and the emirate's own expat-focused journalism has documented families who found the schooling bill, not the rent, to be the real shock of year one.

Australia inverts the arrangement. Medicare, funded through general taxation and a dedicated levy, gives every resident and a wide circle of reciprocal-agreement visitors access to public hospital care and subsidised doctor visits — genuinely one of the more generous universal systems among wealthy countries. The catch, as the Australian Bureau of Statistics' own price data shows, is that childcare fees have been rising at roughly double the headline inflation rate, with the average full-time place now costing more than AU$36,000 a year before subsidy, even as government childcare subsidy spending is projected to exceed AU$20 billion annually by 2029–30. Subsidy and sticker price are rising together, which is its own quiet absurdity.

Housing completes the picture, and here Australia's welfare state runs into arithmetic it cannot win. Anglicare Australia's 2025 Rental Affordability Snapshot found that fewer than one in a thousand rental listings nationally were affordable for someone on the Disability Support Pension, that Youth Allowance recipients could afford essentially none, and that even a single full-time minimum-wage earner could afford well under one percent of listings — numbers that moved only marginally despite a rising national vacancy rate. The welfare state exists; it is simply losing a footrace against the rental market it was designed to cushion against.

The Reckoning splits along a line that Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data helps explain in a sentence: the UAE scores high on power distance and low on individualism, consistent with a model where the state provides little and the employer, or the extended family, is expected to provide the rest, while Australia scores among the most individualist countries in the dataset — which sits oddly alongside a universal healthcare system until you notice that Australians overwhelmingly vote to keep Medicare and still budget privately on top of it. Both countries, in other words, have built a system that matches their own cultural instincts about who should be responsible for whom; the discomfort only shows up once an outsider tries to budget for it using the instincts of a third country entirely.

The counterintuitive part is which system punishes patience. In the UAE, waiting rarely helps — rents and school fees are largely set annually and move with the market regardless of how long you stay, so year five can cost as much more than year one as year two did over year one. In Australia, waiting can actively help on the healthcare side, since the Medicare Levy Surcharge rewards early enrolment in private cover, but it actively hurts on the housing and childcare side, where every year of delay is a year further behind a market moving faster than wages. The "free" or "tax-free" headline in both places obscures a second, unadvertised bill arriving under a different name — insurance premium, district cooling surcharge, childcare gap fee, bond lodgement — and it is this second bill that actually determines whether the move was affordable.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *expat.com forum — a newly arrived software hire on roughly 5,000 AED a month was warned by several longer-term posters that this was survivable only by sharing a three-person flat, cooking at home, and skipping a car entirely; one poster bluntly called it a "poor class struggling life" rather than the glossy expat package advertised.*

> *expat.com forum — an Adelaide-based poster described budgeting around Aldi and Asian grocers rather than mainstream supermarkets, packing lunch to avoid city prices roughly five times higher, and treating a dentist visit as a planned expense rather than a routine one, since even basic private cover didn't make it cheap.*

> *expat.com forum — a long-term Canberra resident advised newcomers to commute in from a cheaper NSW town rather than live in the capital itself, and noted that reliable home-based childcare, rather than a centre, was what made two working parents' budget actually function.*

> *Gulf News feature journalism — an Indian engineer in Abu Dhabi, three years into raising his daughter there, described the adjustment as unexpectedly isolating for her despite a materially comfortable life, while a Filipina teacher in Dubai for six years said her children struggle to answer a question as simple as where they're from.*

> *Gulf News feature journalism — an American mother of three called school fees steep but said she was pleasantly surprised by the quality of early-years education, and watched her children pick up Arabic and a wider cultural frame of reference she hadn't expected moving in.*

### Conclusion

If the decision comes down to a single number, make it this one: in the UAE, the risk is front-loaded into a system you can at least see — rent, school fees, and insurance premiums that are steep but knowable in advance, provided you don't trust Numbeo's averages over the actual asking price. In Australia, the risk is structural and slower-moving — a genuinely good universal healthcare system sitting on top of a rental and childcare market that official statistics themselves describe as increasingly unaffordable, which means the real cost of the move is not what you're quoted on arrival but what the market does to you in year three.

Either way, budget for the bill that doesn't have a name on the brochure. If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: move to the UAE for the paycheque and the sunshine, move to Australia for the healthcare and the long run, and in both cases double whatever number the relocation consultant gave you, because that number was never the whole story.
