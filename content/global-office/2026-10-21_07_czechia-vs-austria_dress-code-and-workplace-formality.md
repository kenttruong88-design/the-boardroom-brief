**Vienna Will Judge Your Shoes. Prague Will Just Ask Why You're Still Wearing Them Indoors.**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Austria, a 2010 government dress code that simply asked civil servants to wear closed-toe shoes and long sleeves became a genuine national controversy, debated by fashion editors and former ministers alike. In Czechia, plenty of office workers keep a pair of indoor slippers at their desk, the way they would at home, and nobody considers this remarkable enough to argue about. Austria formalizes its formality until it becomes news. Czechia informalizes its formality until it becomes footwear.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a pair of indoor shoes or slippers at your desk — it's a genuine, widespread office habit, not a sign of an unprofessional workplace | Assume "business casual" means the same thing it does at home; recruiters report real confusion over where the line sits, especially for women |
| Default to business casual and watch what your specific colleagues wear before committing to a style | Overdress deliberately to signal independence or authority — one foreign instructor found conservative, formal dress read as "too independent" and cost her a teaching assignment |
| Keep clothes visibly ironed and neat; Czechs are specifically noted to register this detail | Expect the midday break to be purely functional — leaving around 11:30am for a sit-down lunch, beer included, is close to a national institution |
| Use formal address (and titles) with colleagues, especially older ones, even after years of working together | Assume dropping formal titles signals warmth; in many offices it's read the opposite way until you're explicitly invited to do so |
| Mark birthdays and name days with the expected small ritual — cake, open-faced sandwiches, handshakes — it's a genuine office custom | Underestimate how conservative the default office silhouette actually is; a 2010 dress-code fight over "business attire interpreted quite traditionally" still describes most workplaces |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to dark, well-tailored, conservative clothing for any first meeting — quality and fit are specifically noticed | Assume formality in Vienna means black-tie-every-day; actual full formal dress is largely reserved for the city's many balls and galas |
| Learn and use academic and professional titles with "Herr" or "Frau" until invited to drop them | Expect creative or startup offices, especially in Vienna, to follow the same rules as traditional corporates — those sectors run visibly more relaxed |
| Default to "smart casual" day-to-day if you're unsure — it's explicitly recommended as the safe, well-liked middle ground | Expect your home-market CV to translate without German; most employers want German speakers, formality or not |
| Expect a genuinely high uncertainty-avoidance culture (Hofstede scores it 70) to show up as dress-code consistency, not just etiquette | Mistake Austria's low power-distance score (11) for workplace informality generally — hierarchy shows up in dress and address even where it doesn't show up in org charts |
| Treat the care Austrians put into grooming and accessories as a genuine professional signal, not vanity | Show up underdressed to "just a quick meeting" — Austrians are described as noticing quality even in low-stakes interactions |

---

Czechia's dress culture reads as a study in low-key contradiction. Hofstede scores the country's uncertainty avoidance at a high 74 — a culture that prefers clear rules and established norms — and yet the defining workplace image, by multiple accounts, is office slippers tucked under a desk, the same instinct that governs a Czech home. The contradiction resolves once you separate comfort from formality: clothes are expected to be "ironed and neat," professional titles and formal address carry real weight (Czech distinguishes formal and informal address grammatically, and colleagues keep that distance for years), but none of that extends to banning indoor footwear. A 2010 controversy over a ministry dress code — which simply required long sleeves, slacks or knee-length skirts, and closed-toe shoes — drew pushback for being impractical in an overheated building, not for being too strict in principle; even critics agreed a t-shirt still wasn't office-appropriate.

Austria's formality is more legible and more performed. Hofstede scores Austria's masculinity at a notably high 79 and uncertainty avoidance at 70 — a competitive, rule-bound culture that, per multiple business-etiquette guides, defaults to dark suits, elegant understated professional wear, and real attentiveness to the quality of what's being worn. Power distance, oddly, scores very low at 11, which sounds like it should produce informality — but in Austria, flat hierarchy coexists with strict dress and address norms; you don't need a steep org chart to be expected to use someone's academic title correctly. The exception that proves the rule: formal, truly dressed-up attire is largely reserved for Vienna's genuinely enormous ball-and-gala calendar, not daily office life, which by most accounts runs closer to "smart casual" than black tie.

### The Reckoning

Here's the twist: on paper, Czechia should be the less formal culture (lower masculinity score, a wry, slipper-wearing office culture) and Austria the more rule-bound one (high masculinity and uncertainty avoidance, a 2010 news cycle over sleeve length). In practice, both countries converge on roughly the same outcome — conservative, traditional dress as the safe default — by two completely different cultural routes. Czechia gets there through social observation and the quiet penalty of being noticed for wrinkled clothes or presumed overconfidence. Austria gets there through genuinely higher-stakes cultural rules that occasionally become public arguments. The Czech route punishes you quietly; the Austrian route might make you the subject of a magazine quote.

Neither country, notably, rewards boldness. The foreign worker who dresses more formally than expected in Prague risks being read as arrogant; the one who dresses too casually in Vienna risks being read as unserious. The actual skill being tested, in both capitals, is the same: watch the room before you choose anything.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expats.cz ("The Girl's Guide to Surviving a Czech Office") — A research associate who'd moved from a U.S. office found Prague's read on "business casual" far looser than expected for women specifically, noting that "spaghetti-strap shirts and short skirts are completely acceptable here, as is some cleavage" — a contrast she hadn't anticipated given the country's reputation for conservative dress.*

> *Expats.cz (same feature) — A former English instructor who leaned the other way, wearing corduroy blazers and long skirts to teach business English, found the formality backfired: her students complained, her director questioned her style as "too independent," and she wasn't invited back to teach that section again.*

> *Talesmag ("Real Post Report," Vienna) — A diplomatic spouse who relocated for her husband's posting described Vienna as "more formal/nicely-dressed" as a broad rule, but said genuinely formal attire is "really only required for the balls," recommending smart casual as the safest everyday default — while noting that most local employers still expect German fluency regardless of how someone dresses.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Czechia to Austria, the real adjustment isn't dressing up more — it's accepting that the dress code here occasionally becomes an actual public debate, so treat any written policy as something people genuinely care about, not boilerplate. If you're moving the other way, understand that Czech formality hides in details — ironed clothes, correct titles, a slipper left discreetly under the desk — rather than in a visible uniform, so the mistake to avoid isn't underdressing, it's performing formality so hard that colleagues read you as trying too hard to be taken seriously. In both capitals, the actual rule is identical: dress like you already belong there, not like you're applying to.
