**France Will Legally Evacuate the Building So You Can Eat Lunch Properly. Pakistan Will Just Keep Bringing You Tea Until You Admit You Have Time to Talk.**

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇫🇷 France

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In France, 43 percent of workers spend over 45 minutes at lunch — the highest share of any country in a 14-nation survey — a habit with roots in an 1894 French decree that literally required workplaces to be evacuated during meal breaks, for ventilation reasons that had nothing to do with food at all. In Pakistan, the ritual isn't a scheduled block of time off the clock; it's tea, repeatedly, throughout the day, used specifically to create the kind of informal pause where hierarchy briefly loosens and actual conversation happens. Both countries have built an entire social infrastructure around refusing to let work talk happen without food or drink nearby. They just disagree on whether that infrastructure needs a dedicated hour.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept tea when it's offered, repeatedly, throughout the day — declining it reads as refusing the actual social gesture, not just the drink | Treat a tea offer as optional small talk you can skip to "get to the point" faster — the point often happens during the tea, not after it |
| Use formal titles ("sir," "boss") by default with superiors, especially in traditional or local firms | Assume multinational offices have fully dropped hierarchy; many Pakistani professionals describe genuinely adjusting over time to first-name culture, not starting there |
| Expect team bonding to happen in short, frequent, informal pauses rather than one scheduled weekly event | Mistake a flat-sounding team culture (no visible ego, shared workload) for the local norm; several professionals describe it as a deliberate adjustment from what they grew up with |
| Build relationships through repeated small gestures over time, not one big "team-building" day | Expect written invitations for every informal gathering; much of the bonding is ambient and assumed, not scheduled |
| Respect the country's very low individualism score (Hofstede rates it just 14) by reading group cohesion as the actual currency of office trust | Push for Western-style blunt, time-efficient meetings without the social preamble; it can read as cold rather than professional |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out real lunch time — French workers report the highest share of 45-plus-minute lunches of 14 countries surveyed, and it's treated as normal, not indulgent | Eat at your desk by default; it's seen as unusual, and working through lunch doesn't read as dedication the way it might elsewhere |
| Separate "shop talk" from lunch itself if colleagues steer the conversation elsewhere — it's a genuine norm, not evasion | Expect colleagues to join you for drinks after work the way they might at lunch; after-work socializing with the boss specifically is much rarer than the long-lunch culture suggests |
| Use coffee and smoke breaks (even if you don't smoke or drink coffee) as real venues for resolving tension and building alliances | Go over your boss's head to their boss, even informally; chain-of-command etiquette is taken seriously, especially at larger firms |
| Expect meetings to run long with real debate — directness and disagreement are normal and don't necessarily damage the relationship | Assume long lunches or the 1894-rooted lunch law exist to protect French cuisine specifically; the original reasoning was about workplace ventilation, not the food |
| Greet everyone, every time — "bonjour" to reception, colleagues, and the person in the lift is close to mandatory, with "bonne journée" on the way out | Skip greetings to save thirty seconds; the omission registers more than the time saved |

Pakistan's office social rituals run on frequent, repeated, low-stakes contact rather than one scheduled set-piece. Hofstede scores Pakistan's individualism at a striking 14 — one of the lowest in the world — alongside a power distance of 55 and a long-term orientation of exactly 50, a profile that predicts group cohesion mattering more than almost any individual preference, including, apparently, whether you actually wanted that fourth cup of tea. The specific mechanism — tea, offered and re-offered throughout the day — functions less as refreshment than as a repeatable social reset button, the kind of low-commitment pause that lets hierarchy loosen briefly without anyone having to formally schedule "bonding time." Professionals moving into more international or multinational Pakistani workplaces describe a real, gradual adjustment away from default formal address ("sir," "boss") toward first-name culture — not a switch flipped on day one, but a shift that takes months to feel normal.

France's rituals are the opposite mechanism aimed at a similar outcome: one long, dense, genuinely protected block of time rather than many small ones. The Edenred-sponsored survey found 43 percent of French workers take over 45 minutes for lunch, dwarfing comparable figures in the US (3 percent) and UK (10 percent), and the root of the habit is almost comically unromantic — an 1894 decree requiring workplaces to be evacuated and ventilated during meal breaks, following 19th-century hygiene theory, not a cultural reverence for cuisine that got written into law later. Hofstede scores France's individualism at a comparatively high 71, which makes the intensity of its communal lunch culture genuinely surprising on paper — a highly individualist culture that nonetheless treats a solitary desk lunch as borderline suspicious. The resolution: French individualism shows up in personal boundaries and directness, not in an aversion to shared ritual; those can, and clearly do, coexist.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast is frequency versus duration. Pakistan's social infrastructure is built from dozens of small, repeated gestures spread across the day and the relationship; France's is built from one long, legally-rooted, deeply protected block that happens roughly once daily and is otherwise left alone. Both cultures agree that work conversation shouldn't happen without food or drink physically present — that much converges completely — but they disagree almost totally on pacing. An employee trying to import Pakistan's rhythm into a French office would look like someone constantly interrupting with small talk; an employee trying to import France's single long ritual into a Pakistani office would look like someone opting out of the ambient bonding that happens in between.

Neither country, notably, treats the ritual as optional fluff. In both places, skipping it entirely — refusing every tea, eating alone at your desk every day — reads less as efficient and more as a quiet signal that you don't actually want to be part of the team.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Sporkful (food-culture journalism) — An American expat working in Strasbourg described colleagues actively separating lunch from shop talk, gently redirecting her with "let's talk about that later" whenever she tried to bring up work during the meal, and noted that the daily shared "coffee time" she now joins after eating often runs longer than many Americans' entire lunch break.*

> *Sporkful (same account) — She admitted the productivity and collaboration case for France's long, protected lunch ritual eventually persuaded her more than the health argument did, specifically after watching colleagues rally around a coworker following his father's death — support she credited directly to the relationships built over shared meals.*

> *Moniepoint company blog (named employee profile) — A Pakistani professional recalled that growing up, "we would call our superiors boss or sir" by default, and that he initially preferred formal address even on his first international teams, needing real time to adjust before becoming comfortable with colleagues using his first name.*

> *Moniepoint company blog (same profile) — He contrasted that upbringing with his current team's deliberately flat culture, saying "I love that there is No Ego at all" and praising a workload genuinely shared among colleagues — framing it as something he grew to appreciate rather than something he'd always expected.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Pakistan to France, the real adjustment isn't learning to socialize less — it's learning to concentrate all of it into one long, sacred midday block and otherwise leave people alone, because constant small check-ins that feel warm in Lahore can feel intrusive in Lyon. If you're moving the other way, understand that skipping the tea isn't a neutral time-saving move; it's opting out of the actual mechanism through which trust gets built, cup by cup, all day long. Ask, in either country, not whether colleagues are friendly, but when and how often they expect to prove it to each other — because the answer is never "never," it's just a question of rhythm.
