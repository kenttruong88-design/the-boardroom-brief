# Peru Invites You to the Cousin's Birthday; Chile Invites You to Stay at the Table After Lunch

🇵🇪 Peru · 🇨🇱 Chile

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Chile, according to a Randstad survey reported by Diario Financiero, 80 percent of workers have friends at the office. In Peru, the Bumeran study reported by La Republica says 87 percent believe work friendships strengthen their commitment to the company. Both countries thus consider the colleague a potential friend and the office a potential social club, which is a useful fact for the relocating professional and a slightly alarming one for anyone who regards lunch as a time to read a book.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to after-office gatherings and birthdays | Decline every invitation and wonder why nobody tells you anything |
| Greet everyone when you arrive and when you leave | Slip out of the room without a word |
| Thank your hosts before leaving a meal | Leave the table the moment you finish eating |
| Defer to senior colleagues, who speak first and set the pace | Open with your own agenda in front of the seniors |
| Bring a small gift to a home gathering | Arrive empty-handed at a family occasion |
| Let Peruvian colleagues initiate any cheek kiss | Treat personal questions about your family as intrusive |

### In Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay at the table for the sobremesa, the conversation after the meal | Finish your food and leave, which signals a lack of interest in the relationship |
| Accept an invitation to "once", the early-evening tea-time spread | Treat it as a snack rather than an acceptance |
| Greet each person individually on arrival and departure | Wave at the room in general |
| Begin with family and football before business | Open with the agenda |
| Show up on time for meetings, though not necessarily for parties | Assume lateness at a social event will be read as rudeness |
| Expect that managers may appear only at special occasions | Take a manager's absence from the after-office as a snub |

## Peru

Peru is a country where work friendship is both an asset and a hazard. The Bumeran study, reported by La Republica and Revista Economia in October 2025, surveyed 3,307 workers and 465 HR specialists across five Latin American countries. The reported Peruvian figures: 87 percent say work friendships strengthen belonging, 83 percent say they improve performance and motivation, 49 percent say friends would strongly influence whether they stay, and 53 percent have a friendship with a colleague outside work. About one in three felt betrayed by a work friend. Seven in ten say their company promotes friendships through events and after-offices. The two news reports disagree on small details, and neither gives a Peru-only sample size, so these figures should be taken as reported.

The Cultural Atlas describes the machinery behind this: trust, or confianza, is built through personal relationships, dinners and sports with partners, nepotism is somewhat common, and reciprocity of contacts is expected. A Gestion opinion piece from a bank executive in 2020 found that 78 percent of that bank's employees had at least one friend in the office, though the author also noted how little local data there is. An expat blogger writing about making friends in Peru says new Peruvian friends may invite you to a cousin's birthday after a short acquaintance, and that family and friend gatherings blur into each other, with friendships forming faster and more interdependently than in Europe.

The newcomer's task is to say yes, and to expect a return. The office is a social network with payroll attached, and the tradeoff for belonging is that betrayal also happens in a small circle. A third of Peruvians surveyed felt it.

## Chile

Chile's version is more structured and slightly more cautious. The Randstad survey found that 80 percent of Chilean workers have friends at work, 43 percent say their employer actively promotes friendships, 44 percent say it frequently organizes social activities such as after-offices, and 29 percent say birthdays are celebrated in a special way. Fifty-one percent say managers join social events only on special occasions. The same report noted concern that the Ley Karin, the country's harassment law, could dampen social events, though Randstad's chief executive argued that such events are an extension of work culture.

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data for Chile gives context for how the office feels: 33 percent of employees are engaged, against 30 percent for Latin America and the Caribbean and 20 percent globally, and 17 percent report daily loneliness, against 12 percent for the region and 22 percent worldwide. Chile thus looks friendlier than the global average and a little lonelier than its neighbors. The Expat.com guide to networking in Chile says socializing with colleagues is not just accepted but expected, that team lunches are a daily habit, and that leaving right after eating, and skipping the sobremesa, signals a lack of interest in the relationship.

What follows for the newcomer is a calendar that includes a lot of lunch. Chilean bonding is regular, ritualized and somewhat hierarchical, with managers appearing at the big events and not the small ones. A foreigner who leaves at 1:55 pm sharp will be recorded as efficient and unreachable.

The regional comparison adds some perspective. The Randstad survey that produced the Chilean figures also covered Argentina and Uruguay, where 81 and 84 percent of workers respectively have friends at work, so Chile's 80 percent is typical of the region and not an outlier, and its 44 percent figure for frequently organized social activities compares with 36 percent in Argentina and 33 percent in Uruguay. Chilean employers, it seems, arrange rather more than their neighbors. On the Peruvian side, the Expat.com networking guide says that lunch can bond colleagues but that declining occasionally is not antisocial, which is a useful reminder that the expectation is attendance with room for the occasional excuse. The Expat.cl guide to Chilean customs adds that being invited to once, the tea-time spread between about five and eight in the evening, is a small badge of acceptance, and that a 10 percent tip is customary in restaurants.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights, as recalled from the published scores, places the two countries close together: power distance around 64 for Peru and 63 for Chile, individualism around 16 and 23, and uncertainty avoidance around 87 and 86. These are collectivist, hierarchical cultures that prefer to know whom they are dealing with before they deal. Chile scores higher on indulgence, at roughly 68 against 46, which may account for the after-office calendar.

The practical contrast is in the style of closeness. Peru moves quickly into the personal, with cousins, birthdays and family introductions, and treats the friend as a business asset. Chile moves into the personal through ritual, with lunch, sobremesa and once, and treats the relationship as something to be earned over time. Both reward attendance. Peru punishes the cold. Chile punishes the early leaver.

For a mover weighing the two, the useful test is how you feel about being known. Peru's survey figures describe a workplace in which friendship is expected to carry weight, including the risk that a friend becomes a source of disappointment. Chile's describe one in which friendship is common but is arranged through events, lunches and a calendar that the employer helps to fill, and in which the harassment-law debate shows that even the arrangement is under review. Neither country expects you to be an extrovert. Both expect you to turn up, and to be seen turning up, at roughly the hours when the conversation happens.

## Conclusion

If you want to be adopted quickly, choose Peru, and be prepared to meet the extended family before your probation ends. If you want to be accepted gradually, choose Chile, and be prepared to sit through a great many lunches, none of which is optional in any way that matters.

In either country the right preparation is to accept invitations early, to greet people individually and to ask about family before asking about deadlines. Ask your prospective employer how often the team eats together and who attends. The brochure will describe a collaborative culture. The reality will be a long table.
