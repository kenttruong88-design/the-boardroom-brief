# Switzerland Will Discuss Your Rent but Never Your Salary, While Belgium Has Turned Remuneration Into a Treasure Hunt

🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Swiss people will happily talk about the price of a train pass, the cost of a lettuce and the merits of a half-fare card. What they will not discuss is what they are paid. According to The Local Switzerland, salary is a private subject: you should not bring up your own income or ask others about theirs, while everyday costs are fair game. Belgium is more forthcoming, but its salary conversation is conducted mostly in the language of vouchers, bonuses and cars, where the headline figure is the least interesting part.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Agree gross pay at the interview, before the contract is signed. Swissinfo says this is the standard sequence. | Don't expect a legal floor. Switzerland has no national minimum wage, though some cantons and industries set one. |
| Offer a range if you are unsure. Michael Page found 58 percent of Swiss job seekers prefer to discuss a range. | Don't bring up your pay at dinner. The Local lists it as private, as do Swiss commentators. |
| Use salary comparison websites. Michael Page found 53 percent of respondents rely on them. | Don't assume a raise conversation will be offered. Employés Suisse's survey found almost half of employees have no opportunity to discuss pay. |
| Ask whether a 13th month is paid. IamExpat says some employers pay one, and Swissinfo notes it is not the same as a bonus. | Don't count on a bonus. About one in three employees receives one, averaging 11,967 francs, per Newly Swissed. |
| Compare cantons before choosing a home. IamExpat cites up to 27 percent regional and local tax in Neuchatel against about 14.9 percent in Zug. | Don't treat the national median as your median. Zurich is 7,502 francs a month and Ticino 5,708, per Newly Swissed. |

### Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether the 13th month is included in the gross figure. An Expat.com poster called 13.92 months a standard Belgian structure. | Don't judge an offer by the gross figure. The OECD puts the tax burden for single workers near 40 percent, against about 25 percent on average. |
| Ask for meal vouchers. They can be worth up to 10 euros a day tax-free, and about 74 percent of workers receive them, per Partena via Brussels Times. | Don't assume a company car is on the table. Employers use them mostly for higher earners, particularly above roughly 80,000 euros gross. |

## Switzerland: A Nation Fluent in Prices and Mute on Pay

The numbers themselves are impressive, which may explain the reluctance to share them. Newly Swissed, citing 2024 Federal Statistical Office data, puts the median gross monthly wage at 7,024 francs, up about 3 percent from 6,788 in 2022. The bottom tenth earn under 4,635 francs and the top tenth over 12,526. IamExpat, drawing on the same statistical office, gave a median of about 81,600 francs a year for 2022. There is no national minimum wage, and gross pay is typically agreed at interview, so the conversation you have in the first meeting matters more than the one you hope to have in year three.

That second conversation is where the culture shows. A Michael Page study reported by Staffing Industry Analysts found that 30 percent of Swiss-based workers and candidates had asked for a raise in the previous 12 months, against 40 percent in Germany and France. Only 19 percent of Swiss job seekers proposed a specific figure, against 41 percent of Germans, and 18 percent skipped asking because they doubted the outcome, against 8 percent in the two neighbours. A Demoscope survey for Employés Suisse in 2025 found that almost half of employees had no opportunity to discuss salary at their company, and 54 percent of women did not expect a raise, against 37 percent of men.

What Swiss workers want instead of a conversation is telling. Michael Page found that 85 percent of respondents value a company pension plan and 73 percent private health or life insurance, while the Employés Suisse survey found that a quarter would take shorter hours at the same pay over a standard raise. The gender pay gap, according to Swissinfo, stands at 11.5 percent on average and widens at managerial level, which is the sort of statistic that silence tends to preserve. In tech, The Register reports a typical expected salary of 106,900 francs, with 66 percent of surveyed professionals saying they deserve more.

The author of Newly Swissed offers four explanations for the silence: envy, privacy, modesty and trust in the system. The practical consequence is information asymmetry, which employers can exploit and expats can reduce with a salary comparison website. Taxes, meanwhile, depend on where you live. IamExpat cites the same median earner facing up to 27 percent regional and local tax in Neuchatel and about 14.9 percent in Zug, so the real negotiation may be conducted with a canton.

## Belgium: Where the Gross Salary Is the Opening Bid

According to the OECD's Taxing Wages 2026, reported by the Brussels Times, the average gross wage in Belgium was 62,348 euros in 2025, up from 60,370 the year before. After inflation of about 3 percent, real growth was only 0.3 percent, which only Austria undercut within the EU. Single workers without children face a tax burden close to 40 percent, among the highest in the OECD, against an OECD average of about 25 percent. For a one-earner couple with one child the figure falls to 19.9 percent. The tax system, in other words, has opinions about family structure.

Employers respond by moving pay out of the payslip. According to the Brussels Times, extra-legal benefits are used mostly to offset the tax burden on higher earners. Meal vouchers, worth up to 10 euros a day and free of tax and social security, reach roughly 74 percent of workers. Company cars can be used privately, which makes them a prized benefit, and earners above roughly 80,000 euros gross are more likely to get one. There are also eco-vouchers, a bicycle allowance, home-office reimbursements and CCT 90 bonuses of up to 4,255 euros. Even the official alternative to the car, the mobility budget, reached only 0.43 percent of employees in 2024.

Adjusted for purchasing power, the Brussels Times notes, Belgium's average of 87,530 dollars ranks seventh in Europe, and the gap between OECD countries narrows once prices are considered. That is a respectable showing for a country whose workers see a lot of their pay leave before they do.

Negotiation is allowed, and expats on Expat.com consider it normal, but it is a negotiation over a basket. One poster described a 54,000 euro offer with end-of-year premium, holiday pay, a car and meal vouchers as a standard Belgian structure of 13.92 months. Another estimated that 3,800 euros gross a month would leave about 2,300 net. The skill involved is closer to arithmetic than to assertiveness.

## The Reckoning

The Swiss problem is silence. Salaries are high, taxes in many cantons are moderate, and the individual worker negotiates alone, in a culture where asking for more is statistically less common than in the neighbouring countries and talking about it is considered poor form. If you do not push for a range or a figure at the interview, the system will not volunteer an improvement. The Belgian problem is complexity. Pay is more openly discussable, but the headline figure is shaped by a tax wedge near 40 percent for singles, so the winning move is to negotiate the structure rather than the number.

Neither approach is irrational. Swiss reticence is a rational response to a market where information is thin and trust in the system is high. Belgian packaging is a rational response to a tax system that rewards benefits in kind. For the newcomer, the difference is practical. In Switzerland you need data and a range. In Belgium you need a checklist and, according to some forum advice, a calculator run by a trade union.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a worker offered a Belgian package of 54,000 euros plus year-end premium, holiday pay, company car and meal vouchers asked whether it was enough for a family. Replies called it a standard structure, warned that Belgian tax is high, and pointed to the expat tax regime as a possible help.*

> *Expat.com — in a separate thread, posters said negotiating in Belgium is acceptable but depends on company culture, and advised looking at the whole package, including vouchers, insurance and transport, instead of the gross figure alone.*

> *Teamblind — in a thread about a headline 42 percent tax rate for a Zurich salary, several posters said the calculator overstated it, that employer contributions inflate the figure, and that deductions for commuting and children can cut the real bill considerably.*

> *DEV Community — a Swiss developer described money as a sensitive topic where earning well is fine but emphasising it is frowned on, and advised setting salary expectations with the company early.*

> *Newly Swissed — a young Swiss writer called the contrast between constant talk about prices and total silence about income one of the most fascinating features of Swiss culture.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Switzerland, prepare like a statistician: know the median for your canton and occupation, bring a range, ask about the 13th month and the pension plan, and settle your gross pay at the interview, because you may not get a formal second chance. Do not expect colleagues to help you benchmark, since they will talk about the price of cheese instead.

If you are moving to Belgium, prepare like an accountant: ask what is included in the gross figure, what comes as vouchers or a car, and whether you qualify for expat tax status. Then check the net. The bottom line is that Switzerland hides the number and Belgium hides the number under several other numbers.
