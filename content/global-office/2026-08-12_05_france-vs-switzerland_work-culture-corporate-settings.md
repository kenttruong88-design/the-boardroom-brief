**France Will Debate Your Idea to Death. Switzerland Will Schedule a Meeting About Scheduling the Meeting.**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In France, the average employee works roughly 1,491 hours a year — well under Switzerland's 1,529 and nearly 250 hours shy of the OECD average of 1,736 (OECD) — and yet French professionals will still answer your email at 9pm on a Sunday, calmly, without complaint, and without expecting a raise for it. Switzerland, meanwhile, pays some of the highest corporate salaries in Europe and asks in return that you never, under any circumstances, be two minutes late to a meeting whose agenda was finalized eleven days in advance. Neither country works less because it is lazier or more because it is virtuous; they have simply organized the same problem — how much of yourself belongs to the company — around opposite defaults. This is the practical guide to surviving both.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet each colleague individually every morning — a handshake or *bise*, not a group nod | Walk past people to your desk without acknowledgment; it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Take your full lunch hour, away from your screen | Eat a sandwich at your keyboard — colleagues will notice and quietly judge |
| Route a new idea through your manager privately before raising it in a group meeting | Pitch a bold change cold, in front of the room, on day one |
| Use every RTT and vacation day without apology | Assume unused leave signals dedication — it signals poor planning |
| Negotiate hard before you sign, especially on salary | Expect a raise to materialize later; asking can feel taboo once you're in |
| Budget for a long goodbye if you resign | Assume two weeks' notice — French notice periods often run two to three months |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive at meetings a few minutes early, agenda read in advance | Show up "on time," which in Swiss terms already means late |
| Address people formally (*Sie* / *vous*) until invited to switch | Assume fluent English or an early first-name basis makes you an insider |
| Let competence, not charisma, do the talking | Oversell an achievement — understatement reads as credibility |
| Respect evenings and weekends as genuinely off-limits | Message colleagues after hours expecting a same-day reply |
| Join a club, league, or association if you want a social life with coworkers | Wait for after-work drinks culture to happen on its own — it largely doesn't |
| Expect thorough, consensus-built decisions | Push for a fast, unilateral call — someone will want to sign off first |

**France**

France scores high on Hofstede's power-distance dimension, and the French-speaking parts of Switzerland track closely behind it — both cultures treat organizational hierarchy as a fact of nature rather than something to apologize for, in contrast to the flatter, consensus-driven instincts of German-speaking Switzerland (Hofstede Insights). In practice, that means the French org chart is less a diagram than a weather system: everyone knows which way authority flows, and you route around it rather than through it. One American who spent two years in a Parisian corporate job put it plainly — the office felt less individually competitive than her US jobs, more team-first, with advancement tied to collective results rather than personal hustle, and colleagues who worked late without the American assumption that overtime buys you a promotion (Business Insider).

That same account is a useful corrective to the "French don't work hard" stereotype: her colleagues answered weekend emails and stayed late just as often as her American ones, minus the martyrdom. What genuinely sets France apart is job security and its side effects. The CDI (permanent contract) is prized precisely because it is hard to walk into and hard to walk out of — trial periods are treated as a real threshold worth celebrating, resignations trigger notice periods that can stretch to three months, and asking for a raise mid-tenure can feel like requesting a favor rather than a negotiation (Business Insider). Deloitte's own French operation — ranked the country's fourth-best employer in 2025 after a Great Place to Work survey returned 72% positive responses — still describes a structure running strictly from analyst to principal, evidence that even the firms selling flexibility keep the ladder intact (Deloitte France).

**Switzerland**

Swiss corporate culture runs on precision, punctuality, and what Expatica calls, without much romance, "directness" — clear agendas, formal correspondence, decisions built through consensus rather than declared from the top, even inside famously hierarchical firms (Expatica). The consensus-building is not theater: reaching agreement is genuinely expected to take longer than it would in a more top-down culture, because nobody wants to be the single name attached to a decision that goes wrong. That habit shows up in the country's Hofstede masculinity score of 70 — a success-oriented, achievement-driven profile that coexists, oddly, with a workplace that rewards patience and procedural thoroughness over speed (Hofstede Insights).

Switzerland also works more hours than France — 1,529 annually versus 1,491 — while still landing well under the OECD average, and it pairs that with a legal minimum of four weeks' leave plus roughly nine public holidays, which sounds generous until you notice the leave is respected precisely because so little else about the calendar bends (OECD). The harder adjustment for newcomers is social, not procedural: Switzerland does not really do after-work drinks, colleagues who seem reserved in month one often stay reserved through month six, and the country has ranked as low as 61st out of 67 nations surveyed on friendliness toward foreigners — a statistic that coexists uneasily with its reputation as one of the best places in the world to build a career.

**The Reckoning**

Put the two side by side and the paradox sharpens: France works fewer hours but demands more of your emotional register — you're expected to know a colleague's family situation, their politics, their weekend plans, and to route ambition through personal deference to a boss who is, in Hofstede's terms, comfortable being deferred to. Switzerland works slightly more hours and demands almost none of your emotional register at all — competence is the entire currency, small talk is optional, and the hierarchy operates through procedure and sign-off chains rather than personal loyalty. France's hierarchy is felt; Switzerland's is filed.

The counterintuitive lesson for anyone choosing between them is that the country with the shorter official workweek is the one that will ask more of your personality, and the country with the fatter paycheck is the one that will ask the least of it. Choose France if you want your job to feel like a relationship. Choose Switzerland if you want your job to feel like a well-run system that happens to pay you extremely well for showing up on time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/france — Pitched a process change directly in an all-hands during his first month, expecting the open-floor style he'd known elsewhere. Got quietly pulled aside afterward and told, kindly, to run ideas past his manager privately first — the meeting wasn't the place to introduce something new, it was the place to ratify what had already been discussed upstream.*

> *Quora — A commenter working with French colleagues noted the feedback culture skews critical by design: managers flag what's wrong and rarely mention what went right, which reads as harsh to anyone expecting the "compliment sandwich" standard elsewhere.*

> *Blind — An engineer who moved from a US tech company to a Geneva-based employer described a routine expense approval bouncing between four people over two weeks, because nobody in the chain wanted to be the sole signature on the decision.*

> *r/expats — Swiss coworkers seemed frosty for months, distant even in the kitchen, until this person joined a local sports club — the actual venue for Swiss socializing, since after-work drinks culture barely exists — after which the same colleagues turned out to be generous with time and genuinely warm.*

> *Internations — Advice from a longtime member: don't take fluent English or a friendly tone as an invitation to first names — wait to be asked. Also worth noting how many Swiss colleagues have been at the same company for a decade or more; it recalibrates what "commitment" is supposed to look like before you've said a word.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for anyone weighing these two is to stop asking which culture is "better" and start asking which currency you'd rather be paid in: France pays in belonging — slower career mobility, real bureaucracy, but colleagues who will eventually know your children's names — while Switzerland pays in precision and salary, with a social contract that respects your weekend far more than it respects your need for small talk. Neither is the relaxed European utopia sold in relocation brochures; both simply relocated the exhausting part somewhere you weren't looking.

If you need one rule to pack: in France, learn who to ask before you propose anything; in Switzerland, learn to enjoy your own company, because nobody is inviting you for a drink.
