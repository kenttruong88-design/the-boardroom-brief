**Dress For the Job You Have, Not the Job You'd Have in Johannesburg**

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇸🇬 Singapore

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Fly from Cape Town to Singapore and you will swap a hoodie-and-sneakers "creative casual" office for one where a foreign colleague once needed a full afternoon just to decode why everyone kept calling her "Ma'am" instead of her name — two English-speaking, common-law, business-friendly economies that somehow arrived at completely opposite answers to the question of what a professional adult should wear, and say, to work.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your specific office about dress code before day one — it varies wildly by city and sector, not just company | Assume Cape Town's jeans-and-trainers norm travels north to Johannesburg's blazers-and-heels finance crowd |
| Keep a "client blazer" at your desk for presentations, even in casual offices | Wear it to a braai-adjacent Friday team lunch — you will be the only one |
| Default to "smart casual" until you've read the room for a week | Over-dress on day one in a way that reads as trying too hard |
| Address senior colleagues formally (Mr/Ms/Dr) until invited to use first names | Assume flat, first-name-basis American-style informality applies from the jump |
| Budget real time for small talk before meetings start | Launch straight into the agenda — it reads as cold, not efficient |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear long sleeves, closed shoes, and err formal for first meetings with clients or seniors | Wear shorts, slippers, or anything beach-adjacent, even in 32°C heat |
| Treat "ASAP" requests as genuinely urgent unless told otherwise | Assume a tight deadline is negotiable just because no one explains why it's tight |
| Let seniors speak first in meetings, and wait to be asked for your opinion as a junior | Jump in to "challenge" an idea in front of the room — raise it privately afterward instead |
| Keep a jacket in your bag for client-facing days even if the office itself is business casual | Rely on the office's own casual Friday norm to predict what a client site visit requires |
| Expect replies outside office hours and reply reasonably promptly yourself | Vanish after 6pm with no WhatsApp check — slower-but-visible beats fast-but-silent |

South Africa's offices run on a dress code dictated less by national character than by zip code and the local weather forecast. A widely read 2025 feature in Business Report described Cape Town's tech and creative sector as having normalised jeans, sneakers, and a hoodie as acceptable office wear, with a blazer reserved for client-facing days — while describing Johannesburg's finance and corporate law crowd as treating "power dressing" as close to a mindset, with tailored trousers and heels as the baseline, not the exception. Durban splits the difference, favouring linen and sandals that still aim for polish. None of this is written into law, but South African labour culture still carries real formality underneath the casual surface: the 2024 BusinessTech analysis of Quarterly Labour Force Survey data found South African men average roughly 44 hours a week and women roughly 40, a gap that has narrowed only slightly since 2015, and the transport sector clocks the longest hours of any at 49.

Hierarchy in South African offices tends to be respected rather than performed. A recurring theme in TalesMag's diplomatic-community archive — posts spanning 2009 to 2022, long enough to track a real trend — describes dress expectations softening over that period from universal "coat and tie" to a default of business casual with a Friday dress-down, while deference to seniority (sometimes explicitly tied to how close a manager is to retirement) has stayed constant. The result is an office culture that looks relaxed on casual Friday and reads as traditionally formal the moment a senior walks in.

Singapore's formality is less regional and more structural. The civil-service-adjacent expectation of long sleeves, closed shoes, and a blazer on hand runs across sectors and survives the country's brutal humidity almost by sheer institutional will. Beneath the clothing question sits something the clothing is really signalling: Hofstede Insights scores Singapore at 74 on Power Distance against South Africa's 49, meaning the dress code is a visible marker of a hierarchy that is, by the numbers, genuinely steeper — juniors are expected to look the part of someone who defers, because in practice, they usually do.

**The Reckoning**

The irony is that both countries are "business casual" on paper and almost nothing alike in practice. South Africa's casualness is geographic and sector-specific — a Cape Town developer and a Joburg attorney are, functionally, dressing for two different countries. Singapore's formality is near-universal but shallow in a different sense: it governs what you wear and when you're allowed to speak, but office hours themselves (MOM's 2024 parliamentary data puts mean full-time hours at 43.2–44.5 depending on income band, having fallen from roughly 45 across the board since 2019) aren't wildly out of step with South Africa's 40–44.

What actually differs is where the formality lives. In Johannesburg, dressing sharp is self-expression — a blazer says something about you. In Singapore, dressing sharp is closer to an organisational uniform — it says something about your rank, and rank is the thing that is genuinely non-negotiable. A newcomer who treats Singapore's dress code as a fashion choice they can opt out of, the way they might in Cape Town, is making a category error that will outlast the first bad first impression.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, as reported by People Matters — A foreign graduate on his first Singapore job described being handed his mentor's overflow work with no real guidance, recalling being told "What do you think?" or "Figure it out yourself" whenever he asked a question, and said the resulting stress was "literally making me sick," down to unexplained hives and facial swelling his doctors couldn't otherwise account for.*

> *Must Share News — An Australian marketing professional who moved to Singapore said the hardest adjustment wasn't the hours but the flipped hierarchy: used to a flat Australian office where junior staff challenge ideas openly, she learned that in Singapore "juniors are expected to keep to the status quo and voice opinions only when asked," and that urgency at work is often performative — tasks marked ASAP that then sit unreviewed for weeks.*

> *ExpatExchange — A contributor's culture-shock thread for Singapore flagged the brisk daily tempo and strict punctuality expectations as the biggest adjustment, alongside how much social rank shapes ordinary office interactions — something they said was easy to underestimate from the outside.*

> *TalesMag diplomatic-community archive — One contributor's advice for Johannesburg newcomers was blunt: pack a coat and tie for client meetings, business-casual basics for every day, and hiking clothes for weekends, because "dress depends on your job and how close your supervisor is to retirement" as much as on any written policy.*

> *Business Report (Cape Town) — A Johannesburg-based observer summed up the north-south divide in local terms a newcomer won't find in any onboarding deck: "Capetonians dress like it's a Saturday morning braai, not a workday" — meant as mild professional side-eye, not an insult.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for a transfer, the honest calculus isn't about which office is more "chill" — Cape Town's casual dress code and Johannesburg's sharp tailoring both sit on top of real, respected hierarchy; Singapore's uniform formality sits on top of a steeper one, full stop. Pack the blazer either way, but in Singapore, pack your opinions for private conversations too. The thing nobody puts in the relocation deck: in South Africa, dressing wrong gets you a look; in Singapore, speaking out of turn gets you remembered. Choose your wardrobe, but choose your moment to talk even more carefully.
