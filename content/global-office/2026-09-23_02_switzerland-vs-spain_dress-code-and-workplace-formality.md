# The Suit and the Siesta: Dressing for Work in Switzerland and Spain

**🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇪🇸 Spain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![Switzerland vs Spain — Dress code and workplace formality](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-02-switzerland-vs-spain-dress-code-and-workplace-formality-hero)
*Photo: cottonbro studio via Pexels*

A well-pressed shirt in Zurich is read as a statement about your reliability before you've said a word. A well-pressed shirt in Madrid is read as a statement about your taste — and possibly your salary — before you've said a word. Both countries care intensely about how you show up. They disagree, sometimes violently, about what showing up well actually looks like. Switzerland's business culture treats formal, understated dress as a proxy for discipline; Spain's treats it as a form of self-expression that happens to also signal seniority. Move between them and you will, at some point, be either wildly overdressed or quietly under-scrutinized.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Overdress for your first two weeks, then calibrate down once you've seen what colleagues actually wear | Assume "business casual" means jeans — in banking and insurance it usually still means a blazer without a tie |
| Keep it simple, clean, and well-pressed — Swiss taste rewards understatement, not visible logos or flash | Show up in anything visibly wrinkled or worn — the Swiss read sloppiness as a proxy for sloppy work |
| Check LinkedIn group photos of your specific employer before day one — dress norms vary sharply by canton and sector | Expect casual Friday to mean casual — it typically still means no tie, not shorts |
| Default to muted colors — navy, grey, black — especially in finance and pharma | Overdo cologne, jewelry, or visible wealth signaling — modesty reads as trustworthy here |
| Ask directly if unsure — Swiss colleagues will give you a straight, useful answer, not a vague deflection | Assume the reserved dress code means a reserved culture everywhere — tech and startups in Zurich are considerably more relaxed |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in good shoes and a well-cut jacket — Spanish colleagues notice quality over label, and it signals you take the job seriously | Confuse "business casual" with sloppy — Spanish casual still means ironed and considered, never gym clothes |
| Expect formality to track sector — law, banking, and government stay suited; media, tech, and hospitality are looser | Assume the whole country dresses like Madrid finance — southern and coastal offices run noticeably more relaxed |
| Notice that ties have quietly disappeared from most banks in the last several years — don't over-formalize | Skip grooming details — well-kept shoes and accessories matter more here than the price tag of the suit itself |
| Match the energy of the room — Spanish office style leans elegant-relaxed rather than corporate-stiff | Wear shorts or flip-flops even in casual coastal offices during summer heat — comfort has limits |
| Let a slightly bolder color or cut read as confidence, not unprofessionalism | Assume no dress code means no standard — Spaniards still judge grooming and fit closely, just less rigidly than the Swiss |

### Switzerland

Swiss workplace dress is less about fashion than about signaling conformity to a shared standard — a fittingly Swiss preoccupation. Formal business attire remains the default across most sectors, and banking in particular still favors the suit and tie even as other industries have loosened slightly with casual Fridays. What's notable is less the formality itself than its purpose: appearance in Switzerland functions as evidence of discipline and trustworthiness, especially in finance, where a client handing over money wants to see someone who looks like they take the job — and the money — seriously. Hofstede Insights scores Switzerland at 58 on uncertainty avoidance, a moderate-high figure consistent with a culture that prefers clear, stable rules over improvisation, including rules about what to wear.

This preference for restraint cuts against ostentation specifically. Multiple etiquette guides note that the Swiss dislike extravagant displays of wealth and instead reward clothing that is simple, clean, and well cared for — visible logos or flashy branding tend to read as insecure rather than impressive. That norm holds even in Zurich's growing tech sector, where the culture is looser but the underlying value — understatement over display — persists in different form: less about the suit, more about not trying too hard to look casual.

### Spain

Spanish dress codes have real regional and sectoral range, but the underlying logic differs sharply from the Swiss one: appearance in Spain functions as a form of expressed self-respect and social competence, not primarily a signal of institutional discipline. Traditional sectors — government, law, large-city finance — still expect business formal attire, suits and dress shoes included. But the very formal style once typical of Madrid's banking district has genuinely relaxed; ties have not been mandatory at several major banks for years, according to business etiquette guides, a shift that would have been unthinkable a generation ago.

What replaces formality isn't casualness so much as elegant-relaxed: a clean, well-fitted shirt and good trousers, no tie, but absolutely no jeans-and-hoodie either. Managers in particular are expected to be well-groomed with quality, well-cut clothes, and attention to shoes and accessories is treated as a genuine marker of professionalism rather than vanity. The general guidance from etiquette resources is consistent: when unsure, dress slightly more formal than you think necessary, because being visibly underdressed reads worse in Spain than being visibly overdressed.

### The Reckoning

The countries converge on one point and diverge sharply on another. Both reward restraint over flash — a Swiss banker and a Madrid manager would both wince at a loud pattern or an obvious logo. But the reasoning underneath is different: Swiss restraint is about not standing out from an institutional norm, while Spanish restraint (within a more expressive baseline) is about taste and self-presentation as a personal competence, not organizational conformity.

The more useful practical divergence is where formality tracks. In Switzerland it tracks sector fairly predictably — finance and pharma stay buttoned-up regardless of geography, while tech stays loose regardless of finance's example next door. In Spain, formality tracks a messier combination of sector, region, and generation — a 28-year-old manager in Barcelona media and a 55-year-old partner at a Madrid law firm are, functionally, dressing for two different countries. Guessing wrong in Switzerland reads as not having done your homework. Guessing wrong in Spain reads as not understanding the room — a more personal, and in some ways more forgivable, mistake.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering why Swiss bankers "make such a fuss" about dressing formally even at a desk with no client contact, one respondent explained it as conformity for its own sake: in Switzerland, people do a great many things simply "because that's how it is done," dress included, regardless of whether the reasoning still applies to a given role.*

> *Blind — A thread comparing Google's Zurich and US offices described the Swiss office as genuinely laid-back day to day, but noted the culture was still surprisingly promotion-driven and "very American" underneath the relaxed exterior — the casual dress didn't signal a casual pace.*

> *Expat.com forum (Switzerland business etiquette thread) — A new arrival asked how strictly to interpret "business casual" at a Geneva employer and was advised by longer-term expats to check LinkedIn photos of the actual team first, since written dress-code language in Swiss job offers tends to undersell how formal the reality still is.*

> *Quora — Someone asked why Spaniards seem to favor "simple and cheap" outfits and got pushback in the answers: several respondents distinguished between everyday street style, which is genuinely practical and climate-driven, and office dress in finance or law, which they described as carrying real sartorial investment — tailored suits and designer shoes were the norm, not the exception, in their Madrid workplace.*

> *A relocation guide contributor (Expatica) described the disorientation of arriving in Geneva expecting Swiss efficiency to translate into casual pragmatism about clothes, and instead finding a workplace where a slightly wrinkled shirt on day one drew more comment than a technical mistake would have.*

### Conclusion

If you're packing for one of these moves, the honest rule is: in Switzerland, buy less but buy well — three or four genuinely well-made, understated pieces will serve you longer than a varied wardrobe, because the culture rewards consistency over range. In Spain, buy for the specific sector and city you're landing in, because "Spanish business dress" isn't one thing — Madrid finance and Valencia tech might as well be different countries. Priya's line for a friend packing a suitcase: in Zurich, dress like you're trying not to be noticed; in Madrid, dress like you'd be a little embarrassed not to be.
