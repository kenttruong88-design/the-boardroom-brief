# Poland Reports a 4 Percent Pay Gap and Czechia 18.5 Percent, and Both Insist the Real Problem Is Elsewhere

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two neighbours separated by a mountain range and a rather long history of joint border disputes report almost opposite verdicts on how they pay women. Eurostat's 2024 figures put Poland's gender pay gap at about 4 percent and Czechia's at 18.5 percent, against an EU average of 11.1 percent. A woman considering a transfer to Warsaw or Prague could be forgiven for reading that as a recommendation. She should read the footnotes first, which are where both countries keep their complaints.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask the employer to state the pay range up front, since pre-employment pay disclosure rules took effect in December 2025 | Assume the full EU pay transparency rules are already law; the transposing bill was still a draft |
| Ask how promotion decisions are made, because stereotyped hiring and promotion is the obstacle women report most | Treat a low headline pay gap as proof there is no ceiling above you |
| Check the share of women on the leadership team and the board | Be reassured by the national figure alone; boards at large listed firms are 18.4 percent women |
| Expect gender to be marked in every Polish sentence, as grammar and not as a verdict | Read grammatical gender as an insult, or its absence as progress |
| Ask about flexible work, which 20 percent of Hays respondents in Poland call a barrier when it is missing | Assume flexibility exists because the office has a beanbag |
| Learn enough Polish to follow the office conversation | Rely on expat circles to explain how local colleagues actually behave |

### Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the salary range and the pay of comparable roles, which four-fifths of Czechs say employees should be able to know | Wait for a transparency law; employers are not yet required to disclose ranges |
| Negotiate explicitly, because recruiters themselves say women often do not know how to ask | Assume a performance review will correct a gap on its own |
| Ask how parental leave returns are handled, as long absences are linked to fewer promotions | Plan your career around the 28-week leave allowance without asking about re-entry |
| Check which sector you are entering; the gap is 35.6 percent in finance and insurance and 28.4 percent in information and communication | Take the average of 18.5 percent as the figure for your own industry |
| Watch what your superiors wear and follow the dress code | Assume dress codes are neutral; some expat women describe them as objectifying |
| Document any discriminatory treatment as you go | Expect protection to be easy; 62 percent of Czechs say getting it is difficult |

## Poland

Poland's headline number is the envy of its region. Eurostat's 2024 data, as reported by Euronews, show a gender pay gap of about 4 percent against an EU average of 11.1 percent. Careers in Poland, citing Eurostat, notes that Poland ranks third in the EU for women in management, behind only Latvia and Sweden. It also records that women hold 18.4 percent of board seats at the 140 largest companies on the Warsaw exchange, according to the 30% Club Poland, which is a rather different sort of leadership. One country can be both a leader and a laggard if the statistic is chosen with care. A Hays survey of finance and accounting professionals offers a more cheerful detail, which is that 46 percent of respondents report a female direct supervisor, so the ceiling is not quite a ceiling for everyone, merely for those aiming at the top of the building.

Women themselves are less relaxed than the headline. Hays Poland's 2024 report found that 53 percent of women reported gender-related career obstacles, against 25 percent of men, and that 54 percent of those obstacles involved hiring or promotion decisions based on stereotypes. In a Hays survey published in March 2025, 46 percent named discrimination and bias as the main barrier to women's careers, and 22 percent named the pay gap itself. In other words, a low gap in average hourly pay and a high rate of reported bias are quite compatible, because the first measures what people are paid and the second what they are allowed to become.

The legal ground is moving. According to WTW, Labor Code amendments requiring pre-employment pay disclosure took effect on 23 December 2025, and a draft bill would transpose the rest of the EU directive, including pay reporting for employers with 150 or more staff. Euronews described Poland in June 2026 as having partially implemented the directive. Hofstede Insights scores Poland at 64 for masculinity and Czechia at 57, meaning a culture that rewards competition and achievement, which is accurate enough about the labour market and does not explain why the bonus meeting runs long.

## Czechia

Czechia's number is less flattering. Eurostat figures reported by Expats.cz in March 2026 show women earning 18.5 percent less per hour than men, the second-highest gap in the EU behind Estonia. The gap peaks at 21.6 percent for women aged 35 to 44, which is to say the years when children arrive, and reaches 35.6 percent in finance and insurance and 28.4 percent in information and communication. Economists quoted by the outlet pointed to a male-dominated industrial sector and limited part-time work. As of 2022, Eurostat data cited by Expats.cz showed that only about 10 percent of Czech women work part-time, and that parental leave runs 28 weeks, with longer absences linked to fewer promotions and a higher risk of demotion on return.

The attitudes behind those figures are measurable. The Solvo Institute's Femina Index, published in March 2024, gave Czech society a score of 53 out of 100 on emancipation, found that half of respondents believed women should mainly handle cooking, cleaning and laundry, and that two-thirds found it unusual for the mother of a six-month-old to return to work. A Median poll for the Justice Ministry, reported in October 2026, found that 29 percent of women and 17 percent of men had experienced discrimination or harassment in the past five years. Job searches were the most common setting, and 46 percent of women had met someone paid less than colleagues for the same work.

Czechia also sits at the foot of the board table. Expats.cz, citing Eurostat in 2022, put women at 13.8 percent of corporate board seats against an EU average of 25.8 percent, an improvement on the 9 percent of 2016 that still leaves the country well behind. The same outlet, citing the OECD, ranks Czech women 23rd among 33 OECD countries for labour market equality. Expat women are not spared. In an Expats.cz reader poll of roughly 90 to 100 respondents, about 70 percent saw good professional opportunities, yet only around a third felt paid fairly against male colleagues, and about a third reported harassment at work. The remedy is awaited rather than present. Czech employers are not currently required to disclose pay ranges, according to Expats.cz, and Euronews reported in June that draft legislation was only expected. Four-fifths of those in the Median poll nevertheless support an employee's right to know average pay in comparable roles, which is the one point on which Czech society appears to have reached a consensus. Jaroslava Rezlerová of ManpowerGroup told Expats.cz that women often do not know how to negotiate higher wages or find out what male colleagues earn, which is hard to learn from a pay structure that nobody publishes.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Poland is the easier place for a woman to be paid fairly and Czechia the harder, and the difference is large enough to survive any argument about methodology. Poland has the smaller gap, a stronger record in management and a pay disclosure rule already in force at the hiring stage. Czechia has a gap that is among the widest in the EU, attitudes that its own surveys describe as traditional and no disclosure obligation yet. If a pay figure were the whole of the matter, the answer would take one sentence.

It is not, and the Polish data say so. Hays finds that more than half of Polish women report obstacles and that stereotyped promotion decisions are the main one, while the 30% Club puts board seats at under a fifth. Czech women, for their part, describe a workplace with good opportunities and a pay structure that is difficult to question. The practical difference is one of tactics. In Poland, ask about promotion and leadership. In Czechia, ask about pay, sector and what happens after parental leave. Both countries reward the candidate who asks the question before accepting the offer.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience of a Polish-speaking commenter who explained that Polish grammar marks gender almost everywhere, so mentioning someone's gender there is not considered offensive in the way it can be in English, while acknowledging that there are real biases against women in Eastern Europe.*

> *ESL Cafe forum — paraphrased experience of a former Peace Corps volunteer who reported that female volunteers in Poland found the lack of companionship hard, along with pressure around age and Polish chivalry, though most enjoyed their service and were ready to leave when it ended.*

> *The Krakow Post — paraphrased experience of a Polish businesswoman and networking organiser who said women now match or outperform men in university enrolment yet largely disappear from management, because cultural expectations still assign them the household and men avoid paternity leave for fear for their jobs.*

> *Expats.cz reader survey — paraphrased experience of an expat woman in Czechia who described company dress codes as sexist and objectifying toward women.*

> *Expats.cz reader survey — paraphrased experience of an expat woman in Czechia who said that raising fairness for women drew hostile pushback, that everything seemed to revolve around men, and that Czech girlfriends had discouraged her from a doctorate on the grounds that men do not like that.*

## Conclusion

For a woman choosing between the two on pay alone, Poland has the better numbers and the more advanced rules, and Czechia has the worse numbers and a pending law. Before accepting either offer, ask for the range, ask who is promoted and how, ask about the leadership team and, in Czechia, ask what happens to your career after parental leave. Treat the national average as a weather forecast and the employer's own data as the thing you are actually standing in.

Neither country is a bad place to work, and the survey data on both sides report good professional opportunities alongside persistent bias. It is simply that in Poland the glass ceiling comes with a lower pay gap, and in Czechia it comes with a higher one, and in both cases the ceiling is apparently load-bearing.
