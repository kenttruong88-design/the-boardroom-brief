# Lunch Alone Will Get You Talked About in Seoul. In Toronto, It's Tuesday.

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Almost 40% of Canadians eat lunch at their own desk, alone, and nobody thinks twice about it — it's simply what a busy workday looks like. In South Korea, eating alone carries enough social weight that it has its own name, honbap, and until recently, doing it regularly at work could quietly mark you as friendless or difficult. Food, in other words, is doing two completely different jobs in these two office cultures: fuel in one, social infrastructure in the other.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to hoesik (team dinners) when you can, especially early on | Say no to a poured drink outright — pour it into a nearby glass or sip token amounts instead |
| Pour drinks for seniors with two hands, and let them pour yours | Turn your back when drinking in front of a senior colleague — turn slightly away instead |
| Expect lunch to often be a group activity, not a solo break | Assume "honbap" (eating alone) is stigma-free everywhere yet — attitudes are shifting but unevenly by generation and workplace |
| Order communally when dining with colleagues — shared side dishes are the norm | Rush a company dinner or leave early without checking the room first |
| Ask a Korean colleague which restaurants are considered "team lunch" spots — there's often an unwritten rotation | Assume this is purely social — attendance can genuinely be read as a work signal |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Feel free to eat at your desk — 39% of Canadians do, and no one reads it as antisocial | Assume a team lunch invite is mandatory — it's usually genuinely optional |
| Pack your own lunch if you want — 72% of Canadians do, it's completely normal | Expect colleagues to notice or comment if you consistently eat alone |
| Expect real regional variation — Quebec eats together more than Ontario does | Assume "Canadian food culture" is uniform — it varies significantly coast to coast |
| Use your actual lunch break (roughly 11:30am–2:30pm) — it's yours, use it or don't | Feel obligated to attend every office potluck or team meal — declining has no real social cost |
| Ask about workplace norms explicitly if your office is genuinely social — some are, many aren't | Assume eating quickly at your desk signals poor performance — it's often read as the opposite |

### South Korea: Eating Alone Used to Be the Exception

South Korea's food culture at work has historically run on shared meals as social infrastructure. Hoesik — team dinners, often involving multiple rounds of drinking — function, according to reporting from [This Life in Trips](https://thislifeintrips.com/understanding-hoesik-korean-drinking-culture-with-ongo-food-tours-review/), less as an optional social extra and more as an extension of the workday itself, complete with real hierarchy rules: pouring with two hands, not drinking before seniors, rarely refusing an offered drink outright. [Korea.net](https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/People/view?articleId=121988) notes that dining together signals unity so strongly that historically, eating alone could mark someone as having few friends or lacking social skills.

That's shifting. [KEIA reporting](https://keia.org/the-peninsula/table-for-one-how-dining-culture-is-changing-in-South-korea/) documents the rise of honbap (solo dining) as an accepted, even normalized choice for a growing share of younger Koreans, and some companies are actively scaling back mandatory hoesik as a recruiting incentive. Meanwhile the underlying eating patterns are shifting too: PMC-published research on Korean adults from 1998–2018 shows minimally processed food intake declining sharply across breakfast, lunch, and dinner, replaced by ultra-processed alternatives — with temporary workers skipping lunch at twice the rate of permanent employees, evidence that Korea's competitive, hours-heavy work culture is straining the very meal rituals it built.

### Canada: Lunch Is Yours to Lose

Canadian workplace eating runs on the opposite assumption: your lunch break is personal time, and how — or whether — you socialize during it is entirely up to you. A Dalhousie University study reported by [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/nova-scotia/eat-lunch-desk-canada-food-habit-study-dalhousie-1.2687449) found 39% of Canadians eat lunch at their desks, edging out the 37% who eat at home, with Atlantic Canada leading at nearly 50%. Seventy-two percent pack their own lunch. There is real regional texture underneath the national average — only 36% of Quebecers eat lunch alone, compared to 61% of Ontarians, suggesting Quebec's more communal food culture persists even inside the office.

Crucially, none of this desk-eating or solo-lunching carries the social read it would in Korea. Quora respondents confirm there's no norm against eating alone at a Canadian workplace — it's simply one option among several, shaped more by individual job pressure than cultural expectation.

### The Reckoning

The real divergence isn't what's on the plate — it's what a shared meal is for. In Korea, food has historically operated as a hierarchy-softening, trust-building mechanism that happens to also involve eating; skipping it wasn't just skipping a meal, it was opting out of a relationship-building system. In Canada, food is fuel, full stop, and the office imposes essentially no social meaning onto how or with whom you consume it.

Hofstede Insights data captures why this divergence runs so deep: South Korea's individualism score of just 18 sits at the collectivist extreme, while Canada scores 80, among the most individualist nations measured — though newer research cited in academic literature suggests Hofstede's classical framework may understate Korean individualism and overstate Canada's by a meaningful margin, a reminder that these scores are a starting sketch, not gospel.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved from a more socially mandatory office food culture described their first months in a Canadian workplace as almost disorienting in a good way: no one commented, tracked, or seemed to care whether they ate at their desk, in the break room, or skipped lunch entirely — the absence of scrutiny took longer to trust than they expected.*

> *r/korea — A foreign hire described their first hoesik as effectively a second job interview disguised as dinner — three rounds, a norebang stop, and a clear sense afterward that how you handled the evening had actually been observed and would be discussed.*

> *This Life in Trips (hoesik reporting) — One account described the specific etiquette failure that actually damages standing: not refusing a drink outright, which is manageable, but turning your body away from a senior while drinking it, a much smaller gesture that locals notice immediately and newcomers rarely know to avoid.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A Korea-based employee noted that younger teams increasingly treat hoesik attendance as genuinely optional, but cautioned that this varies enormously by company and manager — assuming the newer, looser norm applies everywhere is a mistake newcomers still make regularly.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding which culture to prepare for, the honest advice is about energy allocation, not etiquette memorization. In Korea, budget real social energy for meals — they are, functionally, part of the job, even as that expectation loosens generationally. In Canada, save that energy for wherever your actual work happens, because no one is grading your lunch.

What I'd tell a friend headed to Seoul: learn to nurse a drink slowly and pour with two hands, because the meal is the meeting. What I'd tell one headed to Toronto: eat at your desk if you want to, nobody's counting.
