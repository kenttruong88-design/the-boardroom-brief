# Stay for the Family Feeling, or Leave for the Better Offer

**🇺🇬 Uganda · 🇵🇹 Portugal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Portugal, a survey of working expats ranks the country 61st out of 68 for career prospects and 53rd for job security — and nearly three in five of those same expats still say they're satisfied with their job day to day. In Uganda, HR leaders describe a labor market where critical digital and transformation roles are "now being priced in dollars, not shillings" specifically because loyalty alone can no longer compete with what international employers are offering. Both countries have quietly concluded that the traditional bargain — stay long, get promoted — doesn't really work anymore. They've just responded to that conclusion in opposite directions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇬 Uganda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly about internal mobility and rotational programs before accepting a role | Assume tenure alone guarantees promotion — younger staff increasingly expect faster advancement |
| Notice who "steps up" during a crisis — it's an informal leadership filter here | Wait passively for recognition; initiative during high-pressure moments gets remembered |
| Negotiate for skills in digital, data, or transformation roles — they're increasingly priced in dollars | Assume shilling-denominated salaries are non-negotiable for specialized skills |
| Build a relationship with your direct manager, not just the company brand | Assume a strong mission statement reflects lived workplace culture — ask current employees instead |
| Expect informal networks and word-of-mouth to matter as much as formal job postings | Rely solely on job boards — referrals and visible reputation carry real weight |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come in with realistic expectations about the ceiling for career advancement | Expect the fast vertical promotion pace common in the US or parts of Asia |
| Value the work-life balance — most expats rate it genuinely positively | Sacrifice a stable, well-regarded job chasing a marginal title upgrade |
| Use recruitment agencies; they're a trusted, well-established channel here | Rely only on generic international job boards — local sites like Net-Empregos dominate |
| Treat freelancing or remote-first roles as a legitimate, common path | Assume everyone around you is on a traditional single-employer career ladder |
| Watch for job-scam red flags — the market is broadly safe but not scam-free | Wire money or share documents before verifying a recruiter's legitimacy |

### Uganda: Loyalty Is Negotiable Now

Uganda's labor market tells two stories depending on where you look. Nationally, unemployment sits around 12.2% with the informal sector still dominating overall employment (UBOS), and youth unemployment specifically has become what one local outlet called a "ticking time bomb" — a structural mismatch between the number of graduates and the number of formal roles. But inside the formal, urban, professional tier that a relocating expat would actually enter, the dynamic looks different: senior HR leaders describe a market now actively losing talent to remote and international employers, with one Stanbic Bank executive noting that younger professionals "want to grow quickly... sophisticated problems, complex environments, and leaders who will stretch them," rather than the patient, seniority-based advancement older employees once expected.

The result is a workplace culture in transition. Organizations are experimenting with "transparent internal marketplaces, rotational programs, leader-led development" specifically to keep people who would otherwise leave for a foreign-currency salary (CEO.co.ug interviews). Employee reviews of large employers like MTN Uganda echo the tension directly: a dynamic, inclusive, mentorship-heavy culture that people genuinely value, undercut by "low minimum wage relative to long working hours" and "limited development opportunities in some departments" as the most commonly cited reasons for leaving. Loyalty in Uganda isn't dead, in other words — it's increasingly conditional on the employer proving it's worth the wait.

### Portugal: Stability Over Speed, By Design

Portugal's career culture runs on a different logic entirely, one that shows up clearly in Hofstede's numbers: a Power Distance of 63, an Individualism score of just 27, and a remarkably high Uncertainty Avoidance of 104 — one of the highest in the Hofstede dataset, and, per the researchers who documented it, "the dimension that defines Portugal very clear." A workforce that avoids uncertainty that strongly does not, as a rule, job-hop for the thrill of it. It optimizes for predictability, and the InterNations Expat Insider survey data bears this out precisely: Portugal ranks near the bottom for career prospects (61st of 68) and job security (53rd of 68) among expat-relevant destinations, yet still pulls strong marks for work-life balance and overall job satisfaction — a genuinely unusual split where the day-to-day experience of work is good even though the long-term career ladder is short.

Part of the explanation is who Portugal actually attracts: the same survey notes roughly a quarter of respondents are retirees, alongside a large freelance and remote-work population — people for whom "career prospects" were never really the point of moving there. For anyone chasing traditional upward mobility, the practical playbook described by relocated workers is narrower and more local than expected: Portuguese-specific job boards like Net-Empregos, government employment services (IEFP), and recruitment agencies do far more of the real work than international platforms, and the job search itself often takes "weeks" of trial and error to learn, per one relocated professional's account, before it starts producing results.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive twist: Uganda, the country with the thinner social safety net and the more volatile formal job market, is where loyalty is actively being priced and renegotiated in real time — employers are building internal mobility programs precisely because staying quiet and staying put no longer guarantees retention. Portugal, a stable EU economy with strong labor protections, is where the market has functionally settled into a lower-mobility equilibrium — not because people are contractually locked in, but because a historically low tolerance for uncertainty (that UAI score of 104) makes the whole workforce culturally disinclined to gamble on a switch, even when the numbers suggest career progress has stalled.

Put differently: Ugandan professionals are job-hopping more *because* the market is unstable and opportunity is unevenly distributed; Portuguese professionals are job-hopping less *despite* limited career upside, because stability itself has become the reward they're optimizing for. Job loyalty here isn't a single axis running from "loyal" to "mobile" — it's two different responses to two different kinds of uncertainty.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey (Portugal) — Aggregated survey data rather than a single voice, but a striking one: expats in Portugal rank the country near the very bottom for career prospects and job security among dozens of destinations, and yet the same respondents report some of the strongest work-life balance satisfaction in the survey — a disconnect the report's authors frame as Portugal appealing more to retirees and freelancers than to professionals chasing traditional advancement.*

> *Worktugal (Substack, Lisbon job search account) — A relocated professional described the first weeks of job hunting in Portugal as pure trial and error — "I spent weeks clicking around before finding the sites that actually worked" — before landing on Net-Empregos and Indeed.pt as the platforms that actually mattered, plus a recurring warning that job-scam listings are common enough to verify any recruiter twice before sharing documents.*

> *CEO.co.ug (named trade-press interview, Kampala) — Sylvia Mulomi of Stanbic Bank Uganda described the generational shift in blunt terms: younger professionals "want to grow quickly... sophisticated problems, complex environments, and leaders who will stretch them" — a direct rebuttal to the assumption that patient, tenure-based loyalty is still the default expectation among Ugandan employees.*

> *Indeed employee reviews, MTN Uganda — Reviewers consistently praised the "dynamic" and "inclusive" culture and real mentorship opportunities as reasons to stay, but just as consistently cited "unbearable" work pressure at peak periods and frequent supervisor monitoring as the quieter reasons colleagues quietly started job-hunting elsewhere, even at a well-regarded employer.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two: Uganda rewards professionals who negotiate actively, show initiative visibly, and treat loyalty as something the employer has to keep earning — don't expect tenure by itself to get you promoted. Portugal rewards the opposite temperament: settle in, expect the career ladder to be shorter and slower than you're used to, and decide upfront whether work-life balance and stability are worth more to you than rapid advancement, because the data says you're unlikely to get both at once. The honest version for a friend: go to Uganda if you want to prove yourself fast; go to Portugal if you've already proven yourself and just want your evenings back.
