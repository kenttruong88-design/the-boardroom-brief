**Lagom Versus "No Worries": Sweden and Australia Can't Agree on What a Generation Owes a Workplace**

**🇸🇪 Sweden · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Stockholm, a 24-year-old can question her manager's decision in a meeting and be thanked for her candor. In Sydney, a 24-year-old can call his manager by first name, crack a joke about the boss's golf handicap, and still be the last person told about the redundancy round. Both countries market themselves as allergic to hierarchy. Only one of them actually is, and it is not the one with the bigger backyard. Korn Ferry's global survey of 15,000 workers found that 45 percent of Baby Boomers report no problems at all working across generations, versus 17 percent of Gen Z — a 28-point gap in who thinks the office is fine, as reported by Fortune. The two countries resolve that gap in almost opposite ways, which is the whole point of this guide.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings regardless of your age or title — silence reads as disengagement, not respect | Address colleagues by title or assume your manager wants to be called "boss" |
| Log off at 5pm and expect emails to wait until morning; norms against after-hours contact are real, not performative | Schedule meetings during July, when most of the country is on statutory leave |
| Bring up a six-hour workday pilot or flexible scheduling in interviews — it's a live policy conversation, not a fringe idea | Mistake consensus-seeking (*lagom*) for indecision; it's slower by design, not by accident |
| Join the union, or at least understand what collective agreement covers your role — it sets your pay more than your CV does | Assume a flat org chart means no real authority exists; it's just less visible |
| Ask directly what "lagom" effort looks like for your specific role, since it varies by team | Oversell yourself in an interview; measured competence reads better than enthusiasm |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone, including the CEO, from day one | Confuse informality with flat power — seniority and tenure still decide who gets heard |
| Ask directly about flexible work, parental leave and bonuses in the interview; younger applicants increasingly set these terms | Assume job-hopping makes you look unreliable; under-25 tenure has held around 20 months for 40 years |
| Expect unscheduled calls outside hours occasionally — it's treated as part of the job, not an intrusion | Expect the loudest complainer about "entitled young workers" to be acting on hard data rather than nostalgia |
| Factor in two years' notice on relocating for work; dual-income households and tight housing make moving harder than it looks | Take workplace banter about generations personally; it's mostly a stand-in for deeper disagreements about pay and security |
| Check if your industry requires licensing before assuming you can switch tracks easily, as electricians and tradespeople often can't | Assume "no worries" culture extends to redundancy conversations — those still move up the hierarchy you were told doesn't exist |

Sweden's reputation for flatness is not folklore. Hofstede Insights ranks the country among the lowest in the world on Masculinity (a dimension measuring competitiveness and status-seeking) and comparatively low on Power Distance — the kind of score that makes consensus culture legible as data rather than vibes. Tobii founder Henrik Eskilsson put it plainly to The Local: top-down structures suit armies, not companies that need everyone thinking. Yubico's Stina Ehrensvärd went further, telling the same outlet Sweden has "the flattest organisations in the world," where questioning the boss is simply normal procedure. The catch, as Professor Lotta Dellve of the University of Gothenburg told Welcome to the Jungle, is that Sweden tried a more directive, top-down management style for roughly 15 years before abandoning it — "we saw an increased level of sick leave and less engagement," she said, which is a remarkably clinical way of describing a national management failure.

What the brochure leaves out is that flatness has not made young Swedes more loyal. hibob's research on the Swedish tech workforce found 14 percent of Swedish workers under 30 say they will always switch companies after a couple of years, nearly double the European average of 9 percent, and only 21 percent plan to stay in their current role at all — versus 29 percent elsewhere in Europe. Pay barely moves the needle: only 31 percent would leave for better compensation, well below the European average, because satisfaction tracks communication and manager relationships instead. Dellve's own research flags that mental stress is "especially high" among young workers, even inside the consensus model that is supposed to protect them. Flat does not mean content.

Australia tells a near-inverted story. The country sells itself on "no worries" informality, yet the Australian Bureau of Statistics data analyzed by demographer Simon Kuestenmacher in The New Daily shows job mobility among 15-to-24-year-olds fell to 11.5 percent in 2025, down from 23 percent in 1996 — meaning a worker born in 2001 is about half as likely to change jobs at 24 as a Gen Xer was at the same age three decades ago. Kuestenmacher's conclusion, inconvenient for every boomer-bashing headline, is that Gen Z is the most occupationally loyal cohort Australia has measured, kept in place less by devotion than by punishing housing costs, dual-income constraints and occupational licensing.

Deloitte's 2025 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey, in its Australian cut, complicates the loyalty story further: 64 percent of Australian Gen Z and 59 percent of millennials live paycheck to paycheck, both above the global average, and 49 percent of Gen Z report feeling stressed or anxious all or most of the time, versus 36 percent of millennials. Gallup's regional data adds a macro note of its own — job-market optimism across Australia and New Zealand fell 12 points in 2025, the sharpest regional drop Gallup tracked. Meanwhile Kantar's 10-country survey finds Gen Z workers twice as likely as Boomers to hold a second job, which in the Australian context reads less like hustle culture and more like arithmetic.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony the tourism boards will not print: Sweden's flat, consensus-driven workplace, built explicitly to include junior voices, produces some of the highest voluntary job-switching among young workers in Europe, while Australia's loud, egalitarian, first-names-for-everyone culture is currently producing the most loyal — or most trapped — young workforce the country has measured in thirty years. Flatness, it turns out, is not the same as retention. Swedish Gen Z leaves because the system genuinely listens and they can afford to test the market elsewhere; Australian Gen Z stays because housing, licensing and dual-income logistics make leaving a luxury, not because the "no worries" culture has won their hearts.

The generational-friction numbers invert too. Korn Ferry's 45-versus-17-percent comfort gap is an Anglophone finding, drawn from a largely English-speaking, individualistic sample — exactly Australia's cultural profile. Sweden, despite ranking as more individualist than most of Europe on Hofstede's scale, reports less overt generational combat, because its norms (unionized pay-setting, protected after-hours boundaries, six-hour-day pilots) remove several of the flashpoints — pay negotiation, overtime expectations — that Australian generations actually fight about. The country that talks less about hierarchy manages it more; the country that talks less about generational conflict is quietly having more of it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *HackerNews — A Sweden-based commenter, reflecting on the country's generous paid parental leave, noted it has visibly shifted expectations around fathers taking an active caregiving role — a generational contrast he found starker than anything about pay or hierarchy, especially against workplace cultures elsewhere that still treat paternity leave as optional.*

> *Welcome to the Jungle — Professor Lotta Dellve of the University of Gothenburg described colleagues checking in with each other over whether they'd done "enough" that week, a lived version of lagom that younger employees often have to learn team by team, since no manager hands out a rulebook for how much effort is the right amount.*

> *The Daily Aus — A millennial contributor recounted a working life stitched from DJ gigs, tutoring, corporate law, retail and handing out contraception for a local council, framing the pattern not as flightiness but as the only available response to a cost of living that makes any single job insufficient on its own.*

> *The New Daily — Demographer Simon Kuestenmacher argued that bosses complaining about "entitled" young staff are usually comparing today's graduates to a memory of their own younger selves, and that the real shift is bargaining power moving toward applicants in a tight labour market, not any collapse in young workers' willingness to show up.*

### Conclusion

If the decision comes down to generational friction alone, know what you are actually choosing between: a Swedish system that minimizes conflict by regulating the inputs (pay, hours, after-hours contact) before disagreement starts, versus an Australian system that minimizes conflict by sounding informal while leaving seniority, redundancy order and promotion timelines exactly where they always were. Neither country has solved the problem of four generations wanting different things from the same job. Sweden has just made the argument quieter and the exits easier; Australia has made the argument louder and the exits harder to afford.

The honest version I'd tell a friend over a drink: move to Sweden if you want a workplace that argues with you in meetings and leaves you alone after five o'clock; move to Australia if you want a workplace that calls you by your first name and still remembers exactly how long you've been there.
