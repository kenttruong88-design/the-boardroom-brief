# Moldova and Ireland Both Allow Six Months of Probation, and Neither Promises Anyone Will Show You the Stationery

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A new employee in Moldova and one in Ireland can each be kept on probation for up to six months, which is long enough for a certain kind of employer to decide that onboarding is something the employee does to himself. The legal frameworks are, if anything, closer than the cultures. In Ireland, a May 2024 Hays survey reported by RTE found that 70 percent of employers had seen staff leave within the first year because of a poor fit, and 37 percent of respondents said they had left, or nearly left, over poorly managed onboarding. In Moldova, there is no equivalent survey that I could find, which is itself a finding. The practical question for a prospective emigrant is who is responsible for teaching you the job.

## Moldova: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the probation clause closely; for full-time indefinite contracts it can now run to six months | Assume the old three-month norm still applies |
| Ask in writing what your performance indicators are; the Labour Code now requires them to be written and set after consulting you | Accept vague targets, or indicators revised without notice |
| Build relationships first: workplace culture rewards informal conversation and rapport before business | Open a meeting with the agenda and nothing else |
| Learn Romanian or Russian; language shapes how much you can absorb from colleagues | Assume English will carry you through informal instruction |
| Find your own mentor among senior colleagues, because a formal scheme may not exist | Wait to be assigned a buddy |
| Keep a record of instructions and feedback | Treat a verbal remark as the whole process; dismissal for poor performance requires a written warning |

## Ireland: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a written statement of core terms within five days of starting, and the rest within a month | Start work without asking for it |
| Ask for the employee handbook and any collective agreement | Assume policies will be explained to you |
| Look for a mentor early; a union official and a career consultant quoted by The Irish Times both advise it | Treat the first fortnight as the whole of onboarding |
| Ask your manager for specific feedback and pair with an experienced colleague, as Irish forum replies suggest | Hide basic questions out of pride |
| Check your probation length; the statutory maximum is six months, with limited exceptions | Accept an open-ended probation clause |
| Note that mandatory training required by law must be free and counts as working time | Pay for or do compulsory training in your own time |

## Moldova: Hierarchy With a Handshake

The Moldovan labour market is a place where the figures are changing faster than the habits. The National Bureau of Statistics reports average gross monthly earnings of 15,987.1 lei in the first quarter of 2026, up 9.7 percent on a year earlier, and the government has set a 2026 average salary forecast of 17,400 lei. The rules around the first months of employment have also been rewritten. PwC Moldova's summary of the 2022 amendments to the Labour Code records that probation was extended from three to six months for full-time employees on indefinite contracts, and that the Code gained a new chapter on performance evaluation. Indicators must be set in writing, no more than once every three months and after consulting the employee, and dismissal for poor performance now requires repeated failure against them, following a formal evaluation, a written warning and a reasonable time to improve.

On paper, then, the Moldovan new hire is better protected than the folk image suggests. What the law does not require is anyone to teach you. The World Bank's Missing Skills study, based on a 2016 survey of Moldovan employers, found that firms cited work ethic, technical skills and motivation as the biggest skills obstacles, that about 26 percent of firms had experienced a labour shortage in the previous year, and that 15 percent regarded young employees' skills as a bigger obstacle than those of older staff. That survey does not examine training or mentoring directly, and I found no systematic public data that does. It does suggest an employer base that regards capability as a thing candidates bring rather than a thing companies grow.

The workplace culture around that expectation is conventional. Safeguard Global describes Moldovan companies as having clear reporting lines, with employees encouraged to contribute ideas and decision-making consensus-driven only in progressive workplaces, while senior leadership retains strategic control. Informal conversation at the start of meetings is common. A newcomer will therefore be welcomed warmly, told that the structure is clear and left to infer the rest. Reddit commenters discussing the IT sector in February 2025 said that hiring depends heavily on background, portfolio and personal connections, and that the market is far harsher for juniors than for mid-level or senior developers, which makes the first job a scarce good and probation a high-stakes event.

## Ireland: Welcome, Handbook, Good Luck

Ireland's onboarding regime is more codified and no more reliably warm. Under the European Union (Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions) Regulations 2022, as summarised by Learnsignal, employers must provide core terms in writing within five days, remaining terms within a month, and probation may not exceed six months except in limited cases. Training that the law requires for the job must be free and counts as working time. The Irish Times, in September 2025, quoted a Siptu legal rights official urging new starters to ask for the handbook and any collective agreement, and both she and a Trinity College Dublin career consultant recommended finding a mentor early, with the union steward suggested as a source of informal support.

The advice is useful and also telling: if the system reliably supplied a mentor, the newspaper would not need to tell you to go and find one. The Hays survey explains why the advice is needed. The survey attributed 44 percent of early departures to a job that did not match what applicants were told, and 37 percent of respondents said they had left or nearly left because onboarding was poorly managed. Hofstede Insights scores Ireland at 28 for power distance and 68 for masculinity, a combination of accessible superiors and a competitive streak that is carried lightly. The result is a workplace that is friendly about everything except, occasionally, the question of what you are supposed to do on Monday.

The money explains some of the stakes. The Central Statistics Office put average weekly earnings in Ireland at €1,046.88 in the second quarter of 2026, as reported by IrishCentral, up 3.9 percent on a year earlier, with professional, scientific and technical activities averaging €1,294.65 a week. A new hire who leaves in the first year takes a full professional salary, and the months of recruitment that went into it, out of the door. That is the commercial logic behind the Hays figures, and it is the argument to put, politely, to a manager who regards induction as optional.

## The Reckoning

Compared directly, Ireland offers the better paperwork and Moldova the clearer statement of who is in charge. The Irish new hire is entitled to a written statement, a bounded probation and free mandatory training, and can reasonably expect a friendly manager. The Moldovan new hire has a recently strengthened performance-evaluation regime and a workplace culture that values relationships and rapport, but fewer public signs of formal mentoring schemes. Neither country guarantees a mentor, and in both the guarantee that matters is the one you secure for yourself.

The divergence is in how the system handles ignorance. In Ireland, forum replies treat asking colleagues for help as ordinary and the cost of not asking is borne by you; in Moldova, a hierarchy in which senior leadership keeps control may make questions to seniors feel heavier, and the cost of poor fit falls hard on juniors in a market where, by commenters' account, connections matter. For a candidate with experience, either country can work. For a graduate, the Irish probation is gentler and the Moldovan one riskier.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/moldova) — paraphrased experience: Asked how saturated Moldova's IT market is, commenters called it crowded and very hard for juniors, with Chisinau the only real hub. Getting hired, one said, depends on portfolio, experience, interview skills and personal connections, and mid-level and senior developers fare much better.*

> *Boards.ie (2012) — paraphrased experience: A veteran employee moved to a new role in the same company and was handed a user manual, with no training and no tour of the basics. A reply said that learning by doing, or a handover if you are lucky, is common, and not how it should be.*

> *Boards.ie (2018) — paraphrased experience: A childcare worker two weeks into a new job, on six months' probation, felt constantly corrected and feared that raising problems would mark her as unfit. Replies advised asking the manager for specific feedback and pairing with an experienced colleague.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report (Chisinau, 2010) — paraphrased experience: An expatriate government employee wrote that without Romanian or Russian, people struggle to communicate in business, that dress runs from formal to business casual, and that local salaries are low.*

> *ExpatExchange (Chisinau) — paraphrased experience: One expatriate listed the language barrier, widespread bureaucracy, low wages and a small expat community among the main difficulties of settling in.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Ireland, ask for your written statement on day one, find your mentor in week one and keep a list of the questions you were too polite to ask. If you are moving to Moldova, read the probation and performance-evaluation clauses with a lawyer's patience, learn enough Romanian or Russian to take instruction, and cultivate a senior colleague who will actually answer.

Both countries will give you six months to prove yourself. Neither will fully explain what proof looks like. That, at least, is a skill you can show up already possessing.
