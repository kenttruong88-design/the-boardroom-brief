# The Office Where Everyone's Equal, and the Office Where Everyone's Family

🇮🇳 India · 🇳🇴 Norway

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Norway, the law requires four in ten boardroom seats to belong to each gender; in practice, barely one in five belongs to a woman. In India, the law requires every workplace over a certain size to maintain a committee against sexual harassment; in practice, female labour-force participation only recently climbed back above where it stood a generation ago. Both countries, in other words, have discovered that writing equality into statute and achieving it at the water cooler are two very different projects — they have simply started from opposite ends of the gap and are closing it, slowly, in opposite directions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address female colleagues by professional title and name | Assume a woman at the table is admin staff, or someone's wife |
| Expect personal questions ("When are you marrying?") even in professional settings, and have a neutral deflection ready | Ask directly about pregnancy plans in a promotion conversation — it is both bad manners and, increasingly, a legal risk |
| Ask how active your company's Internal Committee (mandatory under the 2013 POSH Act) actually is | Assume every firm enforces its harassment policy with equal seriousness just because it exists on paper |
| Use company transport after dark where it is offered | Treat a colleague declining a late-evening meeting as unprofessional — commute safety is a real, and gendered, constraint |
| Expect women concentrated in HR, teaching, and client-facing roles, fewer in core engineering leadership | Read that clustering as a lack of ambition — it reflects decades-old enrolment patterns, not current capability |
| Ask about flexible-hours or work-from-home policy explicitly | Assume generous maternity leave (26 weeks) means equally generous support on the way back in |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with your boss regardless of their seniority or gender | Mistake that informality for a lack of real structure — decisions still move through slow, consensus-driven committees |
| Expect male colleagues to take their full "father's quota" of paternity leave as routine | Assume a man's long leave means he has been sidelined — it is normal, not a career setback |
| Note that large, listed firms are legally required to keep both genders above 40% on the board | Assume that quota has reached the rest of the org chart — women hold roughly a fifth of board seats and a similar share of senior management overall |
| Expect blunt, direct disagreement from women at every level of a meeting | Read that directness as rudeness — it is the same tone used with everyone, not a gendered edge |
| Remember the public sector, which employs about seven in ten working women, runs on different norms than private industry | Assume Norway's equality statistics apply evenly across sectors — construction, oil, and tech remain heavily male |
| Expect 4pm departures for childcare pickup, from any parent | Schedule a "quick" 5pm call assuming no one has somewhere else to be |

### 🇮🇳 India

India sits 129th of 146 countries in the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report 2024, having closed 64.1% of its overall gap and slipped two places from the year before. Hofstede Insights scores the country 56 out of 100 on Masculinity, placing it on the achievement-oriented, status-conscious side of the scale — a culture where visible success, including at work, matters, and where competition is treated as a normal feature of office life rather than something to be smoothed over. That orientation shapes how promotion conversations, titles, and public credit get handled, often regardless of the gender of the person receiving them.

The more encouraging number is participation. India's female labour-force participation rate rose to roughly 42% in 2023–24, according to the Ministry of Labour and Employment, up sharply from about 23% in 2017–18 — a genuine structural shift, even if it still trails the global average. Pay, however, has not kept pace with presence: various wage surveys have put India's gender pay gap at anywhere from roughly a quarter to over a third, depending on sector and methodology, and McKinsey's 2025 "Women in the Workplace" study — its first to cover India alongside Nigeria and Kenya, surveying 324 organisations and 1.4 million employees — identifies a "broken rung" at the first step into management as the point where women's advancement most often stalls, long before the C-suite becomes a question.

Legally, India is more protective than its rankings suggest: the 2013 POSH Act mandates an Internal Committee against sexual harassment at any workplace with ten or more employees, and the law has real teeth in large, urban, organised-sector firms. The gap between that legal floor and daily practice, though, is where most of the friction in this piece lives — and it tends to widen the further one gets from Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Gurugram.

### 🇳🇴 Norway

Norway ranks 3rd of 146 countries in the same WEF report, trailing only Iceland and Finland, and its Hofstede Masculinity score sits at roughly 8 out of 100 — among the lowest recorded for any country, and the near-mirror opposite of India's. In Hofstede's framework, that makes Norway a textbook "Feminine" culture: consensus, work-life balance, and modesty about personal achievement are the operating norms, and a Norwegian manager bragging about a win would read as slightly odd rather than impressive.

The numbers mostly back up the reputation. Statistics Norway found women earned about 88% of men's wages in 2022, up from 83.3% in 2001, and corporate boards at state-owned and large listed firms have been legally required to keep at least 40% of each gender since 2004 and 2008 respectively — a rule the government is now phasing in for mid-sized firms through 2028, eventually covering some 20,000 companies. Shared, earmarked parental leave is normal enough that a father taking several months off barely registers as noteworthy.

But the equality is uneven once you look past the headline figures. Women hold only about a fifth of board seats across the broader economy (the 40% rule applies narrowly, to the companies it actually covers), men hold roughly 63% of management positions, and nearly 70% of working women are concentrated in the public sector — meaning the famously egalitarian Norway many expats hear about describes a subset of Norwegian employers rather than the whole labour market.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and a pattern emerges that neither country's marketing quite admits to: India has the steeper legal and cultural climb but is closing ground fast on participation, while Norway starts from a near-ideal legal position and has largely stalled on translating it into occupational mixing. A Masculinity-dimension gap of 56 versus 8 looks decisive on paper, but it describes tone and ambition-signalling more than it describes who actually ends up running the construction site or the maternity ward — and on that count, both countries still sort people along remarkably traditional lines, just with different levels of embarrassment about it.

For someone relocating, the practical takeaway is almost a reversal of expectations. In India, the written protections (POSH, 26-week maternity leave) are more generous than outsiders assume, but enforcement is patchy and depends heavily on the specific employer. In Norway, the written protections are genuinely the strongest in the world, but they coexist with a labour market still quietly sorted by sector — so the question worth asking before signing a contract in either country is not "is this country good for women at work," but "is this specific employer, in this specific sector, actually living up to its own country's reputation."

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Feminism in India — An FMCG sales professional described asking her manager about her overdue promotion, only to be told that if she turned out to be pregnant, she'd forfeit it for the nine months of pregnancy plus six months of maternity leave — a scenario she had never raised, in a conversation that was never reported to HR.*

> *Feminism in India — A category manager at a decades-old division spent three months cc'd, never "To'd," on a metrics email, then discovered the sender defaulted every group message to "Dear Sirs" because other managers might object to being called anything else; she eventually got it changed to "Dear All," and now advises others not to ignore small slights, since they compound.*

> *The Local Norway — An Italian professional living in Tromsø said a hiring committee excluded her from a shortlist the moment it read her name, and that formally appealing the decision got her branded "difficult" rather than resolving the problem.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter recalled a documentary on Norway's labour market finding a construction firm that was 10% women and a maternity ward with no men at all, despite the country's top rankings on gender-equality indices — a reminder that legal parity and occupational mixing are not the same achievement.*

> *Hacker News — Another commenter, discussing workplace policy more broadly, noted that in Norway improvements to parental leave and childcare provision have tended to arrive together, almost in lockstep — a rare case of family policy actually being joined up rather than piecemeal.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is the fairy tale its own statistics sometimes suggest. India's progress on participation is real and worth taking seriously, but it is progress layered on top of a workplace culture that still treats ambition and status as things to be performed, and where legal protection is only as good as the HR department enforcing it. Norway's progress is older and deeper, codified into boardrooms and parental leave calendars, but it has plateaued in exactly the places — construction sites, engineering floors, the C-suite outside the quota-covered firms — where day-to-day occupational life actually happens. A Masculinity score of 56 and a Masculinity score of 8 describe two very different conversational cultures; they do not, on their own, tell you who gets promoted. Ask about the Internal Committee in Mumbai and the sector breakdown in Oslo before you ask about the ranking, because the ranking will tell you less than either answer will.
