# Suits You: Australia Wears Thongs to Work, Germany Wears a Blazer to the Supermarket

## 🇦🇺 Australia · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Sydney, a 26 percent plurality of workers report their office dress code is, officially, a uniform — which is to say, someone else already solved this problem for them, freeing the remaining energy for arguing about thongs (the footwear, not the underwear, though the debate has genuinely covered both) ([SEEK Employer](https://talent.seek.com.au/hiring-advice/article/right-fit-wear-affects-work)). In Germany, meanwhile, a person who shows up to a routine Tuesday meeting looking too sharp is not admired — they are quietly suspected of interviewing elsewhere ([The Local Germany](https://www.thelocal.de/20220815/sandals-mean-freedom-eight-tips-on-how-to-dress-like-a-german)). Two wealthy, orderly, rule-respecting nations, two completely opposite theories of what clothing communicates at work.

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to smart casual unless told otherwise — it's the largest single category after uniforms | Assume "casual" means gym wear; there is a difference and colleagues will notice |
| Save the suit for client pitches, law, and banking | Overdress for an internal meeting — it reads as trying too hard, or job-hunting |
| Wear closed shoes in trades-adjacent or client-facing roles even in summer | Wear thongs (flip-flops) in food, medical, or client-facing settings — widely considered a line |
| Let the tropical postcodes set their own rules (Brisbane's Bermuda shorts are real) | Import a Melbourne winter wardrobe wholesale to a Darwin office |
| Use the first week to read the room before committing to a style | Panic-buy a full formal wardrobe before your first day — most workplaces will tell you if you're wrong |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to conservative, well-fitted, unshowy clothing — competence over flair | Wear anything that looks like you're announcing a promotion you haven't received |
| Invest in good shoes; scuffed trainers undercut an otherwise fine outfit | Assume "casual Friday" means actually casual — it means slightly less formal, still ironed |
| Match the industry: suit-and-tie for banking, consulting, and law; blazer-over-jeans for Mittelstand and engineering | Wear shorts, flip-flops, or visible logos in a client-facing role, regardless of the heatwave |
| In Berlin startups and creative fields, streetwear is genuinely fine | Transplant Berlin-casual into a Frankfurt bank or a small-town Mittelstand firm |
| Dress for the job you're interviewing for, literally — wear what you'd wear once hired | Stand out. Being noticeably better or worse dressed than the room invites questions |

### Australia: The Uniform Is the Dress Code, Except When It Isn't

Australian workplace dress resists a single national rule because, per SEEK's own employer research, uniforms (26 percent), smart casual (25 percent), smart/business casual (16 percent), plain casual (16 percent), and formal business attire (11 percent) each command a real slice of the market — no single style even reaches a third ([SEEK Employer](https://talent.seek.com.au/hiring-advice/article/right-fit-wear-affects-work)). That fragmentation tracks neatly with Hofstede's numbers: Australia scores a low 38 on power distance, meaning hierarchy is downplayed and a manager wearing the same polo as the graduate hire is a feature, not an oversight, while a high 90 on individualism rewards workers for asserting their own judgment about what "appropriate" looks like rather than deferring to a house style ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison-tool)).

The exceptions prove the rule. Law and banking still run on the classic suit, and firms like Clyde & Co made headlines by publicly relaxing that standard, which only confirms how unusual relaxing it still is in that sector ([Law Society Journal](https://lsj.com.au/articles/dressing-down/)). Thongs — the footwear — have reportedly been adjudicated safe for the office everywhere except food, medical, and childcare settings, an actual debate that occurred in a serious national publication ([Fashion Journal](https://fashionjournal.com.au/fashion/work-appropriate-shorts/)). Climate does real work here too: Brisbane's tropical humidity has normalized tailored shorts in ways Melbourne's four-seasons-in-a-day unpredictability never will, producing a country where dress code is set as much by latitude as by hierarchy.

### Germany: Nobody Wrote the Rule Down, Which Is the Point

Germany presents the inverse puzzle: strict-looking outcomes with almost no formal enforcement mechanism. Most companies have no written dress code at all, yet the room reliably converges on dark, understated, well-tailored clothing anyway ([World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/germany/business-dress-code/)). The explanation sits comfortably inside Hofstede's data: Germany's uncertainty avoidance score of 65 rewards predictability and discourages standing out, and its masculinity score of 66 ties clothing to visible competence and seriousness rather than personal expression ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/germany/)). The absence of a rulebook isn't laxity — it's a system confident enough in shared norms that it doesn't need one written down, and the penalty for guessing wrong (interpreted as either sloppy or overreaching) is social rather than administrative.

Industry still segments this sharply. Banking, consulting, and politics keep the suit-and-tie standard alive without irony, while manufacturing, engineering, and the broader Mittelstand have quietly drifted to blazer-over-jeans, and Berlin's startup and creative scenes have drifted further still into hoodie territory ([DEVENHALL](https://www.devenhall.com/blog/business-dress-germany-casual-serious); [WeAreDevelopers](https://www.wearedevelopers.com/magazine/328-german-business-culture-and-etiquette)). Casual Friday exists, technically, but multiple guides note it means marginally less formal, not actually casual — Germany relaxed the rule and then quietly re-tightened the definition of "relaxed" ([Hockerty](https://www.hockerty.com/en-us/blog/business-casual-for-men)).

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the irony sharpens: Australia has more visible dress-code diversity (five roughly equal categories, per SEEK) but far less anxiety about getting it wrong, because low power distance means nobody's watching that closely. Germany has almost no written dress code in most companies, yet arguably more anxiety about getting it wrong, because high uncertainty avoidance means everyone is watching, silently, and drawing conclusions about your professionalism, your intentions, or your job search. Australia formalizes the absence of formality (a documented uniform category); Germany informalizes the presence of formality (unwritten rules everyone somehow knows).

The practical upshot for anyone relocating between the two: an Australian moving to a German Mittelstand office who dresses "smart casual" by Sydney standards will read as underdressed and slightly careless. A German moving to an Australian office who wears their Frankfurt-conservative wardrobe to a Tuesday stand-up will be quietly wondered about — not for looking wrong, but for looking like they haven't relaxed yet, which in Australia is its own kind of social error.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An expat moving to Berlin from a more rule-bound corporate culture described being thrown by the total absence of a written dress code, only to realize everyone still dressed within an unspoken, narrow band; overdressing got read as either job-hunting or trying to impress the boss.*

> *The Local Germany — A contributor summed up the local logic bluntly: dress too sharply for an ordinary day and coworkers will assume you have an important meeting, or that you're angling for a raise; for everyday business, the advice was to dress as plainly as possible.*

> *Blind — A poster working in an Australian tech office noted that despite routine 40°C days, shorts remained rare in the office, contradicting the assumption that a hot climate would automatically produce a genuinely casual dress code.*

> *Quora — Someone new to Australian workplace culture was struck by how quickly first names and casual dress extended even to senior staff and managers, requiring a recalibration of how much informality actually signaled about someone's seniority or authority.*

> *Quora — A prospective hire preparing for a German startup interview was advised, counter to instinct, to simply wear what they'd wear once actually working there — treating the interview outfit as a preview of the job rather than a formal audition costume.*

### Conclusion

Neither country's system is more "relaxed" than the other; they've just distributed the anxiety differently. Australia externalizes the decision into a visible category (uniform, smart casual, formal) so individuals spend less energy second-guessing; Germany internalizes the decision into an unwritten social contract that everyone is expected to intuit correctly on the first try. The safest strategy in both places is identical and unglamorous: watch the room for a week before committing to a wardrobe, because the written policy — where one exists at all — is the least reliable source of truth in either country.
