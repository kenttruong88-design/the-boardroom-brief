# Polished by Climate, Polished by Choice: Fashion in Singapore and Colombia

**🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇨🇴 Colombia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![Singapore vs Colombia — Fashion culture](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-04-singapore-vs-colombia-fashion-culture-hero)
*Photo: Felicity Tai via Pexels*

In Singapore, a linen blazer at 32 degrees Celsius and 85 percent humidity is a small daily act of professional discipline — nobody enjoys it, everybody does it. In Bogotá, at a far more forgiving 14 degrees year-round, women arrive at the office in heels and full makeup not because the thermostat demands it, but because looking put-together is treated as a form of respect, both for the job and for yourself. Both cities produce workplaces where appearance clearly matters. One achieves it despite the weather; the other achieves it regardless of it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress one level more formal than you think necessary until you've read the room — finance and law skew formal, tech and startups skew smart casual | Assume the heat excuses casual dress — sleeveless tops and shorts read as disrespectful even in client-facing roles |
| Invest in lightweight, breathable fabrics — fine cotton, linen blends, tropical wool — since you'll be dressed up in serious humidity daily | Show up in anything visibly rumpled from the commute — polish is noticed even when the fabric is forgiving |
| Default to Western-style formal basics: long sleeves, tailored trousers, closed shoes for client-facing meetings | Confuse "business casual" as license for jeans in traditional sectors — banking and law still expect a full formal register |
| Keep a blazer at your desk for unplanned client meetings — the norm shifts fast between internal and external contexts | Overdo bold prints or bright colors in conservative sectors — understated and neat reads as more competent here |
| Notice how fast the norm is shifting toward smart casual in fintech and creative industries and match your specific office, not the stereotype | Assume all of Singapore dresses the same — a law firm and a Grab-adjacent startup two blocks apart can have opposite expectations |

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take grooming seriously — polished shoes, tasteful jewelry, and neat hair are read as basic professional competence, not extra effort | Show bare legs at the office in Bogotá — skirts and dresses are worn with hosiery even in warmer months, per long-standing local norms |
| Expect daytime business to mean suit-and-tie or equivalent formality — Bogotá and Medellín offices run more formal than many Western equivalents assume | Assume coastal norms (looser, brighter, Caribbean-influenced) apply inland — Bogotá is considerably more conservative than Cartagena or Barranquilla |
| Let quality and fit matter more than trend-chasing — a well-cut, classic piece outperforms an expensive but flashy one | Overdo flash or gaudy jewelry — tasteful and simple reads as more credible than visibly expensive |
| Match the local standard of always looking finished — even casual Fridays tend to mean "nice jeans," not gym wear | Underdress for a first meeting assuming informality — conservative first impressions matter disproportionately in Colombian business culture |
| Ask a local colleague before a client trip outside your usual city — regional dress expectations shift meaningfully within the country | Assume dressing up is vanity here — it's treated as a visible marker of taking the relationship, and the meeting, seriously |

### Singapore

Singapore's workplace dress code sits at an unusual intersection: a genuinely humid tropical climate paired with a business culture still substantially shaped by Western corporate norms inherited through its finance and legal sectors. Business formal remains standard in banking, law, and client-facing consulting — long-sleeved shirts, ties, tailored trousers for men; dresses, blouses, or smart pantsuits for women — while smart casual has become the default in tech, fintech, and creative industries, according to multiple Singapore business-etiquette guides. The generally accepted rule, repeated across etiquette resources, is to dress one level above what feels necessary until you've actually observed your specific office.

What makes Singapore distinctive isn't the formality level itself but the discipline required to sustain it against the climate. Etiquette guides consistently recommend fine cotton, linen blends, and tropical wool specifically because standard business fabrics become genuinely uncomfortable in daily humidity — yet the expectation to look polished doesn't relax to compensate. Hofstede Insights ranks Singapore around 14th globally on individualism, moderately collectivist by global standards, which tracks with a workplace culture where fitting a shared professional standard — however uncomfortable — is treated as a basic form of respect for colleagues and clients rather than a personal choice.

### Colombia

Colombian fashion culture carries a sharper regional divide than most countries this size. Coastal cities like Cartagena and Barranquilla lean loose, bright, and Caribbean-influenced; Bogotá, sitting at altitude with a cooler climate and more conservative business culture, dresses considerably more formally. In the capital specifically, women reportedly never show bare legs at the office — skirts and dresses are worn with hosiery as standard practice — and sleeveless tops are rare even in casual settings, per multiple region-specific guides. Business etiquette resources describe daytime professional dress in Bogotá and Medellín as traditionally suit-and-tie for men, with women expected to balance polish (heels, tasteful makeup, simple jewelry) against a cultural aversion to appearing "flashy" or overdone.

Hofstede scores Colombia notably low on individualism — around 13 on the index, reflecting a strongly collectivist culture where family and group relationships take precedence over individual expression. That collectivism shows up in dress as a shared, legible standard rather than personal statement: looking well put-together in a Colombian office isn't self-expression so much as a visible signal that you take the relationship — with colleagues, with clients — seriously, consistent with a business culture where personal relationships and trust-building precede transactional business itself.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony here is that Singapore's formality is largely functional — inherited finance-and-law professionalism maintained despite an unhelpful climate — while Colombia's formality is more expressive and relational, maintained despite a climate that would happily permit something looser. Ask a Singaporean office worker why they're wearing a blazer in 85 percent humidity and the honest answer is "because that's the standard here." Ask a Bogotá office worker the same question about their heels and hosiery, and the honest answer leans closer to "because showing up polished is how you show you respect this room."

The practical consequence for a mover: Singapore's dress code is more sector-legible — you can research your specific industry and get it largely right before arriving. Colombia's dress code is more relationally and regionally variable — the same job title in Bogotá and Cartagena might dress two different ways, and the deeper norm (dressing up as relationship-building) is harder to research than a sector guideline and easier to get wrong by simply not trying hard enough.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering "what does it feel like to work as a foreigner in Singapore" described the adjustment to daily formal dress in the heat as a genuine early shock, noting that colleagues who'd grown up there seemed to simply not register the discomfort the same way, treating full business attire as unremarkable rather than an imposition.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing Singaporean work culture more broadly called it intensely output-driven, noting that showing up polished and put-together functioned as one small, low-cost way employees signaled commitment in a culture where visible effort matters, even when the actual work happens after hours at a desk rather than in how one dresses.*

> *Tales from a Small Planet (talesmag.com, a diplomatic-spouse and expat posting-report forum) — A contributor stationed in Bogotá described the local dress code as noticeably more formal than expected going in, specifically flagging that skirts worn without hosiery drew visible attention in professional settings, a detail no pre-departure briefing had mentioned.*

> *Substack ("Medellín Unmapped," a local-perspective newsletter) — The writer contrasted Medellín's startup and remote-work scene, where dress has loosened considerably among younger tech workers, against older, more traditional Bogotá firms just a short flight away, describing the two cities as running on almost different professional wardrobes entirely.*

> *Substack (a remote-work expat account, "six weeks remote working in Colombia") — The writer described initially dressing too casually for in-person client meetings in Bogotá based on assumptions carried over from more relaxed Latin American coastal destinations, and having to visibly upgrade their wardrobe mid-trip after noticing the gap with local colleagues.*

### Conclusion

If you're relocating for either of these moves, the practical prep differs by what kind of research actually helps. For Singapore, sector research pays off directly — look up your specific industry's norm and you'll likely be close to right on day one. For Colombia, city-specific and relationship-aware research matters more than sector research — what's appropriate in Bogotá finance won't automatically transfer to Medellín tech, and getting dress right there is less about following a rulebook than about reading how much your appearance is meant to say about how seriously you take the people in the room. Priya's line for a friend packing for either move: in Singapore, dress for the job description; in Colombia, dress for the handshake that comes before it.
