# Argentina Puts Women in Parliament, Italy Puts Them on a Part-Time Contract

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Consider two countries that share a language of expressive hands and a fondness for a long lunch. In Argentina, women hold 44.7 percent of national parliamentary seats, according to the World Bank's gender data. In Italy, according to Eurostat figures reported in April 2026, only 58.0 percent of women aged 20 to 64 are in work, the lowest rate in the European Union, against an EU average of 71.3 percent. One country has put women in charge of the legislature. The other has put them in charge of the household, unpaid, and then asked why the employment statistics look the way they do.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet with a cheek kiss only after your counterpart leads, and a handshake in formal settings | Treat a hug or a cheek kiss as an invitation to anything beyond a greeting |
| Say plainly and early when a comment about your appearance is unwelcome | Accept "tranquila, no pasa nada" as a substitute for an actual response |
| Budget time for small talk about family and football before any decision | Open a meeting with the agenda and expect it to be received warmly |
| Ask whether a role is registered (en blanco) before you accept it | Assume the contract in front of you reflects the pay gap you read about |
| Use titles such as Licenciado or Doctor until told otherwise | Challenge a senior colleague in public as a display of candour |

### Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about parental leave, part-time conversion and return-to-work terms before signing | Assume a part-time offer is a voluntary arrangement that can be reversed |
| Research the specific manager and team, not the national stereotype | Treat Milan and the South as one workplace culture |
| Request the salary band in writing and compare it with sector figures | Accept "it is the contract" as a reason for a gap with male peers |
| Build relationships with senior executives, who tend to engage on merit | Expect lower-level staff and suppliers to extend the same courtesy at first |
| Check the company's real shift and unpaid-overtime habits before relocating | Expect the working week to end when the contract says it does |

## Argentina

Argentina presents the paradox with some confidence. According to the World Bank, 50.8 percent of Argentine women participated in the labor force in 2023 against 71.1 percent of men, a gap of about 20 percentage points, and women held 37.9 percent of senior and middle management posts in 2022. INDEC, the national statistics office, reported in 2025 that women hold 44.3 percent of the country's 22.7 million jobs, but that men earn more in every occupational category, and that the gap widens with age. In domestic service, women hold 96 percent of registered positions, which is where the pay is lowest.

The informal economy does much of the editing. According to INDEC data summarised by Infobae in March 2026, nearly four in ten employed women work informally, 3.1 percentage points more than men, and the regional spread runs from 38.2 percent in Patagonia to 49.5 percent in Cuyo. The World Bank also notes that women spent 23.4 percent of their day on unpaid domestic and care work in 2013, against 9.2 percent for men. A woman may chair a committee in the morning and, by the figures, still be expected to run the evening shift at home.

INDEC's newer figures, quoted by Infobae, put female participation at 53 percent against nearly seven in ten men, with the gap widest in Cuyo at 20.9 points. In other words, the capital's boardrooms and the provinces' labor markets are describing different countries, and a relocation to Buenos Aires should not be mistaken for a relocation to Argentina. The national average conceals a lot of geography.

For the foreigner, the day-to-day texture matters more than the headline. Expat.com describes Buenos Aires as a place where business runs on relationships, where decision-making sits near the top of the organisation, and where start-ups and some multinationals are noticeably flatter. Hofstede Insights scores Italy at 70 on achievement and success versus Argentina at 56, which suggests the local competitive streak is real but less frantic than the one waiting in Rome.

## Italy

Italy's problem is not one statistic but a stack of them. According to the OECD's Education at a Glance 2024, as reported by 9Colonne, young Italian women with university degrees earn 58 percent of what their male peers earn, the widest gap among OECD countries. According to INPS data reported by Il Sole 24 Ore, women earn on average about 25.7 percent less than men, hold 74.2 percent of the country's 4.238 million part-time contracts, and make up only 21.8 percent of managers, even though they are roughly 60 percent of graduates.

The mechanism is not subtle. Women perform 71 percent of unpaid care work, and in 2024 nearly 61,000 parents of children up to three years old left their jobs, seven in ten of them women. Eurostat data cited by L'Unione Sarda in March 2026 shows that employment among women aged 25 to 49 falls from 68.5 percent with no children to 42.3 percent with three or more. Italian mothers are, in effect, running a second career for which the pension system will later send a smaller cheque: INPS puts women's average pension at about 1,000 euros a month against 1,486 for men.

Politics offers little consolation. According to Il Sole 24 Ore, women hold 33.6 percent of parliamentary seats in Italy as of 2024, which is respectable, and only 15 percent of municipalities are led by a woman, which is not. The same coverage notes that more than seven in ten voluntary resignations for family reasons involve women. Italy is, on paper, a country that educates women thoroughly and then declines to employ them at the level of their qualifications.

Expat women should read the numbers alongside the mood. The InterNations Expat Insider survey of 2017, reported by The Local Italy, ranked Italy 56th of 57 countries for expat women's jobs and careers, with only 43 percent satisfied with their work against a 62 percent global average. The same survey found that 52 percent of expat women in Italy said they earned less than in a comparable role at home, against 31 percent globally, and only 15 percent had moved for their career, compared with 46 percent of expat women worldwide. One in four had come for love, which the labor market does not count as a qualification. The survey is old, and Italy has not become Sweden in the interim, but the direction of travel has not obviously reversed.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Argentina wins on representation and on the presence of women in senior roles, while Italy loses on almost every labor-market measure that matters to someone planning a career. A 17 to 20 point participation gap in Argentina is still serious, but it is narrower than the distance between Italian women and their EU peers, and Argentine women are visible in politics and management in a way the Italian figures do not suggest. The Argentine system has a different defect: informality, which hides pay, pensions and protections behind a handshake rather than a contract.

The sensible reading is that Argentina is the more open place for a woman to be promoted and the less reliable place to be paid properly, while Italy is the reverse on both counts, with a salary more likely to appear on a payslip and then to be smaller. Neither country has solved unpaid care, which is the quiet variable behind both sets of numbers. In Italy it becomes a part-time contract, and in Argentina it becomes an unrecorded job.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *BAexpats forum — paraphrased experience: A long-term male resident argued that Argentina is more open to women than the United States, pointing to a woman winning two presidential elections by wide margins and a woman running the national airline. He suggested outsiders mistake familiar habit for hostility. A fellow poster, notably, asked to hear from women before the thread went further.*

> *Matador Network — paraphrased experience: An American woman who moved to Argentina described constant hugs, cheek kisses and old-fashioned courtesy, and learned to separate friendliness from flirtation. She set firm limits on remarks about her body, found the reflexive "tranquila, no pasa nada" well meant but dismissive, and says she emerged more assertive and clearer in how she communicates.*

> *The Next Web — paraphrased experience: A feature on women in Latin American tech, with the author's experience centred on Buenos Aires, describes networking events that turned flirtatious and a colleague kissed without consent at a start-up gathering. One interviewee described a boss who hired only male directors, and another said she did not get support at the moment she reported a colleague.*

> *Reddit r/Italian — paraphrased experience: In a thread about Italian bosses, commenters said individual managers vary more than nationality does, though several described unpaid overtime, restricted holidays and resistance to flexibility, with Milan singled out as workaholic. One said their Italian subsidiary improved after local managers were replaced by foreign ones. The advice was to research the specific team before accepting.*

> *The Local Italy — paraphrased experience: Foreign women running businesses in Italy describe being assumed to have a hobby or a husband who owns the firm, being sidelined by men in local meetings, and suppliers who prefer to deal with men. Several said senior executives respected them once they made their case, and that Italian clients proved loyal.*

## Conclusion

The practical choice is between two different kinds of friction. Argentina offers visibility and a more plausible route into management, but you should verify that the job is registered and that your pay is not quietly discounted by the informal market. Italy offers formal contracts and structure, but you should interrogate the part-time clause, the parental-leave terms and the actual overtime culture of the specific employer before signing anything.

In both countries, the single most useful document is the one that tells you who will do the laundry, and neither government has drafted it.
