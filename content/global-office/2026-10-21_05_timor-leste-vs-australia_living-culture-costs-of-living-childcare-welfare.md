**In Dili, the Average Salary Won't Cover Your Wi-Fi Bill in Canberra. In Canberra, Childcare Costs More Per Day Than Most Dili Households Spend in a Week.**

🇹🇱 Timor-Leste · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The average monthly wage in Timor-Leste is about $400. A single expat's monthly cost of living in Dili, including rent, runs to roughly $1,820 — meaning the local economy and the expat economy are, in effect, two different currencies wearing the same note. Meanwhile in Australia, the average cost of a single day of childcare has reached $130, rising to $200 in some cities, which means two days of Australian daycare can cost more than a month of Dili rent. Both countries have a "living culture" problem; they've just optimized for opposite ends of it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇱 Timor-Leste

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry cash everywhere and expect ATMs to run dry on weekends — this remains roughly a 90% cash society | Expect routine card payments; only about 10–15% of businesses reliably accept them |
| Budget for private, imported-standard health insurance (roughly $150/month) and a real medevac plan, not just local clinic visits | Treat local medical facilities as sufficient for anything beyond basic care — a cracked tooth or broken bone typically means being flown out |
| Expect to pay $200–250 a month for household help, which is both normal and often essential given limited services | Assume the national $400 average salary applies to your own cost structure — expats routinely spend four to five times that monthly |
| Expect housing via compound living near embassies or international offices, with short commutes but basic finishes | Expect your home country's grocery variety; selection is limited, though meat and vegetables are often good |
| Research the national poverty data honestly before judging local wage norms — a 2014-era measure found over two in five people below the national poverty line | Mistake the dollarized economy (Timor-Leste uses the US dollar) for US-level purchasing power; it emphatically is not |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget seriously for childcare specifically — it's the cost parents most often underestimate, and now averages $130 a day | Assume Medicare covers everything; bulk-billing covers free care for under-16s, but specialists, medication, and plenty of consultations are extra |
| Expect roughly $12,750 a year in average costs per child, before school fees, before extracurriculars | Assume public school is genuinely free; "voluntary" contributions of several hundred to $1,000 per student per year are common |
| Plan for one parent to cut hours or shift to flexible work — the majority of parents already do, which shapes household income more than headline salary does | Underestimate upfront costs; budget up to $6,000 just for baby equipment before the weekly costs even start |
| Treat the welfare state as real but partial — Medicare and public hospital maternity coverage are genuine safety nets, not marketing | Expect universal, cheap daycare on the European model; Australia's system runs closer to a part-subsidized private market |
| Compare household wealth figures with household cash-flow reality separately; Australia ranks high on net wealth, largely property-driven, while weekly living costs still bite hard | Assume high median wealth means low financial stress; extracurricular costs alone average close to $1,900 a year per child per activity |

Timor-Leste's living-cost picture is really two economies stacked on top of each other. The dollarized local economy, where the average monthly wage sits around $400, and the parallel expat economy, where a single person's full monthly cost of living — rent, food, utilities, insurance — runs to roughly $1,820, and a family of four to around $3,601, per cost-of-living estimates. The World Bank's most recent poverty brief, built on 2014-era data pending an updated 2024 living-standards survey, put 41.8 percent of the population below the national poverty line and 69.2 percent below the $3.65-a-day lower-middle-income benchmark — figures that, even allowing for a decade's improvement, describe a country where "living culture" means something closer to subsistence planning than lifestyle choice for most residents. Timor-Leste has no individually published Hofstede cultural-dimension score — it sits outside the original study entirely — so there's no tidy cultural shorthand here, only the economic data itself.

Australia's version of the same question is a wealthy country's problem: not whether people can afford to live, but how much of a dual-income household's earnings childcare alone will consume. The average annual cost of raising one child reaches $12,750 by one estimate, and daily childcare now averages $130, climbing to $200 in some cities — a cost 59 percent of parents call their highest single expense, and one 38 percent say they underestimated going in. Medicare provides genuine public coverage — free care for children under 16 via bulk billing, covered prenatal care and public-hospital delivery — but it is a floor, not a ceiling; specialist visits, medications, and plenty of day-to-day consultations sit outside it. Hofstede scores Australia's individualism at a high 90, which tracks with a welfare model built around personal and household responsibility layered on top of public coverage, rather than the more comprehensive, less means-tested systems found in parts of Europe.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't rich-versus-poor, it's predictable-versus-improvised. Australia's cost structure is enormous but legible: Medicare's rules are published, childcare subsidies exist on paper even if they don't close the whole gap, and a parent can model their annual child-related spend to the dollar using public data, as this very article just did. Timor-Leste's cost structure is smaller in absolute terms but far less predictable: a 90-percent-cash economy where ATMs run dry on weekends, where "general health services are limited" means medical evacuation insurance isn't optional, and where the entire concept of a published welfare safety net barely exists outside of World Bank poverty-line calculations. Australia makes you budget against a known, if expensive, system. Timor-Leste makes you build your own system from scratch, in cash, with a medevac plan as your actual health insurance.

Neither country's system is more "honest" than the other, exactly — Australia's welfare state simply has more institutions willing to put numbers in writing, which makes its gaps easier to criticize and, in theory, easier to fix.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag ("Real Post Report," Dili) — A U.S. government diplomat posted to Dili described the healthcare reality bluntly: "Huge issue — there are zero decent medical care centers outside of very basic needs," adding that anything beyond routine care, a cracked tooth or a broken bone, means being medically evacuated out of the country entirely.*

> *Talesmag (same platform, separate posting) — Another diplomat described the cost of living less in terms of dollar figures and more in terms of logistics: a near-total cash society where "ATMs running out of cash on the weekends" is a routine problem, and where shipped goods from home take about three weeks to arrive via diplomatic pouch.*

> *Talesmag (a third posting) — A separate report described household help as both affordable and essentially necessary, at roughly $200 to $250 a month, alongside a frank warning that bringing a child with special needs to this particular post was probably not advisable, given how limited specialized local services are.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a move to Timor-Leste, the honest budgeting exercise isn't comparing your salary to the $400 national average — it's assuming you'll spend four to five times that and treating a real medevac insurance policy as non-negotiable, not an upgrade. If you're weighing Australia with children in tow, the honest budgeting exercise is accepting that Medicare will cover the emergencies and the basics, and that childcare, not rent or groceries, is the line item that will actually determine whether the move pencils out. Ask for the real number before you sign anything — in Dili, that number is "how fast can I get out if something goes wrong," and in Canberra, it's "what does the second parent's salary actually buy after childcare."
