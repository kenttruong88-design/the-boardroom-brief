**Philippines to the United Kingdom: Everyone Thinks the Other Generation Ruined the Office**

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Jobstreet's 2025 happiness index found Filipino Gen Z workers are the country's least satisfied generation at work, even though the gap to millennials and Gen X is a matter of a couple of percentage points rather than a chasm. A UK survey commissioned by hiring platform Applied found something structurally similar but far starker: 45 percent of workers under 35 say there's a real generational divide in their workplace, against just 22 percent of those over 55. Two very different office cultures, one shared theme — whichever generation is doing the complaining, it's usually complaining about the one above or below it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address superiors with "ma'am," "sir," or "miss" rather than their first name, even in otherwise casual offices. | Use a boss's first name by default just because you would elsewhere — expat guides flag this as a genuine source of friction. |
| Read a hesitant "yes" carefully — it can mean polite deferral rather than actual agreement, especially across a hierarchy gap. | Take every verbal agreement at face value; confrontation and open refusal are often avoided to preserve face. |
| Expect younger colleagues to prioritise financial stability, savings, and family contribution as explicit, stated career goals. | Assume Gen Z's financial focus means disengagement — an Ateneo study found them highly motivated by learning and mission as well. |
| Recognise long service publicly — tenure is described as deeply valued, with loyalty often marked through awards or acknowledgment. | Assume tenure is irrelevant to standing in a Filipino office just because someone is young and capable. |
| Expect a notable share of Gen Z workers to report dreading work and higher stress than older colleagues, even amid otherwise high national workplace happiness. | Dismiss a younger employee's stress as generational softness; survey data shows real measurable gaps in exhaustion by age group. |
| Build rapport and small talk before getting to business, across generations. | Lead with blunt, transactional directness — it reads as a lack of respect more than efficiency. |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real friction over what counts as acceptable office conduct — casualwear, personal disclosure, and slang in emails all split sharply by age. | Assume shared nationality means shared norms on dress or communication style; workers under 35 and over 55 disagree by double-digit percentages on several of these. |
| Expect younger colleagues to want peer-aged teams and managers more than older colleagues do. | Assume age-mixed teams are automatically comfortable for everyone; 56 percent of 16-34s want same-age peers, versus 25 percent of over-55s. |
| Take communication-style friction seriously as a top-three complaint across every generation, not just a Gen Z issue. | Blame communication gaps on one generation alone; Gen X, millennials, and boomers all name it among their top frictions too. |
| Recognise that younger workers entering during the pandemic may have had less face-to-face integration time than older colleagues did at the same career stage. | Assume reduced social integration reflects personal unfriendliness rather than a structural effect of hybrid and remote onboarding. |
| Separate individual personality from generational stereotype when a colleague is difficult — plenty of posters online make exactly this point under genuine pressure. | Default to "boomers" or "Gen Z" as an explanation for ordinary workplace friction; several experienced posters note the complaint often says more about the person than the cohort. |
| Expect 73 percent of UK business leaders to say, in principle, that a multigenerational workforce benefits the organisation. | Assume stated leadership enthusiasm for multigenerational teams has eliminated the everyday friction measured in the same surveys. |

### Philippines

Filipino office hierarchy runs on both formality and genuine deference, and it starts with language: an expat guide to working in Manila notes that using a superior's first name — a near-automatic habit for many Western arrivals — can cause real friction, where "ma'am," "sir," or "miss" is the safer default regardless of how informal the relationship otherwise feels. That same deference shapes how disagreement is expressed. Confrontation and raised voices are generally avoided to preserve face, and a colleague's "yes" doesn't always mean agreement so much as a polite deferral — reading tone and context, the guide advises, matters more than taking the literal word. ManilaRecruitment.com adds that Filipino management style "emphasizes deep respect for hierarchy" alongside genuine personal concern for the team, and that long service specifically is "deeply valued," often marked through public recognition rather than treated as simply a line on a résumé.

Generational data complicates the stereotype of total deference, though. Jobstreet's 2025 Workplace Happiness Index found Gen Z Filipino workers (18-29) scored lowest on overall happiness at 76 percent, against 77 for millennials and 78 for Gen X — a narrow gap, but one driven by concretely higher reported stress and emotional exhaustion, and nearly four in ten Gen Z respondents saying they often dread going to work. What they do report liking tracks closely with purpose and flexibility: 76 percent cite finding meaning in the work itself, 73 percent value work-from-home arrangements specifically. A separate Ateneo de Manila University study, based on 40 interviews and a 132-person survey of Filipino Gen Z employees, found this generation notably focused on using pay for savings and investment and on contributing to family and society, a more explicit, financially literate framing of career motivation than older generations typically articulate the same way. Hofstede's data scores the Philippines at a steep 94 on power distance — one of the highest figures the index records anywhere — against the UK's 35, a number that helps explain why hierarchy-respecting language and indirect disagreement remain the norm even as younger workers report real strain underneath it.

### United Kingdom

British workplaces don't lack hierarchy so much as they lack agreement on what's socially acceptable day to day, and a 2,000-person Applied-commissioned survey puts real numbers on just how wide that gap runs. Thirty-two percent of UK workers overall report a generational divide at their workplace, but the number nearly doubles among younger staff: 45 percent of 16-34-year-olds report one, against 22 percent of those over 55. The specific flashpoints are oddly granular — 16 percent of younger workers think gym wear, loungewear, or Ugg boots are acceptable office attire against 10 percent of the 55-plus group; 12 percent of younger workers find workplace-email slang fine against 5 percent of older colleagues; 14 percent think sharing relationship or financial details at work is acceptable against 7 percent of older staff. The one place older workers are more permissive: 34 percent of over-55s are fine eating lunch at their desk, against 27 percent of under-35s.

The relationship friction runs deeper than etiquette, too. The HR Director's reporting found 27 percent of UK Gen Z office workers (18-27) describe their colleague relationships as negative, nearly double the 13 percent rate among boomers, with communication gaps cited as a top-three friction point by every generation — 74 percent of Gen Z specifically flagged it. A long, heavily commented Blind thread on exactly this topic captures the texture behind the statistics: one poster called older colleagues "stuck in the past, unable to adapt," while several blamed boomers specifically for pushing return-to-office mandates. But the same thread pushed back hard on itself — multiple commenters, including one self-identified boomer, argued the complaints described individual personalities rather than genuine generational traits, with one noting dryly that "assholes come in all age groups," and another pointing out that reverse ageism against younger workers in tech is real but talked about far less. Hofstede's UK score of 89 on individualism, against the Philippines' 32, is consistent with a culture where generational friction gets debated loudly and publicly as a matter of individual rights and preferences, rather than absorbed quietly into a hierarchy.

### The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries disagree about almost everything except the basic complaint. In the Philippines, generational tension is muted by a strong, shared expectation of hierarchy and politeness — Gen Z workers report real stress and dread, but it surfaces as quiet exhaustion and a desire for flexibility rather than open generational warfare. In the UK, the same underlying tension gets measured, published, and argued about constantly, down to whether Ugg boots belong in the office, precisely because British workplace culture puts a much higher premium on individual self-expression that has nowhere obvious to hide.

Neither country's older or younger cohort comes off as simply right. UK data shows real behavioural gaps in what's considered appropriate, but also that leadership mostly says it values the mix anyway — 73 percent of business leaders call multigenerational teams a benefit, even as 45 percent of their youngest staff feel like outsiders day to day. The Philippines shows a Gen Z cohort that's financially switched-on and purpose-driven, working inside a hierarchy that was built, structurally, around deference to people who got there first. A newcomer to either system should expect the real cross-generational tension to be happening quietly underneath a surface that, depending on the country, is either determinedly harmonious or loudly, publicly argued about.

### Conclusion

Move from Manila to London, or the other way round, and the generational complaint doesn't disappear — it just changes which emotion carries it. In the Philippines, frustration gets absorbed into politeness and quiet stress; in the UK, it gets surveyed, published, and argued about on a Tuesday. Either way, the people doing the complaining are nearly always talking about the same thing: who gets to decide what "normal" looks like at work, and how long the rest of the office has to wait before that answer changes again.
