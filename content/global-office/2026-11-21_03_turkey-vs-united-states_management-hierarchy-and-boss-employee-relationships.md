# In Istanbul the Boss Is a Patriarch You Consult in Private; in America He Is a First Name Who Can Fire You Friday

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇺🇸 United States

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Only 29 percent of American workers were engaged at work in the third quarter of 2026, according to Gallup, the lowest figure since 2011. Gallup's global figure for 2025 was 20 percent, which suggests that nobody should draw a flattering conclusion about management anywhere. The two countries nevertheless run their bosses on opposite principles. In Turkey, the boss is a figure of rank and, in many firms, of family. In the United States, he is called Dave, and can end your employment without giving a reason.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat rank as real; the Expat Arrivals guide says a clear hierarchy exists and authority is respected | Address a senior manager as an equal on day one |
| Invest in the relationship first; meetings open with extended small talk and decisions rarely come in the first one | Press for a decision in the room; senior leaders often consult privately afterward |
| Identify who actually decides, since decisions are made at the top and influencers are not the same thing | Spend months persuading someone who cannot say yes |
| Get overtime consent in writing; the statutory week is 45 hours and overtime is paid at 150 percent | Assume that long hours are free; the cap is 270 hours a year |
| Check whether the employer has 30 or more staff, which triggers job-security protections after six months | Assume a smaller employer needs a reason to let you go |

### In the United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract; at-will is the default in every state but Montana, and a contract can change it | Rely on a handbook or a verbal assurance of job security |
| Treat indirect feedback as feedback; a Blind commenter advised taking hints and adjusting | Wait for a private reprimand before concluding that something is wrong |
| Offer problems with solutions; commenters describe a culture where employees are there to solve problems | Present a difficulty and wait for instructions |
| Negotiate leave before accepting; BLS data show that after one year, 32 percent of private workers get 10 to 14 days | Expect the Turkish statutory leave tiers or European norms by default |
| Use first names and keep the personal talk modest | Mistake a first name for friendship, or a friendly manager for a safe job |

## Turkey

Turkish workplaces are hierarchical by structure and personal by habit. The Expat Arrivals business guide says decisions generally flow from the top, often from the head of the family or company, and that a final decision rarely happens in a first meeting because senior leaders consult privately afterward. WorldBusinessCulture adds that local firms are mostly family-owned or state-owned and highly hierarchical, with many layers of bureaucracy, and that finding the actual decision-maker, as opposed to an influencer, saves effort and money. An expat quoted in the guide says employees in Turkey do not befriend their employers and interact as equals in the Western way, and advises arriving as a learner.

On Hofstede's scores, cited here once, Turkey's power distance is 66 and its individualism 37, against 40 and 91 for the United States. The Expat.com work-culture guide for Istanbul reads like a translation: managers are expected to give clear direction, employees are expected to show initiative, final decisions rest with higher management, communication is tactful and indirect, and Turks work longer hours than Europeans. It adds that multinationals and start-ups are becoming more egalitarian. A Bilkent chapter by Ümit Berkman and Ş. Özen, from 2008, reached a similar conclusion: Turkish management had become less paternalistic and more assertive. Hierarchy, in other words, is in transition rather than retirement.

The law offers a counterweight that the American reader will find exotic. Under the CMS guide, where the employer has 30 or more employees and the worker has six months of service, dismissal requires a valid reason, and notice runs from two weeks to eight depending on tenure. Severance after a year of service is 30 days' pay per year, capped at about 46,655 lira as of January 2025. Reinstatement suits are available. Annual leave runs at 14 days for the first five years, 20 up to fifteen years and 26 beyond, according to a 2014 summary of Law No. 4857 that readers should verify. Hours are limited to 45 a week; overtime needs written consent, is capped at 270 hours a year and is paid at 150 percent. The system is hierarchical in culture and fairly protective in law, and the protection does not apply to everyone. A 2012 mobbing survey reported by the Hurriyet Daily News found 10 percent of 316 employees had experienced it and that victims often stayed quiet for fear of losing their jobs.

## The United States

American management is informal in manner and severe in structure. The National Conference of State Legislatures notes that employment is presumed at-will in every state except Montana, so either side may end it at any time for any lawful reason or none. Exceptions exist for discrimination, retaliation, public policy and implied contracts, but the default is blunt. Montana has required good cause since 1987, with damages capped at four years of lost wages, in exchange for predictability that employers apparently found tolerable.

The manners are lighter than the law. On Blind, an employee on a visa described a supervisor whose communication seemed direct and at times rude after two months with no training. Replies were split: one said that employees are there to solve problems and should learn what they do not know, another that rudeness is not acceptable and that treatment depends on one's value to the manager. European commenters found Americans more likely to talk around problems than to say plainly that something is bad, with one suggesting the habit is more common on the West Coast than in New York. Politeness, it seems, is the form through which a layoff announces itself.

The bosses themselves are under strain. Gallup reports American engagement at 29 percent in the third quarter of 2026, down from a peak of 36 percent in 2020, with the share strongly agreeing that someone at work cares about them down from 40 to 37 percent. Globally, Gallup's 2025 report found manager engagement at 22 percent against 19 percent for individual contributors, a narrowing of the gap that it linked to wider spans of control in some regions. On Expat.com, one member called the American workplace extremely demanding and said a 40-hour week on paper often becomes 50 or 60, with a two-week vacation standard. The expat who chooses the United States for flexibility should read the leave data first.

## The Reckoning

The difference is not simply formal versus informal. Turkey has a visible hierarchy backed in part by statute, in which the boss is a recognised authority and, for employees at larger firms with six months of service, is also bound by dismissal rules. The United States has an invisible hierarchy backed by contract law, in which the boss may be your friend until the moment he is not. One country asks you to respect rank; the other asks you to pretend it is absent.

The costs fall differently. In Turkey you spend relationship capital: the small talk, the patience, the private consultation that happens somewhere you are not. In America you spend vigilance: the reading of indirect feedback, the reluctance to say no, the constant awareness that the arrangement is revocable. The Turkish employee knows where authority sits. The American employee knows only that it sits somewhere, and that it signs the paperwork.

For a newcomer, the practical question is which kind of ambiguity is easier to live with. Turkish ambiguity is social: the decision made in private, the indirect phrasing, the instruction that arrives as a courtesy. It can be learned from colleagues, and it rewards patience. American ambiguity is contractual: the offer letter that says little, the performance review that arrives late, the at-will clause that makes every pleasant conversation provisional. It can be learned from a lawyer, and it rewards paperwork. Neither is hostile. Both are more demanding than the brochure suggests.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A newly hired employee on a visa described a supervisor as direct and at times rude, with no adjustment period and no training. One reply said to treat it as business as usual; another said that rudeness is unacceptable and that treatment tends to track how valuable the manager thinks you are.*

> *Blind — A European commenter said Americans tend to talk around issues instead of saying plainly whether something is good or bad, a view a German commenter shared. The same commenter found New Yorkers more direct than people on the West Coast.*

> *Expat.com, USA forum — A member described the American workplace as extremely demanding, with a standard two-week vacation and a 40-hour week that often becomes 50 or 60, though they valued that colleagues do not pry into private life.*

> *Expat.com, Turkey forum — A poster said many foreign workers hired as nannies are treated very harshly, and advised setting clear boundaries with an employer at the start of the job.*

> *Expat.com, Turkey forum — A poster described bullying in office work and said officials handling residence matters expected bribes, though the details were brief and unclear.*

## Conclusion

If you prefer to know who is in charge and are willing to earn the right to be heard, Turkey will suit you better, particularly with a larger employer and a written contract that records your hours. Learn names and titles, build the relationship before the request, and find out who actually decides. If you prefer being treated as an adult and can tolerate not being treated as secure, the United States is for you, provided you read the contract and negotiate leave before the offer is signed.

The Gallup numbers are a reminder that the manager in either country is probably tired. Engaged managers, Gallup found, report fewer negative emotions and are far more likely to be thriving, and in 2025 managers were no more engaged than the people they manage. Be kind to him, in whichever language, and keep a copy of the contract.

Both countries will tell you the manager is approachable. In Turkey that is a statement about courtesy. In America it is a statement about the org chart, and it expires with it.
