**No One Sits at the Head of the Table (Unless You're in Warsaw): Hierarchy in Norway vs. Poland**

**🇳🇴 Norway · 🇵🇱 Poland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

At an Aker Solutions office in Norway, according to one senior manager, "no boss sits at the head of the table" — and everyone, regardless of title, takes a turn at the dishwasher. At a Polish office a few hundred kilometres south, a junior developer who criticized a sprint plan "plainly and in front of the whole team" thought he was being helpful; his Norwegian manager heard a challenge to her authority. Two countries, one European labor market, and almost nothing in common about who is allowed to speak first.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings regardless of your title — input is expected from everyone in the room, not just the senior staff | Expect your manager to hand down a decision; consensus is built collectively, and unilateral calls are viewed with suspicion |
| Leave the office around 4 p.m. like everyone else — it's the norm, not a sign of low commitment | Schedule a meeting for late Friday afternoon, especially in summer — it will be read as a mild imposition |
| Take your full parental and sick leave entitlements without apology | Assume flexibility means no accountability — trust is the system, and abusing it is noticed quickly |
| Do your share of shared office chores (yes, including the dishwasher) regardless of seniority | Use a title like "Director" or "Manager" as a way to avoid unglamorous shared tasks |
| Give yourself six months to a year to feel fully oriented — Norwegian integration is gradual by design | Expect instant inclusion; Norwegians are famously private and warm up slowly, even at work |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles like *Dyrektor* or *Kierownik* when addressing senior colleagues, especially early in a relationship | Address a senior manager too casually before they've invited informality — titles signal earned position |
| Expect decisions to come from the top, and direction to be clear once given | Wait for a Scandinavian-style consensus process; in most Polish workplaces, that's the manager's job, not the group's |
| Give direct, specific feedback when asked — Poles read bluntness as respect for your time | Soften critical feedback into vagueness; it can be read as evasive rather than polite |
| Recognize that competence earns real influence — a sharp junior colleague can gain sway fast, informally | Assume the org chart tells the whole story; an unofficial hierarchy of trusted problem-solvers often runs alongside it |
| Expect a generational split — younger colleagues in multinational and tech firms often work in a far more egalitarian style | Treat "Polish work culture" as one fixed thing; a 25-year-old in a Warsaw tech startup and a 55-year-old in a state-linked firm may barely recognize each other's norms |

### Norway: Hierarchy as a Social Embarrassment

Norwegian management culture treats visible hierarchy as something close to bad manners. The concept of Janteloven — a set of unwritten Scandinavian social norms that discourage standing out or claiming superiority — extends directly into the office: a senior manager who pulls rank rather than persuading through argument is quietly seen as insecure, not authoritative. The Local Norway's reporting on the subject captures this literally: Mauricio Andres Funes, a senior manager at Aker Solutions, described a culture where "no boss sits at the head of the table," and shared office duties like dishwashing are handled by everyone regardless of rank.

That flatness comes with real structure underneath it, not chaos. Fabian Eikrem, head of immigration at Expat Relocation, told The Local that the system runs on trust rather than oversight — generous rights around parental leave and sick time exist precisely because abusing them is rare and noticed when it happens — and that full cultural integration realistically takes "six months to a year." Cynthia Myrnes, CEO of a relocation firm, added a detail that trips up newcomers from longer-hours cultures: "people leave work around 4pm," and scheduling a Friday-afternoon meeting, especially with good weather forecast, is met with quiet resistance. None of this makes Norwegian workplaces leaderless — decisions still get made, and someone is still accountable — but the visible performance of authority that many cultures expect from a boss simply doesn't happen.

### Poland: Hierarchy as Clarity, Not Cruelty

Polish management culture inherited a more visibly top-down structure, and — contrary to the assumption that this is purely a Soviet-era hangover — it persists because many Polish professionals experience it as clarity rather than oppression. A cross-cultural consultancy's breakdown of Polish workplace norms notes plainly that "managers are expected to lead, not just facilitate," and that "decisions are expected to come from the top" in a way that would read as autocratic in Oslo but reads as basic competence in Warsaw. Titles matter here in a way they explicitly don't in Norway: designations like Dyrektor and Kierownik, per the same account, "signal expertise and earned position" and are used deliberately, especially with someone new.

The counterintuitive part is what Polish hierarchy is not: it isn't a ban on direct feedback. If anything, Polish workplace communication runs more direct than Norway's consensus-softened style — "honest feedback is a sign of respect," as the same source puts it, and criticism delivered plainly and specifically is the polite version, not the rude one. There's also a real informal layer beneath the formal chart: competence earns influence fast, and a sharp junior employee can end up more consulted than their title suggests, running an unofficial hierarchy "alongside" the official one. And the picture is generational — professionals under 35 in multinational and tech firms increasingly operate closer to the flat, egalitarian model that older Polish workplaces would find unrecognisable.

### The Reckoning

The clash isn't really about who has more power — it's about where power is allowed to be visible. Norway hides its hierarchy behind ritual egalitarianism: someone is still in charge, but performing that fact out loud is treated as a minor social failure. Poland does the opposite: authority is named, titled, and expected to act like authority, while paradoxically tolerating much blunter feedback than the "flatter" Norwegian system does.

This produces exactly the kind of collision CultureFlow documented: a Polish developer offering direct, specific critique of a sprint plan "in front of the whole team" wasn't breaking a Polish norm at all — he was following one, since honest, public, specific feedback is the respectful move at home. His Norwegian manager, operating inside a system where authority is supposed to be exercised quietly through consensus-building rather than declared, heard something closer to insubordination. Neither party was wrong by their own rules; they were simply using two incompatible definitions of what "respecting your boss" looks like.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Norway — Mauricio Andres Funes, a senior manager at Aker Solutions, described the literal absence of positional seating in Norwegian meetings: "No boss sits at the head of the table," extending even to who takes a turn doing the office dishes.*

> *The Local Norway — Cynthia Myrnes, CEO of a relocation firm, warned newcomers used to longer-hours cultures that Norwegian offices genuinely empty out by 4 p.m., and that scheduling a Friday-afternoon meeting — especially on a sunny day — is a quiet social misstep.*

> *CultureFlow (cross-cultural consultancy) — A case study described a Polish developer who gave frank, specific criticism of a sprint plan in front of the full team, intending it as helpful directness; his Norwegian manager interpreted the same act as a public challenge to her authority — neither read the moment as their own culture would.*

> *CultureFlow (cross-cultural consultancy) — The same source noted a real generational split inside Poland itself: professionals under 35 in multinational tech firms increasingly work in "a much more egalitarian style" than the traditionally hierarchical norm, meaning the "Polish boss" a newcomer meets can vary enormously by industry and age.*

### Conclusion

The practical test is simple: if a meeting has a visible pecking order and someone is expected to make the call and own it, you're better suited to Poland. If you'd rather earn influence by persuasion, take your turn at the dishwasher, and be home by five, Norway will suit you far better — but only if you can tolerate never quite knowing, in the moment, who's actually in charge. Neither system is more respectful of employees than the other; they've just each decided that the other country's version of respect looks suspiciously like either arrogance or weakness. Pick your discomfort.
