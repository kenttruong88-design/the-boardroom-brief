# In Seoul the Boss Pours, in Kuala Lumpur the Boss Hosts, and Both Expect You to Eat

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two Asian capitals, two methods of ensuring that colleagues know each other better than they strictly wanted to. In South Korea, according to OECD data reported by The Korea Times, employees worked 1,833 hours in 2025 against an OECD average of 1,736, and a meaningful share of the surplus was spent in restaurants, at the invitation of someone senior, pouring soju in the correct order. In Malaysia, the same instinct toward togetherness arrives through a calendar so crowded with festivals that the office is regularly turned into a catering event, and the compulsory element is not alcohol but lunch. Both countries run on the principle that work is a relationship before it is a transaction. They differ chiefly in what is served and who is permitted to leave.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let the most senior person pour first and begin drinking, and turn your head away when you drink in front of them | Pour your own glass or drink before the seniors have started |
| Attend the first-round dinner, especially in your first weeks, and make a polite exit before any later rounds | Assume you are obliged to follow the group to the second or third venue |
| Hold a glass, sip slowly, or quietly switch to water if you do not want to drink heavily | Make a speech about why you do not drink |
| Keep comfortable clothes and clean socks at the office for hikes, bowling and restaurants where shoes come off | Arrive at a floor-seating restaurant in a hole-ridden sock |
| Learn basic Korean, because even modest effort is noticed | Rely on English as your only social currency |
| Ask a trusted Korean colleague about unwritten expectations if something feels uncomfortable | Ignore discomfort at karaoke or physical gestures and hope it passes |

### In Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join team lunches, which are where information and goodwill circulate | Make solo desk lunches a habit without a friendly explanation |
| Accept festival invitations, including a colleague's open house, and bring something to share | Turn down every invitation and then wonder why you are out of the loop |
| Offer to cover for a colleague who needs leave for their own festival, as it will be returned | Assume the national calendar revolves around your own holidays |
| Choose halal and vegetarian options for any shared meal you organise | Make a religious activity compulsory for the whole team |
| Give negative feedback privately | Correct a colleague or supervisor in front of the team |
| Learn a few phrases of Malay | Treat the office festival buffet as a cultural tour you are conducting |

## South Korea

The Korean company dinner, hoesik, was designed in an era that valued speed. The JoongAng Daily quotes a business professor, Cho Chul-ho, who explains that since the 1960s, during rapid growth, informal dinners let employees communicate and cooperate quickly. The format survives in diluted form. The same paper reports that an Embrain survey in 2023 of 1,000 people found 53.2 percent calling hoesik bothersome and 24.7 percent calling it enjoyable, and that a 2022 Hankook Research poll found only only 39 percent of people in their thirties and 40 percent of those aged 18 to 29 considered it necessary, against 63 percent of those over 60. The generation that endured the ritual is, naturally, the one most attached to it.

The rules have loosened but not dissolved. A Saramin survey of 1,824 employees, reported by The Korea Times, found that 64.5 percent could turn down a work dinner without negative consequences, and that 42.4 percent said gatherings now end earlier. In the same survey, however, 24.7 percent said that skipping them could carry hidden costs: isolation, missed information, weaker promotion prospects. An earlier Job Korea survey of 230 employees, reported by The Korea Herald, found over 73 percent describing hoesik as a form of overtime. A 2026 Korea Herald report on Seoul data found that 64.4 percent of respondents had fewer work dinners than before the pandemic, which suggests the institution is shrinking rather than disappearing. The Korea Herald also notes that Seoul Metro recorded a 23.7 percent fall in after-midnight subway use between 2019 and 2025.

The younger workforce is replacing the dinner with alternatives that are nominally optional. The JoongAng Daily lists bowling, screen golf, escape rooms and camping, and a Korea Times columnist observes that even post-work running groups along the Han River risk becoming obligations once people begin tracking pace. The lunch table is not safe either: an Embrain survey of 1,000 office workers, reported by The Asia Business Daily in December 2025, found that more than half of those in their twenties and of entry-level staff regularly eat alone, and that around 65 percent of workers in their twenties and thirties consider it a stress reliever. Hierarchy, in other words, is being negotiated one meal at a time.

## Malaysia

Malaysia's version is more cheerful and, depending on your temperament, harder to escape. The Star reported in September 2026 that colleagues of different faiths routinely cover for one another so that each can travel home for their own festival, with Hari Raya Aidilfitri, Christmas, Deepavali and Chinese New Year each handled by reciprocal arrangement. One employee told the paper that workplaces mark festivals with traditional dress and food, which she regards as a form of cultural education. Free Malaysia Today, publishing guidance from the Jobstore career site in March 2026, advises employers to keep religious activities voluntary and to offer halal and vegetarian options, a reminder that inclusion here is administered as well as felt.

The harder part is that the sociability is not purely optional. A viral post on XiaoHongShu, reported by The Independent Singapore, described an employee in Kuala Lumpur who brought a home-cooked lunch to save money and was warned by HR for repeatedly eating alone, on the grounds that it damaged team morale. A separate post on X, reported by The Sun, described a boss hosting a Raya open house for most of the office, releasing guests an hour early while the uninvited tenth of the staff worked their normal hours. Both episodes suggest that the social calendar carries real weight, and that exclusion, rather than attendance, is the greater hazard.

The structure beneath it is hierarchical. An Expat.com guide to Kuala Lumpur describes a strong sense of hierarchy and advises raising problems privately to avoid public embarrassment. Official statistics frame the labour market as tight: the Department of Statistics Malaysia reports unemployment of 3.0 percent in 2025, with 16.58 million people employed. The statutory week, according to ASEAN Briefing's account of the Employment Act amendments, was set to fall from 48 to 45 hours from 2023, though the Act applies directly only to lower-paid and manual employees, with everyone else governed by their contracts.

## The Reckoning

The head-to-head is simpler than the etiquette manuals suggest. South Korea's ritual is vertical: the senior person sets the pace, the junior person pours, and the evening ends when the hierarchy decides it ends. Malaysia's ritual is horizontal and calendar-driven: nobody is required to drink, but everybody is expected to appear, eat, and cover for a Muslim, Hindu, Christian or Buddhist colleague on the relevant date. Hofstede Insights classifies both countries as collectivist, which explains why neither society regards a colleague's evening or lunch hour as strictly private, and rates Malaysia as unusually comfortable with hierarchy, which explains why the invitation from above is rarely a suggestion.

The trajectories differ as well. Korea is visibly retreating from compulsion: the surveys cited above point to shorter, rarer dinners, a younger cohort that treats evenings as its own, and a vocabulary of choice. Malaysia has no comparable retreat, because its rituals are bound to a multi-faith calendar rather than to a drinking custom, and a calendar cannot be reformed by a workplace survey. The Korean newcomer must manage a declining obligation whose penalties are unevenly applied. The Malaysian newcomer must manage a durable one whose penalties are social rather than formal. The first is a gamble about what you can skip; the second is a certainty about what you should attend.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Transitions Abroad — a foreign English teacher in rural Korea was taken to a staff dinner on her day of arrival after a long flight, found the early dinners exhausting, then came to value them as the place where real colleague friendships formed, and advised that bowing out of an invitation is rarely polite*

> *Expat Guide Korea — a Singaporean working at a Seoul non-profit found that dinners can run until 2 a.m. with an on-time start expected the next morning, that karaoke and friendly touching can be uncomfortable for foreigners, and that a trusted local friend is the best guide to what is acceptable*

> *Expat.com forum — in a thread on after-work culture in Malaysia, one member admitted never having worked there and joked about mall trips, while another said they had worked so constantly that they had not noticed any after-work culture at all*

> *Global Indian — a professional from Chennai describes two years in Kuala Lumpur where team lunches rotate between Hari Raya, Pongal and Chinese New Year, calls the inclusiveness genuinely practised, and reports being invited to a Hari Raya open house*

> *XiaoHongShu, via The Independent Singapore — an anonymous Kuala Lumpur employee who brought lunch from home to save money was formally warned by HR for eating alone, and felt the lunch break should be their own time*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on social grounds alone, ask which obligation you would rather administer. In Korea, you will need to learn the pouring order, keep spare socks at your desk, and decide in advance when you leave, bearing in mind that surveys suggest declining is increasingly tolerated but not free. In Malaysia, you will need a diary of other people's festivals, a willingness to eat at the communal table, and the sense to cover a colleague's shift without being asked. Neither country will let you remain a stranger for long, and both will notice if you try.

A practical rule: accept the first invitation in either country, observe who stays and who leaves, and set your own pattern thereafter. The social contract can be renegotiated, but only by someone who first turned up to sign it.
