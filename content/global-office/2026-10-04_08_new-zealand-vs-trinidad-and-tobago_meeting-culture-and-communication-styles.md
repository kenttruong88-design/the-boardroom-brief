# "Yeah, Nah" vs. Picong: Decoding What People Actually Mean

**🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In New Zealand, a colleague can disagree with your proposal for three weeks without ever technically saying so. In Trinidad and Tobago, a colleague might mock your proposal to your face, good-naturedly, in front of the whole meeting — and mean it as a compliment on your thick skin. Both cultures pride themselves on being easygoing. Only one of them tells you clearly when you've gotten something wrong.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to read subtle hints — direct requests are less common than you'd expect | Wait for an explicit ask before acting; by the time it arrives, you may have already missed the cue |
| Treat "yeah, nah" and similar hedges as real answers that need follow-up questions to clarify | Take a hedge at face value as a firm yes or no; it's often neither |
| Keep achievements and self-promotion understated in meetings | Visibly celebrate your own wins in front of the team; "tall poppy syndrome" punishes standing out |
| Address people formally with titles and full names until told otherwise | Assume instant informality; reserved warmth is the starting posture, not instant familiarity |
| Ask for feedback explicitly and directly, more than once if needed | Expect unprompted, blunt feedback on your work; it tends to arrive sugar-coated or not at all |

#### 🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect meetings to be lively, humor-filled, and prone to good-natured digression | Mistake the humor and banter for a lack of seriousness; real decisions still happen inside it |
| Take friendly teasing ("picong") as a sign of acceptance, not hostility | Respond to picong with visible offense; knowing where the line sits is part of fitting in |
| Hold your own in conversation — being informed and able to debate is socially valued | Stay silent or purely transactional in discussion; it reads as disengaged, not professional |
| Be strictly punctual for business meetings, even if locals run late | Assume the "island time" latitude you see locals get will be extended to you as a newcomer |
| Respect hierarchy even inside an informal-feeling meeting | Mistake the relaxed tone for flattened hierarchy; senior voices still carry more weight when it counts |

### New Zealand: Politeness as a Decoding Problem

New Zealand's workplace communication runs on what one first-person account, written by a foreign resident navigating Kiwi culture, bluntly calls a "guess culture" — a system where people are expected to pick up on subtle hints rather than respond to explicit requests (cecyesparzadiaz.com). The phrase "yeah, nah," she notes, captures the pattern perfectly: a response that commits to neither a yes nor a no, which she argues "drags out decision-making in work, relationships, and social plans" for anyone used to more direct cultures.

The sharper edge underneath this politeness is tall poppy syndrome — a well-documented discomfort with people who stand out or self-promote, explored at length in New Zealand workplace research and in essays describing the pressure to "make yourself small to please other people." Hofstede's data gives this some useful shape: New Zealand's power distance score sits at a strikingly low 22, signaling a genuinely flat, approachable hierarchy, while individualism sits high at 79 (cyborlink.com) — a combination that should, in theory, produce blunt, individual-forward communication, but instead produces something closer to conflict-averse politeness, because standing out is culturally discouraged even in a flat structure. The practical cost, per the same first-person account, is that "problems don't get solved" when real issues get danced around instead of named directly — a dynamic that frustrates newcomers and plenty of native New Zealanders in equal measure.

### Trinidad and Tobago: Directness Disguised as Comedy

Trinidadian communication solves a similar problem — how to be honest without damaging a relationship — with the opposite instinct. Rather than softening disagreement into hints, the culture has formalized directness into performance: picong, a tradition of witty, mocking banter rooted in calypso culture, lets people say sharp, pointed things to each other inside a clearly understood comic frame (Wikipedia). Succeeding at picong — landing a clever jab without tipping into real insult — is a recognized social skill, not an accident, and reading where that line sits is part of actually being accepted into a team.

That same directness-through-humor carries into meetings, which tend to run lively, digressive, and full of banter while hierarchical respect stays firmly intact underneath — senior voices still carry disproportionate weight once a decision actually needs making (jarniascyril.com). The one place directness doesn't extend equally to everyone is punctuality: locals get real latitude on "island time," while foreign hires are held to a visibly stricter clock, a double standard worth planning around rather than resenting.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't rudeness versus politeness — both cultures are, by most measures, warm and conflict-averse about outright hostility. It's where each one locates its honesty. New Zealand buries directness underneath layers of hedging and understatement, so you have to actively dig for what someone really thinks. Trinidad and Tobago puts directness in plain view, wrapped in comedy, so the honesty is right there — you just have to learn to hear a joke as information rather than noise.

The ironic twist: New Zealand's flat, low-power-distance culture should, on paper, be the easier one to read clearly — and it's the one that requires the most inference. Trinidad's meetings look chaotic and joke-heavy on the surface, and they're often the more legible of the two once you've learned the code.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *cecyesparzadiaz.com — A foreign resident's biggest practical frustration with Kiwi communication wasn't hostility but ambiguity: "yeah, nah" and its cousins mean neither yes nor no, and she found herself having to ask the same question multiple ways just to get a usable answer out of a colleague.*

> *cecyesparzadiaz.com — The same writer's sharper observation is a genuine cultural misread risk: New Zealanders present as deeply conflict-averse, which newcomers read as a green light to speak frankly — until feedback comes back defensive rather than constructive, revealing that politeness and thin skin can coexist in the same culture.*

> *Medium (tall poppy syndrome essay) — The advice embedded in this widely discussed New Zealand concept is blunt: visibly celebrating a win or standing out from the group draws social pushback here in a way it wouldn't in more individualist-coded cultures, despite New Zealand measuring as one of the most individualist societies in Hofstede's own data — a contradiction worth sitting with before your first big presentation.*

> *Wikipedia (picong) — The pleasant surprise for anyone moving to Trinidad and Tobago: being teased sharply and publicly by a colleague is frequently a sign you've been accepted, not singled out — provided you can tell, as locals instinctively can, exactly where the joke ends and an actual insult begins.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to New Zealand, learn to ask the same question three different ways, because the first "yeah, nah" is rarely the real answer, and the real feedback will probably never come unprompted at all. If you're moving to Trinidad and Tobago, get comfortable being teased in meetings and teasing back — it's not a distraction from the business at hand, it's often how the business at hand actually gets decided. The honest version I'd tell a friend: in New Zealand, listen for what isn't being said; in Trinidad, listen for what's being said as a joke — it usually isn't one.
