# The Pub Will See You Now: A Field Guide to Not Being Lonely at Work in the UK and Finland

🇬🇧 UK · 🇫🇮 Finland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two entirely sincere theories of how humans become close: one holds that you cannot trust someone until you've watched them lose an argument about nothing over four pints; the other holds that you cannot trust someone until you've sat with them, silently, in 80-degree heat, wearing significantly less than either of you would prefer. The UK bonds through performance — banter, rounds, the pub as a stage where hierarchy gets dissolved in lager. Finland bonds through the absence of performance — the sauna as a room where nobody has to say anything at all, and that is the point. Both systems produce real friendship. Both take longer, and cost more, than the relocation package implied.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇧 UK
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the Friday pub trip at least occasionally, even briefly | Skip every social invite and expect to be read as "just busy" |
| Learn to take the piss and take it back — mockery is affection here | Take workplace banter literally or personally |
| Buy your round when it's your round | Let someone else always cover you; it's noticed, silently, forever |
| Say "we should get a coffee sometime" only if you mean it | Assume politeness ("we must catch up!") is a firm commitment |
| Laugh at the joke about your accent; it's a form of inclusion | Correct people's assumptions about you too earnestly, too soon |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a sauna invitation if one comes — it's a real gesture | Fill comfortable silence with small talk to ease your own nerves |
| Join a harrastus (hobby club) outside work; that's where locals socialise | Expect colleagues to invite you out spontaneously after hours |
| Be direct about what you want — vagueness reads as evasive, not polite | Take initial reserve as unfriendliness; it usually isn't |
| Show up on time, every time, without exception | Overshare personal details before real trust has built up |
| Ask a colleague to a specific activity rather than "hanging out" vaguely | Assume one good conversation means an ongoing friendship has started |

## UK

British workplace bonding runs almost entirely on ritual self-deprecation conducted in licensed premises. Around 22 million people across the UK still socialise with colleagues after work, even after a five-percent dip from pre-pandemic habits, and the pub functions less as a venue than as neutral ground where office hierarchy gets temporarily suspended — "any deference that might be shown in the workplace is replaced in the pub by ironic mock-deference," as observers of English social ritual have long noted. The joke that would be a disciplinary matter in a meeting room is, over a pint, simply Tuesday.

The catch is that this system is fraying under its own weight. Gallup's workplace research finds 20% of UK employees report feeling lonely "a lot of the previous day," rising to 25% among remote workers — a startling number for a country whose entire bonding infrastructure assumes physical co-presence in a room with a dartboard. Meanwhile 82% of UK office workers say they want more team-building events, and 75% believe they'd enjoy their job more with them, according to one 2025 workplace survey — evidence that the demand for connection has outpaced the supply of Friday pub trips that hybrid working quietly cancelled. ONS data adds a wider frame: nearly a quarter of British adults report feeling lonely often or some of the time, even as 62% say they feel a strong sense of belonging to their immediate neighbourhood — belonging to a place, it turns out, is not the same as belonging to a team.

What makes British bonding culture disorienting for newcomers isn't coldness — it's the opposite. The banter is warm, immediate, and can feel like genuine intimacy within a week. Whether it is genuine intimacy is a separate, slower-moving question, and conflating the two is the most common expat miscalculation in the country.

## Finland

Finnish bonding culture inverts the British premise entirely: closeness is not built through talk but through shared, wordless discomfort, most iconically in the sauna, which functions as a genuine piece of business infrastructure rather than a spa amenity. Most Finnish companies, down to small ones, have a sauna on the premises, and it is not unusual for teams to hold informal meetings there or for a single sauna session to build more trust than "hours of discussion in a conference room," as Finnish business commentators routinely put it. The sauna also does something the pub cannot: it flattens hierarchy by removing the props — suits, titles, desks — that normally signal it, which tracks with Hofstede's finding that Finland scores as a strongly Feminine, consensus-driven culture (in contrast to the UK's more competitive, achievement-oriented Masculine score), where managers seek agreement rather than deference and equality is treated as a working value, not a slogan.

The trust that makes this possible is not a workplace quirk; it's a structural feature of the country. Finland has topped the World Happiness Report for nine consecutive years running, with researchers attributing the result less to wealth than to social trust, reliable support, and low corruption. The OECD found 78% of Finns report high or moderately high trust in other people, against a 63% average across member states — a number that explains why a Finnish colleague will hand you real vulnerability once trust is established, even though almost none is offered up front. The friction for newcomers is that "up front" can last a long time: InterNations' Expat Insider research has repeatedly flagged Finland as weak on "Culture & Welcome" and "Finding Friends," with expats describing locals as "too internal" — not hostile, just unhurried, on a timeline no orientation email prepares you for.

## The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: the country that talks constantly (the UK) produces relationships that often stay pleasantly shallow, while the country that barely talks at all (Finland) produces relationships that, once formed, run deep and durable. British bonding is fast to start and slow to deepen — you can be "mates" with a colleague inside a fortnight and still know almost nothing true about them a year later. Finnish bonding is slow to start and, once it clears the threshold, fast to deepen — the wait is real, but so is what's on the other side of it.

The other inversion is who actually gets lonely. On paper Finland is the happiness champion and the UK the also-ran, yet it's Britain's supposedly extroverted, always-down-the-pub culture that shows the higher measured workplace loneliness. A dartboard and a Friday round are not, it turns out, a substitute for structural trust — they're a decent bandage over its absence.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — a recurring complaint in UK workplace threads is the "mandatory optional" nature of team socials: technically you can skip the Friday drinks, but skip enough of them and you're quietly recast as someone who "isn't really part of the team," with no one ever saying so directly.*

> *Quora — several long-term foreign residents in Finland describe the same arc: the first few months feel isolating and the reserve reads as rejection, but the people who push through report that Finnish friendships, once formed, are unusually loyal and low-maintenance — one respondent called it "friendship without the performance of friendship."*

> *TeamBlind — one poster relocating for a tech role described the disorientation of "mandatory optional fun time" in a new market, noting that the calendar invite for a bonding event caused more anxiety than the actual work did, because attendance felt evaluated even when nobody would admit it was.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider discussion threads — a recurring theme among expats in Helsinki is the specific frustration of colleagues who are warm and helpful during the workday but never extend an invitation beyond it, described by more than one respondent as feeling "professionally embraced, socially invisible."*

> *TeamBlind — a separate thread on loneliness abroad offered advice that surfaces again and again for both countries: join something with a fixed, recurring structure — a five-a-side team, a choir, a hobby club — because waiting for organic bonding to happen inside the workplace, in either culture, means waiting for something that was never going to arrive on its own.*

## Conclusion

The honest advice for someone weighing the UK against Finland is to stop asking which country is friendlier and start asking which kind of loneliness you'd rather manage while you wait for something real to form. In the UK, you'll be surrounded by warmth from day one and may still feel unmoored a year in, because the warmth was never designed to hold weight. In Finland, you'll feel unmoored from day one and may, two years in, have the kind of friend who'd help you move flats without being asked twice. Neither system is broken; both are just optimised for a different definition of "knowing someone." The brochure calls this cultural richness. It is more accurately described as two different repayment schedules on the same loan.
