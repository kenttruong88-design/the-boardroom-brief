**The Boss Is 56 and Confused, the Applicant Is 20 and Unemployed: Argentina vs. Australia on Who Actually Runs the Office Now**

**🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Argentina, a 20-year-old can do everything right and still not get a callback, because employers want someone young with, somehow, years of experience already. In Australia, a 56-year-old managing director is the one doing the adjusting, learning to speak "the language of technology" because the 23-year-olds she hires now have more leverage than she ever did at their age. Both countries have a generational problem. They've just ended up on opposite ends of who holds the power.

**Argentina: Generational Friction With an Economic Floor Underneath It**

Argentina's generational story can't be separated from its economy right now. Youth unemployment for 18-24 year-olds sat near 19.4% in Q3 2024 per the Human Capital Ministry, more than double the rate for workers aged 35-49 (4.2%), and informal, unregistered work affects 65.5% of workers under 24 compared to roughly a quarter of workers over 35. Buenos Aires Times spoke with Zuyimar, 20, who described the bind bluntly: "no-one calls me or even gives me a shot at an interview," because employers "want someone young but with years of experience" — a contradiction that leaves her feeling, in her words, like "no matter how much we prepare, it's still not enough."

What makes this specifically generational rather than just a bad economy is who's competing with whom. Buenos Aires Herald reporting on Instituto Argentina Grande data found young people's labour force participation falling at the same time older Argentines (66+) returned to work at rising rates — up 12% year-on-year, with off-the-books work among retirees up roughly a third — as pension purchasing power and real wages both eroded under recent economic policy. Sociologist Candelaria Rueda, who led the research, called it a discouragement effect as much as a jobs shortage: "participation falls because people get discouraged in the face of very high unemployment and very low wages." Randstad's regional Workmonitor survey adds the emotional layer: 34% of Argentine Gen Z workers report feeling misunderstood by employers, roughly double the rate among Gen X or Baby Boomers (17% each).

**Australia: The Power Has Quietly Changed Hands**

Australia's generational story runs in almost the opposite direction. SBS News's reporting on multigenerational Australian workplaces captured a telling admission from Edi de Pellegrin, a 56-year-old managing director: her own generation "never asked what the pay rate was going to be," but she's had to actively "learn to communicate and deliver my messages a lot better" to work with younger staff, specifically by speaking "the language of technology." James Pearson of the Australian Chamber of Commerce and Industry framed the deeper shift structurally: for younger applicants, "what mattered most to them was the values and culture of the company they went to work for" — a genuinely different hiring filter than previous generations applied.

TheNewDaily.com.au's coverage of Boomer-Gen Z workplace friction supplies the unfiltered version of the same tension, quoting frustrated bosses verbatim: "These folks haven't worked a day in their life and are making demands left, right, and centre!" and, on remote work specifically, "Working from home?! I went to the office every day for the last 40 years and never complained once!" The underlying structural point, per that same coverage, is that Gen Z is a comparatively small cohort entering a genuinely tight labour market, which has shifted real bargaining power in their favour — a shift several quoted employers find more galling than wrong.

There's a structural-values layer underneath both stories too. Hofstede Insights scores Argentina at a notably high 86 on uncertainty avoidance — one of the highest in the Americas — which helps explain why Argentine workers of every generation gravitate so strongly toward multinational employers and formal, registered positions over startups or solo ventures: in a genuinely volatile economy, structure itself becomes the benefit people are negotiating for, not a consolation prize. Australia's much lower uncertainty avoidance (51, per Hofstede Insights) helps explain why its own generational friction centres on values and flexibility rather than raw economic survival — Australian Gen Z workers can afford to negotiate on culture fit precisely because the baseline economic floor isn't shifting under them the way it is in Argentina.

**The Reckoning**

Here's the actual contrast: Argentine Gen Z workers are structurally squeezed — competing against both a brutal youth unemployment rate and older workers re-entering the labour market out of necessity — and their generational complaint ("I'm not understood") sits on top of a real economic floor collapsing under them. Australian Gen Z workers are structurally empowered — a small cohort hitting a tight labour market — and their generational complaint from the other side ("they're entitled") is really a complaint about losing leverage, not about losing income.

Same-sounding friction, opposite root cause. An Argentine employer dismissing a young candidate's frustration is dismissing someone genuinely losing ground financially. An Australian employer dismissing a young candidate's demands is often dismissing someone who, uncomfortably for the employer, has actually gained the upper hand.

### Conclusion

If you're trying to figure out which country's generational tension you'd rather navigate, the honest framing isn't "which workplace is more age-friendly," it's "which side of the power shift are you walking into." In Argentina, youth face a genuinely stacked economic deck, and the generational conversation is really a conversation about survival dressed up as a culture gap. In Australia, youth are negotiating from unexpected strength, and the generational conversation is really a conversation about older managers losing control dressed up as a complaint about attitude.

My honest line for a friend: in Argentina, don't let a culture-fit rejection make you doubt your competence — the market itself is the obstacle, not you specifically. In Australia, don't let a frustrated Boomer boss make you feel unreasonable for asking about flexibility — strange as it sounds, you're currently the one with the leverage in that conversation, even if nobody in the room wants to admit it out loud.
