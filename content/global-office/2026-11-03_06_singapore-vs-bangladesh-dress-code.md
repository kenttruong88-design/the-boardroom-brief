[HEADLINE] Suits You, Sir: Why Singapore Over-Dresses for the Weather and Dhaka Under-Dresses at Its Own Risk

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Singapore sits one degree north of the equator and dresses, on any given Tuesday, like a mild autumn in Zurich: blazers, closed shoes, the occasional tie knotted tight enough to leave a mark. Bangladesh sits in the same climatic bracket and has, within the last eighteen months, had its own central bank try to legislate hemlines by memo, then quietly take the memo back. Both countries will tell you their dress code is simply "professional." Neither means the same thing by it, and a new arrival who packs for one and lands in the other will spend the first fortnight either sweating through a borrowed blazer or being quietly judged for showing up in linen trousers to a bank meeting. This is the part the relocation consultant glosses over: formality here is not a style choice, it is a reading of the room, and the room is lying to you about the temperature.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a cardigan or blazer at your desk; office air-conditioning is set for a climate that does not exist outdoors | Assume "business casual" means jeans — many finance, law, and civil-service employers still expect collared shirts and closed shoes through the week |
| Match your register to your floor: a trading desk and a design studio in the same building can dress like different countries | Treat a relaxed-looking client as an invitation to dress down for the pitch meeting; seniority still reads clothes first |
| Wear batik, a cheongsam, or other national dress to client dinners if it's genuinely yours to wear — it reads as respect, not costume | Mistake a loosened policy for a universal one; HR circulars on casual Fridays often don't reach client-facing or government-adjacent roles |
| Ask a colleague what "smart casual" means at your specific company before your first week — the phrase has no fixed definition | Show up underdressed to a statutory board or ministry meeting; the civil service has barely moved since the 1990s |
| Budget for good shoes; closed, polished footwear outlasts most other signals of effort in a Singapore office | Confuse the CBD's suits for the national uniform — walk ten minutes into a heartland estate and the dress code evaporates |

#### 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a saree, salwar kameez with dupatta, or a long-sleeved shirt and trousers until you've clocked your office's actual norm | Wear shorts, sleeveless tops, or flip-flops into any office, bank, or ministry building, regardless of the heat index |
| Pack lightweight linen or cotton in muted, simple colours — you can be modestly dressed without being drenched by 10 a.m. | Assume a written dress memo is permanent; a 2025 central-bank circular on attire was withdrawn within days of public backlash |
| Watch what the most senior women in your workplace wear and quietly match that register — it outranks almost any official document | Treat "no policy was announced" as "no expectation exists"; unwritten dress norms are enforced at least as firmly as written ones |
| Carry a scarf or shawl for client visits, mosques, or government offices even if your daily office is relaxed about it | Mistake a casual multinational startup's dress code for the national standard; most employers remain considerably more formal |
| Let your first two weeks be deliberately over-dressed; it is easier to loosen up later than to walk back a bad first impression | Raise your dress-code frustrations publicly before you understand the room; the backlash to institutional dress memos here runs both directions |

---

Singapore's formality is a patchwork pretending to be a uniform. According to the CIPD, PwC Singapore rolled out a "FlexDress Everyday" policy back in 2018, with its human capital leader Trillion So saying it would let staff "dress more comfortably," and sectors such as FMCG, retail, media, and IT have since drifted further toward casual than finance or law have dared to. Richard Sharma of recruiter Page Personnel Singapore called the shift "a mental game," because employees still worry that dressing down reads as not being effective — which is a very Singaporean anxiety, tidily quantified. The city-state's Hofstede power-distance score sits at 74, high enough that hierarchy still dictates a great deal about who gets to experiment with their wardrobe and who doesn't; a junior analyst in sneakers is making a different statement than a managing director in the same sneakers.

What makes Singapore genuinely strange to newcomers isn't the formality itself but its geography. A Reddit thread in r/askSingapore, started by an American intern worried about overheating in business casual, drew replies describing offices as "cold as fuck" thanks to industrial-strength air conditioning, with locals advising a cardigan as standard kit and noting that IT and enterprise roles have loosened considerably since the pandemic, down to collared tees and jeans even among senior managers. The net effect is a country where you dress for an imaginary European office indoors and a tropical street outdoors, often on the same ten-minute walk, and where the formality gradient runs less by industry than by floor, building, and how recently your company's HR department updated its PDF.

Bangladesh's formality, by contrast, is openly contested terrain, and 2025 proved it. Bangladesh Bank's HR department issued a circular in July instructing women to wear sarees or salwar-kameez with dupattas in simple colours, to avoid short sleeves and leggings, and instructing men to skip jeans and gabardine for formal shirts and trousers, with non-compliance treated as a disciplinary matter, as Prothom Alo reported. A spokesperson framed it as preventing "psychological disparity" among roughly 200 to 250 fresh graduates joining each batch. The backlash was fast enough that Governor Ahsan H Mansur, reportedly learning of it through the media while travelling abroad, ordered its withdrawal within days, with the bank insisting afterward that "no policy decision was taken" — only advice from a departmental meeting, per The Business Standard. Bangladesh's Hofstede uncertainty-avoidance score of 60, far above Singapore's 8, suggests a culture more comfortable codifying such rules in the first place, and considerably less comfortable when the codification backfires in public.

The broader context matters here too. According to the ILO's Dhaka office, the ready-made garment sector alone directly employs roughly 4.2 million workers, around 60% of them women, and indirectly supports some 40 million Bangladeshis — about a quarter of the population. For most of that workforce, "dress code" means a factory uniform, not a saree-versus-leggings debate conducted in a central bank memo; the formality argument that makes headlines in Dhaka's white-collar offices is a minority experience even within Bangladesh's own labour market, which is worth remembering before treating the Bangladesh Bank episode as representative of anything beyond itself.

---

### The Reckoning

Put the two countries side by side and the irony sharpens. Singapore, materially richer, hotter, and more globally plugged in, nonetheless over-dresses out of institutional habit and air-conditioned denial, with formality enforced mostly through unspoken floor-by-floor convention rather than written policy — a soft, diffuse pressure that rarely makes the news because nobody ever drafts a memo about it. Bangladesh, materially poorer and arguably under greater pressure to modernize its image abroad, keeps attempting to write the rules down explicitly, and keeps discovering that writing them down is precisely what turns a quiet norm into a public fight. Singapore's low uncertainty-avoidance score lets ambiguity sit comfortably for decades; Bangladesh's much higher score produces the opposite instinct, and then the opposite consequence.

The counterintuitive bit is who ends up more exposed. In Singapore, a foreigner who misjudges the dress code mostly just looks a little out of place — embarrassing, forgettable, rarely consequential. In Bangladesh, an institution that misjudges the dress code risks becoming a national story within a week, governor included. One country's formality is a private discomfort; the other's is a public referendum, and you will not know which kind you've wandered into until someone else writes the headline.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/askSingapore — a contributor warns an incoming intern that the office air-conditioning runs cold enough to need a cardigan year-round, even as another notes that IT and enterprise dress codes have loosened sharply since the pandemic, with collared tees and jeans now common even among senior managers.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a tech employee asked whether being told to stop wearing six-inch-inseam shorts, while women in the same office wore skirts, was worth an HR complaint; most replies joked, but several flagged the gendered double standard and suggested checking the written policy before escalating.*

> *Must Share News — after an American content creator claimed Singaporeans only wear suits and ties, local commenters pushed back, saying the formally dressed people he saw were simply on their way to the office, that neighbourhoods outside the business district are full of shorts and slippers, and that only upscale restaurants actually enforce a dress code.*

> *Dhaka Tribune, op-ed — a columnist argued that Bangladeshi institutions wrongly treat "smart" as a synonym for "formal," pointing to a university dormitory that banned salwar-with-T-shirt as "obscene" even though its residents were adult women in an all-female building, and asked who gets to decide what counts as improper.*

> *Expat.com forum — a new arrival in Dhaka admitted she skipped wearing a headscarf early on, drew stares in public as a result, and wondered whether her jeans and T-shirts were part of the problem, but found her actual colleagues "totally non-judgemental" about it once she was inside the office.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is less about fabric than about where the rules actually live. In Singapore, formality is unwritten, floor-specific, and air-conditioning-dependent — pack layers, watch your particular office rather than the skyline, and don't mistake the CBD for the country. In Bangladesh, formality is more often explicit, more conservative by default, and occasionally rewritten in public within the same news cycle — dress modestly and a notch more formally than you think necessary, and don't assume a relaxed written policy, or a relaxed unwritten one, will survive contact with the next management change.

If a friend asked me over a drink which one to pack for first, I'd say: bring the cardigan for Singapore and the long sleeves for Dhaka, and in both places, dress for the organisation chart, not the thermometer.
