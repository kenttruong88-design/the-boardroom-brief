**20 Days vs. 160 Million Unused Ones: The Leave Culture Gap Between Fiji and Australia**

**🇫🇯 Fiji · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Fiji legally guarantees its workers just 10 days of annual leave a year. Australia guarantees roughly double that — and its workers are currently sitting on an estimated 160 million days of it, unused, according to HR platform ELMO's employee sentiment research. One country has a leave shortage. The other has a leave surplus it refuses to spend. Neither situation is actually about how much vacation time is on the books.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇯 Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for exactly 10 statutory annual leave days unless your specific employer offers more | Assume a "generous Pacific lifestyle" automatically means generous time off |
| Adjust your own schedule expectations to "Fiji time" rather than fighting it | Schedule tight back-to-back meetings assuming everyone will be punctual |
| Treat your 10 sick days (available after 3 months) as a real, separate entitlement from annual leave | Confuse sick leave and annual leave — they don't carry over the same way, and sick leave needs a medical certificate |
| Expect a 13th-month bonus or year-end payment as increasingly standard practice | Treat a 13th-month payment as a guaranteed legal right rather than a growing custom |
| Show real respect to older colleagues and managers — hierarchy here runs on age, not just title | Assume flat, informal office dynamics just because the overall culture feels relaxed |
| Confirm in writing whether unused leave carries over — it depends entirely on your employer's own policy | Assume unused leave automatically rolls into next year |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Actually book and take your 20 statutory leave days — unused leave is a liability on your employer's books, not a trophy on yours | Let leave accumulate for years assuming you'll "use it eventually" |
| Expect a quieter office in the week before Christmas, when many colleagues take leave for family time | Schedule a major product launch for the last week of December |
| Say plainly if workload is the reason you're not taking leave — it's a recognized, named problem here | Suffer silently under a heavy workload assuming that's just how it is everywhere |
| Use leave loading (often an extra payment on leave taken) as real, calculable compensation, not a curiosity | Ignore leave loading when negotiating total compensation |
| Take the "doona day" or a mental-health day seriously if your workplace offers one | Treat any day off as something to apologize for |
| Plan around roughly a dozen public holidays plus your 20 days — the real annual time-off ceiling is higher than the headline number | Assume 20 days is the full extent of your time off for the year |

Fiji's leave system is small by design and, by the standards of most industrialized economies, quite thin: Rivermate's guide to the country's labor rules puts the statutory minimum at 10 working days of annual leave after a full year of service, with carryover left entirely to each employer's own policy. Sick leave matches it almost exactly — another 10 days, paid, but only available after three months on the job and requiring a medical certificate, with no government-funded backstop if an employer's obligation runs out. What the law doesn't capture, and what every expat guide to working in Fiji mentions almost immediately, is "Fiji time" — a genuinely looser relationship with punctuality and scheduling that coexists, a little paradoxically, with a leave allowance that is itself quite strict and limited. The workplace is also explicitly hierarchical, with real deference paid to age and seniority, which shapes who actually feels comfortable asking for extra time off in the first place.

Australia's problem is the mirror image of Fiji's: not too little leave, but a cultural inability to actually use what's offered. The ELMO Employee Sentiment Index for Q3 2024, surveying more than 1,000 Australian employees, found the average worker sitting on nearly 16 unused annual leave days, with 22 percent holding four weeks or more in reserve. Gen X and Baby Boomers hoard the most — over 20 days on average — while Gen Z, notably, holds onto the least, at under 9 days. Sixty-one percent of respondents cited a real barrier to taking leave at all: heavy workload led the list at 21 percent, followed by saving leave for emergencies and simply not being able to afford a holiday. ELMO's Chief People Officer, Kate Wilkinson, put the quiet tragedy of the number plainly: "Annual leave isn't just for going on a vacation... we shouldn't lose those, because it's so important to have downtime and to switch off." Meanwhile the statutory floor itself — about 20 days a year on top of roughly a dozen public holidays — is genuinely generous by global standards, enough that, with planning, an employee could realistically be away from work for 50 days a year counting weekends.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real inversion: Fiji's leave culture is constrained by law but seemingly unbothered by it — nobody in the expat forums complaining about "Fiji time" is actually complaining about missing vacation days, they're describing a place that has made peace with a slower, looser relationship to the clock generally. Australia's leave culture is generous by law and visibly tormented by the gap between entitlement and use — a workforce that fought for, and won, a strong statutory floor, and now can't bring itself to stand on it. Both numbers tell you less about vacation policy than about trust: Fiji workers trust the informal rhythm of the day more than the formal leave calendar; Australian workers trust neither their workload nor their job security enough to actually take the leave the law already gave them. Hofstede Insights has not published an individual score for Fiji, but Australia's own profile — low power distance (38), high individualism (90), and a notably indulgent 71 — tracks with a culture where leave is negotiated individually rather than handed down as a fixed entitlement.

If you're choosing where to build a career around real rest, the paperwork favors Australia decisively. The lived experience doesn't automatically follow the paperwork — a generous leave balance that nobody uses is, functionally, not that different from a thin one that everybody expects to be thin from the start.

> *Great Managers (Australian workplace guide) — The guide notes a specific, recurring culture clash in reactions to the Australian system: Americans tend to find 20 days of annual leave striking, while British arrivals, used to 25 days at home, are "less impressed" — the same number landing as either generous or merely adequate depending entirely on where you're counting from.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Fiji for work, the honest adjustment isn't about the thinness of the leave entitlement — it's about recalibrating your entire relationship to scheduled time, leave included. If you're moving to Australia, the entitlement itself won't be the problem; your own willingness to actually use it, against a workplace culture that keeps quietly discouraging you from doing so, will be.

Ask either country's hiring manager not "how many leave days do I get," but "when was the last time someone on this team actually took all of theirs." In Fiji, the honest answer is a shrug about the calendar. In Australia, it might be a slightly uncomfortable pause — and that pause tells you more than the number on the offer letter ever will.
