# Chain of Command: Germany's Org Chart vs. Italy's Family Tree

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇮🇹 Italy**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Germany will give you an org chart, a decision-making process, and a start time, and expect you to respect all three with equal seriousness. Italy will give you a rank system just as real, then let you argue your way around it over a two-hour lunch. Both countries are, on paper, hierarchical. Only one of them lets the hierarchy bend for a good enough reason, delivered with enough charm.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive a few minutes early to every meeting, without exception | Treat "the meeting starts at 10" as a suggestion; starting on time is non-negotiable here |
| Address colleagues formally (titles, last names) until invited to switch to first names | Default to first names and casual tone on day one; it can read as presumptuous |
| Come to meetings with a clear agenda and stick to it | Use meeting time for relationship-building small talk; that happens elsewhere, not on the clock |
| Expect to route decisions through each level of management in turn | Expect a fast, informal yes from one enthusiastic manager; sign-off usually climbs the chain |
| Leave work at work — colleagues rarely socialize after hours, and that's not a snub | Read the lack of after-work socializing as coldness; it's a boundary, not a rejection |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Italian — a large share of colleagues won't default to English even in business settings | Assume English will carry you through client or even internal meetings |
| Treat the first meeting as trust-building, not deal-closing | Expect a decision on the spot; agreements typically take longer and more conversation than you'd plan for |
| Dress elegantly — appearance reads as professionalism | Show up underdressed; it undercuts credibility before you've said a word |
| Expect a real lunch break, sometimes up to two hours | Schedule back-to-back meetings through the midday window; much of the country has gone home to eat |
| Respect the family-run firm's informal chain of command, even when it isn't on paper | Assume a flatter title structure means decisions bypass the family at the top |

### Germany: The Org Chart Is Not a Suggestion

German corporate culture runs on predictability, and the predictability is the point. Communication favors clarity over pleasantries — directness isn't rudeness here, it's efficiency, according to guidance from life-in-germany.de aimed at newcomers navigating their first German office. Punctuality carries the same weight: arriving early for a meeting is baseline, and lateness reads as a signal of disrespect rather than a minor inconvenience. The decision-making process reflects the same instinct toward order — each level of management typically reviews and signs off before a decision moves forward, which is slower than many newcomers expect from an economy this efficient, though some firms are experimenting with flatter, faster approval chains.

The frustration that surfaces most often from people actually living inside German corporate structures isn't the hierarchy itself — it's the bureaucracy wrapped around it. Discussion threads on Hacker News, where a disproportionate number of participants are software engineers who've worked inside German firms, return again and again to the same complaint: Germany's administrative and procedural overhead is heavy, and it lands hardest on people who aren't German and don't yet know which form unlocks which process. One frequently echoed sentiment is that the country is efficient at the things it has already decided to be efficient about, and strikingly slow at everything adjacent — a distinction that matters enormously if your job depends on moving fast on something new.

### Italy: Hierarchy, Negotiated in Real Time

Italian corporate culture keeps a real rank system — one guide to working in Italy as an expat describes "a strict rank system that respects power and age" — but the rank system coexists with something Germany's doesn't: live, in-the-room negotiation of outcomes, even with someone above you. Meetings are frequently described as chaotic, with multiple conversations running in parallel and discussion drifting from the agenda as people make their case (housinganywhere.com). Decisions rarely happen on the spot; the first meeting is often about establishing trust rather than reaching a conclusion, and subsequent rounds take the time they take.

Family ownership remains the structural backbone for a large share of Italian firms, and it changes how authority actually flows regardless of what the title on someone's door says — personal relationships, trust, and recommendations from people you already know carry more functional authority than formal process (italychronicles.com). The standard workday — often 9:30 to 18:30 with a lunch break stretching up to two hours — isn't laxness; it's a different allocation of the same working hours, and the numbers back up the stereotype less than you'd think: only about 3% of Italian employees report working extended hours, against an OECD average of roughly 10%, suggesting the long midday pause is a trade against evening overtime rather than a shorter workday overall.

Hofstede's framework puts a number on part of this contrast: Italy scores 76 on individualism against Germany's 67, and — more tellingly for office dynamics — Italy's long-term orientation sits at 61 versus Germany's notably higher 83, reflecting how much more Germany's corporate culture plans, schedules, and defers gratification relative to Italy's.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that Italy, with its looser-looking meetings and family-first structures, actually has a harder, more personal hierarchy to break into — you need relationships and trust, accumulated over time, before the formal rank chart stops mattering as much. Germany's hierarchy is more rigid on paper but, once you understand the process, more learnable: follow the chain, hit your deadlines, and the system treats you the same way it treats everyone else, with no extra credit for charm.

The other inversion: Germans separate work and personal life so cleanly that not being invited out after hours is normal, not a slight — while Italians fold relationship-building directly into the workday, which means a newcomer who treats lunch as purely functional is quietly opting out of exactly the relationship capital Italian offices run on.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — One commenter who documents German bureaucracy professionally described it as "a beast — especially for non-Germans," framing it less as occasional friction and more as a structural constant that foreign hires underestimate before they arrive.*

> *Hacker News — A German engineer who has spent his career in US tech companies contrasted the two systems directly, describing German corporate process as methodical to the point of slowing down decisions that other cultures would make in a single conversation — a trade-off he characterized as thoroughness bought with speed.*

> *italychronicles.com — A write-up on Italian workplace stereotypes notes a specific cultural misread: newcomers expect the extended lunch and relaxed meeting pace to signal low ambition, when the real story is a workforce that works roughly as many hours as anyone else, just arranged around a different midday rhythm.*

> *housinganywhere.com — The guide's practical advice for incoming expats is blunt: don't expect a first meeting to produce a decision. Multiple conversational rounds, sometimes louder and more digressive than a newcomer expects, are simply how Italian teams build the trust a decision eventually rests on.*

### Conclusion

If you want a system you can learn once and rely on, Germany will reward the effort of understanding its process — just budget real patience for the bureaucracy underneath it. If you want a workplace where who you know will eventually matter more than what's written on the org chart, Italy will get you there, but only after you've put in the lunches. The thing I'd actually tell a friend: in Germany, show up on time and you're already ahead; in Italy, show up for lunch and you're already ahead.
