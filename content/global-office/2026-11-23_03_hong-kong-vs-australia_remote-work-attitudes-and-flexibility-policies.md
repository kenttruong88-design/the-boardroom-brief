# Hong Kong Counts the Desks, Australia Counts the Days at Home

🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In October 2025 a Fair Work Commission deputy president ordered Westpac to let a long-serving employee work from home five days a week, finding the bank's evidence for a two-hour commute to its Kogarah office "generalised and insufficient," according to HCA Magazine. In Hong Kong, a 2024 survey reported by Bloomberg found over 90 percent of firms urging staff to spend more time in the office, the highest share among major Asian hubs. Both cities have broadband. They differ in what they believe a person with broadband will do when unsupervised.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get any remote or hybrid arrangement into your employment contract in writing | Assume a verbal promise survives a change in management |
| Ask for specific office days, since there is no statutory right to request flexible work | Cite the Fair Work Act as though it applied |
| Check the cross-border tax and immigration position before working from another jurisdiction | Treat a month in a holiday flat as a neutral decision |
| Plan for sector differences, because finance and client-facing roles tend to be stricter | Assume your industry follows the same rules as the one next door |
| Offer a role-specific business case, with productivity and security covered | Lead with the commute as your main argument |

### Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the formal flexible work request process and keep the written reasons | Accept a refusal that fails to give detailed written reasons |
| Check whether your state has its own work-from-home law, since Victoria has moved ahead | Assume the federal rules are the only ones in play |
| Ask what your team's actual attendance practice is, not just the policy | Trust a stated minimum without watching the car park |
| Document your performance while remote | Rely on goodwill when a new manager arrives |
| Raise equipment and secure-access costs at the start | Absorb the cost of a monitor and call it flexibility |

## Hong Kong: Flexibility by Permission

According to Clyde & Co, Hong Kong employees have no statutory right to request flexible or hybrid work, with arrangements set mainly by employer policy and contract. Lewis Silkin, writing in January, makes the same point from the other side: Hong Kong has no equivalent of the detailed process in Australia's Fair Work Act, and the nearest legal pressure comes from anti-discrimination law, including family status and disability. Hybrid work is common in finance and professional services, but its continuation is at the employer's discretion.

The numbers show a gap between demand and supply. Clyde & Co cites Census and Statistics Department figures showing about 290,000 employees, or 10.6 percent of the non-government workforce, with flexible arrangements, against a 2026 Morgan McKinley finding that 70 percent of Hong Kong professionals rank working from home as their top benefit. In an earlier Morgan McKinley survey, 80 percent preferred hybrid or remote work, and only 20 percent were happy with five office days. A Bloomberg Intelligence survey of 350 workers found 27 percent would ask for a raise if required to attend five days a week, most wanting at least 6 percent, and 24 percent would change jobs. Employers, meanwhile, have been tightening. The South China Morning Post reports that HSBC told client-facing staff to be in the office five days a week, managing directors four, and everyone else at least three.

Attitudes, as opposed to rules, are equally revealing. The South China Morning Post cites a PwC survey of 1,043 employees finding that nine in ten Hong Kong workers preferred remote work, but only 45 percent had the option. It also cites an Instant Group survey finding that Hongkongers average 41 working hours a week, the third highest in Asia-Pacific, with just seven days of annual leave. The same report quotes workers who value in-person contact, which is a reminder that the preference is not unanimous, only widespread. A workforce that puts in long hours and receives little discretion over where to put them is not obviously lacking in commitment. It is lacking in a procedure.

Office landlords have their own view. Hong Kong's office vacancy hit a record 16.4 percent at the end of 2023, according to CBRE as cited by Bloomberg Intelligence, which may explain some of the enthusiasm for filling desks that exist anyway. Employers also cite cybersecurity, data protection and cross-border tax and immigration concerns, according to Clyde & Co. These are legitimate, if convenient.

## Australia: Flexibility by Process

Australia has a statute for it. As Lewis Silkin describes, the Fair Work Act sets out a formal process for flexible work requests, and an employer may refuse only after discussing the proposal, trying in good faith to reach agreement, considering the consequences for the employee and giving detailed written reasons. In the Westpac case, the Commission found the bank had not met those requirements and ordered the arrangement granted. HCA Magazine reports that the Greens' Right to Work from Home Bill, which would give all employees a right to request two days a week, is before a Senate committee, and that Victoria has moved first at state level.

The Victorian details are worth reading with care. K&L Gates reported that the Equal Opportunity Amendment (Work from Home) Bill was introduced on 16 June 2026, with full-time employees entitled to up to two days a week where reasonable, employer responses due within 21 days, and small businesses deferred to July 2027. HCA Magazine describes the right as applying from September 2026. Sources differed on timing and status, so confirm the current position before relying on it.

The public sector supplies the politics. HCA Magazine reports that the New South Wales government's August 2024 memo required more than 430,000 public servants to work principally in an approved workplace, and that a plan to bring all federal public servants back to the office was dropped before the last federal election. It also reports that 80 percent of Australian Public Service staff used some flexible arrangement in the 2025 State of the Service survey. A country that withdraws a mandate before an election has, in a sense, conducted a referendum on lunchtime Zoom calls and received a clear answer.

Private employers are less sentimental. Commonwealth Bank asked its 49,000 staff to attend the office at least half the time, and Yahoo Finance reports that an Employment Hero study found around half of hybrid and remote workers would consider quitting if forced into the office full time. The workers are therefore not asking for a favour. They are asking for a continuation of an arrangement they have already built their lives around, which is a more stubborn position than a request.

Practice is less radical than the headlines suggest. HCA Magazine cites the Australian Bureau of Statistics as finding 36 percent of employed Australians worked from home in some capacity in 2024, down from 37 percent in 2023, and the Australian HR Institute as finding almost half of organisations require two or three office days, with 18 percent requiring four or five. The Morgan McKinley survey put Australian employers pushing for more office time at 65 percent, against 91 percent in Hong Kong. Hofstede Insights offers a possible explanation, scoring Australia at 90 on individualism and Hong Kong at 25, though a score does not explain a Westpac ruling.

## The Reckoning

The difference is in who holds the default. In Australia, the worker asks, the employer must engage and justify a refusal, and a tribunal can overrule a poorly reasoned no. In Hong Kong, the employer decides, the worker negotiates, and the only guarantee is whatever the contract says. Both places have remote workers. Only one has given them a procedure.

Pay and trust reinforce the divide. Hong Kong's employees say they would trade a raise for flexibility, and employers reply with attendance rules, which suggests the market has priced flexibility highly and employers have declined to supply it. Australia's employers also push for the office, but face a process that obliges them to explain why. The result is not paradise, as the CBRE and HCA figures show, but it is at least an argument conducted on written terms.

## Conclusion

For a worker choosing between the two, the safest course is to treat the policy as a contract term, not a mood. In Hong Kong, negotiate the arrangement in writing before accepting the offer, expect sector variation, and keep tax and immigration rules in mind if the plan involves working from elsewhere. In Australia, learn the flexible work request process, check the state rules, and keep records of any refusal.

Neither system offers a guarantee, and either can be undone by a new boss with a new floor plan. The law may decide whether you can ask, but the employer still decides how long you stay. A sensible applicant will therefore ask about attendance expectations at the interview stage, when the answer is cheapest to hear and the leverage is greatest, rather than in week three, when it arrives as a calendar invitation.
