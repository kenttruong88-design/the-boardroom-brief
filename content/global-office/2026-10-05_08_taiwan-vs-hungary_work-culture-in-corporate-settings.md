**The Responsibility System and the 5pm Shrug: Corporate Culture in Taiwan and Hungary**

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇭🇺 Hungary**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Taiwan, staying late isn't a rule written anywhere — it's an unstated expectation that calcifies into a thousand unpaid hours before anyone official admits it happened. In Hungary, the workday mostly ends when it says it will, minimal oversight included, right up until someone suggests skipping lunch — at which point the entire office unites in horror. Both cultures call themselves flexible. They mean wildly different things by it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect open-plan offices with real social texture — colleagues who eat together daily and check in when you're sick, even as a foreign hire | Assume the open layout means low hierarchy; managers still hold real authority even amid the friendliness |
| Read "staying as long as your team stays" as the actual unwritten policy, regardless of what your contract says | Assume a listed 8-hour day is the real day; variable 6-9+ hour stretches tied to team presence are common |
| Accept thoughtful gestures (farewell gifts, a boss buying you medicine) as genuine relationship-building, not favoritism | Mistake personal warmth from a manager for an exception to hierarchy — both can be true at once |
| Expect genuine, heated debate across rank in some workplaces — rigid-hierarchy stereotypes don't always hold in practice | Assume all Taiwanese firms underpay overtime by policy — but verify your specific employer's actual overtime practices before signing |
| Ask directly, before signing, how overtime requests and approvals actually work at your specific company | Assume "flexible hours" marketing matches daily reality — ask current employees, not just the recruiter |

#### 🇭🇺 Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real autonomy over how and when you do your work within a standard 9-to-5/6 schedule, with minimal micromanagement | Assume unpaid overtime is normal or expected; it's uncommon and not the cultural default here |
| Take your full lunch break (30-60 minutes) seriously — and bring a real meal; a sandwich won't cut it for most colleagues | Skip lunch to power through tasks; it reads as odd rather than admirably dedicated |
| Expect more formal conduct with superiors even in an otherwise casual office — jokes with peers, "official" tone upward | Treat every manager as rigidly formal; smaller companies especially can be notably relaxed |
| Show up for team-building events (paintball, escape rooms) and the long multi-course meal that follows — it's genuine social glue | Expect remote work parity with employee preference; a real gap exists between what staff want (2+ remote days) and what employers offer |
| Expect direct disagreement and detail-oriented pushback in discussions — it reflects engagement, not hostility | Mistake consensus-seeking for strength; in Hungarian business culture, too much consensus can actually read as a lack of conviction |

### Taiwan: Warm Colleagues, an Unwritten Clock

Taiwanese corporate culture resists easy stereotyping, and firsthand accounts make that clear fast. One American who worked multiple jobs in Taiwan described, in detail, a workplace that beat his American jobs on basic human connection: open floor plans with 50-plus colleagues fostered constant informal interaction, ritualized group meals made eating alone on a workday feel strange, and colleagues — despite his status as a foreign intern — bought him medicine when he was sick and gave him thoughtful farewell gifts when he left, gestures he noted would raise nepotism concerns in an American office. He even observed "heated debates" crossing rank lines, directly contradicting the rigid-hierarchy stereotype.

But the same account surfaced the real cost clearly: an unstated, never-written expectation to stay as long as his coworkers stayed, producing variable 6-to-9-hour days tied to team presence rather than individual workload — a far cry from the explicit hour caps and "work-life balance" rhetoric of his American government job. That informal pressure has shown up at industrial scale before: roughly 1,000 HTC engineers once signed a letter protesting mandatory 12-hour days with no overtime pay for over a year, accumulating more than 1,000 unpaid hours each under what employees called a "responsibility system" — management's defense being technical compliance with labor law and a formal (if hard-to-access) overtime request process. Academic research on Taiwanese working hours backs up the pattern at the national level: long hours remain common even where individual preference, and the law on paper, both point the other way.

### Hungary: Autonomy by Default, Lunch as a Civic Duty

Hungarian corporate culture, by multiple accounts, centers on real day-to-day autonomy once you're inside the standard schedule. Expat.com's guide to Budapest's work culture describes employees largely handling "how and when" they complete their work with minimal tight oversight, standard hours running roughly 9am to 5 or 6pm, and overtime genuinely uncommon and typically unrewarded when it happens — the inverse of Taiwan's informal overtime pressure. Communication style splits cleanly by direction: casual, joke-filled exchanges with peers, "more official" conduct reserved for superiors, though smaller firms relax even that.

Food is treated as closer to policy than preference: the same guide stresses that "sandwiches or salads won't be enough for most" Hungarian workers at lunch, with a real 30-to-60-minute break expected as standard, not an aspiration. PwC's large-scale employee survey (over 29,400 respondents) adds useful texture to the generational picture: younger workers (19-28) rank base pay, flexible scheduling, and predictability as top priorities, while more experienced employees weight "the personality of their immediate superiors" unusually highly — and a genuine expectations gap has opened on remote work specifically, with 72% of employees wanting at least two remote days weekly against just 68% of employers willing to offer three in-office days as the compromise. Commisceo Global's management guide adds a structural note that cuts against the "flexible and laid-back" stereotype: Hungarians are described as highly individualistic at work, skeptical of forced team conformity, with consensus sometimes read as a sign of weak conviction rather than healthy agreement.

### The Reckoning

The real inversion here is about where the pressure lives. Taiwan's formal rules (an 8-hour nominal day, overtime law on the books) look reasonable on paper, while the actual informal culture — team presence, unwritten expectations, a "responsibility system" that can absorb a thousand unpaid hours — does the real work of extending the day. Hungary runs almost exactly the other way: the formal structure (set hours, real autonomy, protected lunch) holds up in practice far better than its reputation for post-communist bureaucratic rigidity would suggest, with the genuine friction showing up instead in a narrower, more modern argument over remote-work days.

The other contrast: Taiwan's warmth is interpersonal and immediate — meals, gifts, a boss who notices you're sick — layered on top of real structural pressure. Hungary's warmth is structural and procedural — protected time, real autonomy — layered on top of more individualistic, arm's-length relationships day to day. Neither country's social temperature predicts its actual workload.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blake Stephen Anderson (personal blog, Taiwan) — A former intern described Taiwanese coworkers who "ritualized group meals to the point where it felt weird to eat alone on a workday" and bought him medicine when he was sick — a pleasant surprise that, by his own account, outpaced any bond he built in 1.5 years at a comparable American job.*

> *Blake Stephen Anderson (personal blog, Taiwan) — The same account flagged the practical frustration underneath the warmth: an unstated expectation to stay at the office "as long as coworkers stayed," producing variable 6-to-9-hour days with no explicit policy anyone could point to or push back against.*

> *PhoneArena reporting (Taiwan) — Roughly 1,000 HTC engineers once signed a letter describing 12-hour days with no overtime pay sustained for more than a year, under a "responsibility system" that made the extra hours feel mandatory rather than optional — a cautionary data point for anyone told overtime in Taiwan is simply "voluntary."*

> *Expat.com, Budapest — A community guide to Budapest's corporate culture flagged a specific cultural misread to avoid: "sandwiches or salads won't be enough for most" Hungarian colleagues at lunch, so newcomers planning to eat light and power through the afternoon should expect to stand out, not blend in.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two on pure workload honesty, Hungary's formal structure is closer to its lived reality than Taiwan's is — ask a Taiwanese employer directly, before signing, what actually happens after the nominal workday ends, because the written policy and the office norm can diverge by hours. If what you want most is genuine day-to-day warmth with your colleagues, Taiwan's accounts suggest it's real and not performative — just budget emotionally for the unwritten clock that comes with it. And wherever you land, in Budapest specifically: pack a real lunch.
