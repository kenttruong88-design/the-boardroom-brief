## The Dupatta Question: Dressing for the Office in Delhi and Karachi

**🇮🇳 India · 🇵🇰 Pakistan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Pack for an office job in Delhi and you'll be told, sincerely, that a saree is the single most respected garment you can wear to an important meeting — and also that almost nobody under thirty can drape one without help. Pack for Karachi and you'll be told your shalwar kameez is fine without a dupatta, except when it very much isn't, and nobody will specify in advance which meeting is which. Both wardrobes are negotiating the exact same tension — tradition, modesty, and a rising tide of Western tailoring — and arriving at almost opposite daily defaults.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to modest, well-pressed clothing — clean lines matter more than brand or cost | Wear shorts, short skirts, or tight, revealing clothing to a general office; it draws unwanted attention |
| Treat the saree as the highest-status option for senior roles, law, banking, and academia, or for important meetings at any level | Assume you need to wear a saree daily — Indo-western and pant-suits are standard for most professional women |
| Check your specific sector: tech, banking, and startups skew Western and casual; law, government, and traditional finance skew formal and conservative | Assume one dress code applies nationally — Mumbai's startup floor and a Delhi law firm are different planets |
| Feel free to mix Indo-western: a fitted kurta with trousers or jeans is now mainstream office wear | Show up in Western business formal assuming it reads identically to how it would in London or New York |
| Ask a local colleague before a big client meeting — the "power move" garment changes by industry and seniority | Overthink your first week; comfort while you calibrate is more forgivable than a wrong formality guess |

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear shalwar kameez as your default professional baseline — it's accepted business casual almost everywhere | Assume a dupatta is mandatory everywhere; conventions vary by workplace and city |
| Always cover arms and legs, regardless of the specific outfit you choose | Wear tight-fitting or figure-hugging clothing to the office, for men or women |
| Expect men in banks, multinationals, and top government posts to wear full Western business formal — suit, tie, dress pants | Assume denim or fusion wear is acceptable everywhere; it depends heavily on whether the office is formal, semi-formal, or creative |
| Identify which of the three tiers your workplace falls into — formal corporate (banks, law, multinationals), semi-formal (NGOs, schools, agencies), or creative/relaxed (startups, media) | Assume a single national dress code exists — the range between a Karachi bank and a Lahore design studio is enormous |
| Lean into modern fusion (a Chikankari top with trousers, a modest co-ord set) if your office is progressive — it reads as stylish, not unprofessional | Wear something borrowed from Western red-carpet norms wholesale; local modesty expectations still apply even in fashion-forward offices |

### India: A Wardrobe With a Hierarchy Built In

Indian office dress isn't one code, it's several, stacked by sector and seniority. High-tech, banking-adjacent back offices, and startups have imported fairly casual Western norms, while law, traditional banking, and government roles still expect visibly formal, often traditional dress (Wisestep, World Business Culture). For women, the three real options — the saree, the salwar kameez, and Indo-western fusion — aren't interchangeable in status: the saree carries the most institutional weight, generally read as senior-management or client-meeting attire, while a kurta-and-trousers combination or a Western pantsuit is what most working women actually wear day to day (Naaree, Iconik). One HR professional cited in workplace-style coverage put it plainly: a kurti or pantsuit is simple to manage at work; a saree, if you're not practiced at draping one, is genuinely difficult (Naaree).

Expats report a predictable arc: arriving in Western business formal, then gradually adopting Indian or Indo-western pieces after realizing the country's actual dress norms are less uniformly conservative — and less uniformly anything — than they'd assumed before landing (expat account via Mytriphack/Iconik coverage). The core constant across sectors is modesty rather than formality per se: clean, ironed, non-revealing clothing reads as professional almost everywhere, even in the most casual tech offices (Wisestep).

### Pakistan: Modesty Is Fixed, Formality Is a Sliding Scale

Pakistan's baseline is more uniform than India's on the modesty axis — arms and legs covered, no tight or figure-hugging silhouettes, for men and women alike — but highly variable on the formality axis (Salt by Ideas, Ling App). The shalwar kameez functions as the default acceptable business-casual option nationwide, with the dupatta's necessity depending on the specific workplace rather than any fixed national rule (Ling App). Offices sort into three visible tiers: formal corporate environments like banks, multinationals, and law firms; semi-formal spaces like schools, NGOs, and marketing agencies; and creative or relaxed environments in startups, media, and fashion, where denim and fusion silhouettes are increasingly normal (La Mosaïk, The Woven Palace).

Men in top government posts, multinationals, and banks are expected to wear conventional Western business formal — suit, tie, dress trousers — mirroring UK corporate norms fairly closely, according to business etiquette guides covering the country (Doing Business in Pakistan guide, Ling App). The last decade has visibly loosened what counts as acceptable within that modest baseline: Chikankari-embroidered tops paired with trousers or jeans, and modern co-ord sets, are now legitimate professional options in less traditional offices (JEEM, The Woven Palace) — modesty as the fixed constraint, formality and cut as the variable.

### The Reckoning

Both countries are running the same underlying negotiation between heritage dress, modesty, and imported Western tailoring, but they've split the variable differently. In India, the axis that moves most is formality-by-sector — a Bangalore tech office and a Delhi high court could not look more different, while the acceptable range of Western-vs-traditional within each is comparatively settled. In Pakistan, the axis that moves most is Western-vs-traditional by workplace type, while the modesty floor — covered arms and legs, no tight clothing — barely shifts regardless of how formal or relaxed the office is.

The practical trap for a newcomer is assuming the two countries' most conservative and most liberal poles map onto each other. A Mumbai fintech startup's casual dress code and a Karachi design studio's fusion wear look superficially similar in photos; the underlying logic generating each is different, and importing one country's calibration into the other will read as either overdressed or subtly off.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking how to dress for an office in India was advised that the safest early strategy was simply to watch what senior women in the same specific office wore during the first week, since even two floors of the same building could have visibly different unspoken norms.*

> *r/pakistan — A woman starting her first corporate job described being caught off guard that her semi-formal, sleeveless-arms-covered outfit was completely appropriate at her NGO but would have been considered underdressed at a friend's job in private banking down the street — the tiering, she said, was something nobody explained in advance.*

> *The Local (South Asia expat network) — An HR consultant working with foreign hires in Delhi noted that expats who over-invested in traditional Indian formalwear before arrival often ended up overdressed in casual startup offices, while those who assumed a uniformly Western dress code were underdressed the first time a client meeting called for something more traditional.*

> *Quora — A contributor describing typical Pakistani women's fashion clarified that modern outfits, including fitted trousers and contemporary silhouettes, are common in major cities, pushing back against outside assumptions that professional Pakistani dress is uniformly conservative or unchanging.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A poster working at a multinational's India office noted that Western business formal worn to an early client meeting was read as slightly stiff and overly formal by local colleagues, who suggested a well-tailored kurta set would have signaled seniority more effectively than a suit.*

### Conclusion

If you're packing for either move, resist buying a single "professional wardrobe" before you land — the right answer depends entirely on your specific sector and office, not the country as a whole. In India, figure out where your workplace sits on the formality ladder before deciding how traditional to dress. In Pakistan, assume modest coverage is non-negotiable everywhere and let the office's specific tier tell you how Western or fusion you can go. My honest advice, over a drink: buy less than you think you need before you arrive, and spend your first paycheck on whatever your new colleagues are actually wearing.
