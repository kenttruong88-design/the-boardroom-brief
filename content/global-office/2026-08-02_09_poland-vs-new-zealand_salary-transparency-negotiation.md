**The Number Nobody Says Out Loud: Salary Talk in Poland and New Zealand**

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇳🇿 New Zealand**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Poland is about to be legally forced into salary transparency it didn't especially ask for, care of an EU directive with a hard 2026 deadline. New Zealand has no such directive coming, no legal push, and a culture that treats "so, what do you make?" as somewhere between mildly rude and a genuine breach of etiquette. Both countries will tell you they value fairness. Only one of them is being made to prove it on a timeline.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research market rates independently before interviews — pay data is still fragmented | Expect your specific employer to have published a transparent salary band yet |
| Ask directly about the salary range early in the interview process | Assume asking early is rude — it is now a legally protected pre-employment right |
| Track how new EU-driven disclosure rules apply to your specific employer through 2026 | Assume pay secrecy clauses are still enforceable the way they used to be |
| Negotiate confidently — Poland's labor market has genuinely tightened in employees' favor | Assume your starting offer reflects what colleagues in the same role actually earn |
| Watch for gender pay gap reporting requirements rolling out at larger employers | Assume transparency rules apply equally to small and large employers right away |

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do independent salary research — SEEK and Robert Half guides are genuinely useful benchmarks | Expect a colleague to volunteer their salary, even a close one |
| Negotiate anyway, even though over half of Kiwis reportedly don't — it works in your favor | Assume your reluctance to negotiate is shared caution rather than a real cost to your pay |
| Time negotiation conversations around performance review cycles for the best leverage | Bring up pay outside of formal review windows unless there's a clear trigger (new offer, role change) |
| Be direct and honest about your expectations — Kiwi negotiating culture rewards straightforwardness | Use pressure tactics or games; New Zealand's negotiating style is collaborative, not adversarial |
| Check your contract for pay secrecy clauses — discussing pay outside work is generally your legal right | Assume a lack of open salary talk means a lack of legal protection to discuss it if you choose to |

---

Poland's coming transparency shift isn't cultural evolution so much as regulatory imposition, and the timeline is specific: the EU Pay Transparency Directive requires full transposition into Polish law by 7 June 2026, and Poland has taken a two-phase approach, with pre-employment disclosure requirements already in effect since late December 2025 and the remaining gender-pay-gap-reporting and full salary-band requirements due by the June deadline. Until recently, Polish workplace culture ran on the same pay secrecy default common across much of Central and Eastern Europe — Quora threads on salary taboos generally cite Sweden as the notable regional exception where open pay discussion is culturally tolerated, implicitly marking most of the rest of the region, Poland included, as still operating on discretion. What's unusual about Poland's situation isn't the cultural instinct toward privacy — that's ordinary — it's that the culture is now being legislated out of its own default, whether individual employers are ready or not.

New Zealand's pay secrecy runs deeper and has no legislative deadline forcing a reckoning. A SEEK study found 52 percent of Kiwis didn't negotiate their salary at all during their most recent hiring process, and cultural commentary consistently frames asking a colleague what they earn as "uncouth and confrontational" — a strong pair of words for a culture that otherwise prides itself on directness. New Zealand's Hofstede profile actually leans toward low power distance and moderate individualism, the same broad cluster that predicts open communication in most contexts, which makes the pay-secrecy exception genuinely notable rather than a simple extension of general reserve. Some employers actively benefit from that discomfort persisting, since it removes internal pressure to correct pay gaps discovered through comparison. Legally, most New Zealand employees are entitled to discuss their own pay outside pay-secrecy clauses — the taboo is social, not statutory, which makes it more durable, not less: there's no compliance deadline coming to force the issue the way Poland now has.

What both countries share is a striking gap between formal negotiating leverage and how much of it employees actually use. New Zealand's own guides encourage direct, honest negotiation as culturally appropriate and generally well-received, yet the majority of workers skip it anyway. Poland's incoming transparency laws will hand employees genuine new information leverage, but early guidance for employers repeatedly notes that most Polish companies still haven't operationalized real salary bands, meaning employees may have a legal right to information that doesn't practically exist yet at their specific employer.

**The Reckoning.** Poland is being dragged toward transparency by external regulatory force on a hard deadline; New Zealand could choose transparency at any time and has consistently chosen not to, despite a generally open, low-hierarchy communication culture in nearly every other context. That makes New Zealand's opacity the more interesting case, culturally — it's not explained by Hofstede's usual predictors, it's closer to an inherited taboo about money specifically, distinct from the country's broader directness. Poland's shift, by contrast, will likely arrive faster and more completely than New Zealand's, purely because it's being imposed rather than culturally negotiated — a rare case where regulation outpaces culture instead of following it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — A thread on expected salary ranges in Poland described candidates repeatedly having to build their own market-rate estimates from scattered, unofficial sources, since even multinational employers operating in Warsaw frequently declined to share a concrete range until very late in the hiring process.*

> *TeamBlind — A separate post benchmarking a specific Google role in Warsaw against other European offices became a de facto crowdsourced salary database in the comments, illustrating how much informal, employee-driven information-sharing is currently doing the job that formal transparency rules are only now catching up to.*

> *Quora — A response to a general question about salary taboos singled out Sweden as a notable regional exception where open pay discussion is culturally tolerated as long as it doesn't read as bragging, implicitly framing the more common Central and Eastern European default — Poland included — as one of continued discretion, at least for now.*

> *Substack (brokemillennial.substack.com) — A personal finance newsletter making the case for asking coworkers about salary argued that pay secrecy survives mainly because it benefits employers more than employees, and that the discomfort of the conversation is a much smaller cost than the compounding pay gap silence tends to protect.*

> *Geekzone (New Zealand tech community forum) — A thread asking whether discussing salary is even legal in New Zealand revealed genuine uncertainty among posters, several of whom weren't aware that pay-secrecy clauses require an actual contract term to be enforceable — a sign of how effectively cultural taboo has substituted for actual legal knowledge.*

### Conclusion

If you're negotiating a role in Poland right now, don't wait for the 2026 rules to catch up to your specific employer — ask for the range directly and treat the coming legislation as leverage, not a reason to be patient. If you're negotiating in New Zealand, recognize that the silence around pay is a social convention with no legal teeth behind it, and that the majority of Kiwis leaving money unclaimed by not negotiating is not a cultural inevitability you have to share.

The version for a friend: in Poland, the law is about to make the awkward conversation for you, so get ahead of it. In New Zealand, nobody's coming to save you from the awkward conversation — you're going to have to have it yourself.
