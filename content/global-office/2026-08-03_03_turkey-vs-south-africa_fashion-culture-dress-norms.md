# Suit Up in Istanbul, Suit Yourself in Cape Town

**🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇿🇦 South Africa**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Turn up to a first meeting in Istanbul in sneakers and you will be read, instantly and silently, as unserious. Turn up to a first meeting in Cape Town in a full suit and you may be read the same way, for the opposite reason. Both countries judge you by what you wear to work. They have simply not agreed on which direction the judgment runs.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a full suit and tie to a first meeting, especially in banking, government, or established corporates | Don't assume a "creative" job title exempts you — appearance signals respect and professionalism broadly |
| Dress more conservatively outside Istanbul and Ankara, and especially outside the big cities generally | Don't wear anything tight-fitting or overly revealing — it invites unwanted judgment regardless of intent |
| Adjust for age norms — sharper, more formal dressing is expected the older and more senior you are | Don't remove your jacket or tie in a formal meeting unless someone senior does first |
| Keep grooming polished — well-groomed appearance is treated as a proxy for competence | Don't assume small or family-run companies follow the same formality as banks and state institutions — ask first |
| Lean smart-casual in summer (shirt, trousers, no jacket) once you've read the room | Don't wear stiletto heels or short skirts in traditional or provincial office settings |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to smart-business attire for a first meeting until you've clocked the specific office | Don't assume Cape Town's casual tech-and-creative norm applies in Johannesburg's finance sector, or vice versa |
| Bring a jacket even in warm weather — offices are often heavily air-conditioned | Don't wear shorts or flip-flops to any office meeting, regardless of how casual the culture seems |
| Watch what successful, senior colleagues wear before calibrating your own wardrobe | Don't over-formalize in Cape Town's startup and creative scenes — a full suit can read as trying too hard |
| Feel free to incorporate traditional African prints tastefully in business settings — many offices welcome it | Don't assume "business casual" means the same thing across industries — banking and law firms still expect real formality |
| Ask HR directly if genuinely unsure — South Africans respond well to visible effort to get local norms right | Don't judge the whole country's professionalism by one city's dress code — the regional gap is real and wide |

---

Turkish workplace dress operates on a fairly stable logic: formality signals respect, and respect is the currency that actually moves business forward. In official companies, banks, and universities, a well-cut suit is closer to a professional obligation than a style choice, and the [World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/turkey/dress-code/) guide notes explicit age-based gradations — over-35 professionals dress more conservatively and formally by default, while under-30 employees have more room for a trendier, smart-casual register. This isn't just aesthetic conservatism; it tracks with Turkey's high score on Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance dimension (85, against South Africa's 49), a culture that prefers clear rules and visible signals over ambiguity — and a suit is, among other things, an unambiguous signal. Private and family-run firms relax the rules somewhat, but the default assumption a foreign hire should walk in with is: overdress first, adjust down once you've actually seen the office.

South Africa refuses to give you one national answer at all. Expatica's guide to [South African business culture](https://www.expatica.com/za/working/employment-basics/south-african-business-culture-105988/) describes a dress code that's "conservative but not formal" as a baseline, with suits reserved mostly for corporate and financial settings — but the more useful data point is regional: Johannesburg and Durban professionals lean into blazers, tailored pants, and polished dressing as something close to a personal brand statement, while Cape Town's creative and tech-heavy scene has normalized what one recent feature called "casual Friday every day," with jeans and sneakers standard even client-facing. A foreign hire who dresses for "South Africa" as a single culture will either overdress badly in Cape Town or underdress badly in Sandton, because the real variable isn't the country, it's the city and industry.

**The Reckoning:** Turkey's formality is vertical — it scales with hierarchy, age, and institution type, but the underlying rule (dress up, not down) holds fairly consistently everywhere in the country. South Africa's formality is horizontal — it varies by city and industry more than by seniority, which means the exact same blazer that reads as competent in Johannesburg reads as slightly try-hard in Cape Town. The practical trap for a relocating professional is assuming a single packing list works for either country: in Turkey, the risk is underestimating how much a suit matters outside the biggest cities; in South Africa, the risk is assuming any one city's norm generalizes to the whole country. Both mistakes get read the same way by locals — as a small tell that you didn't do your homework before you arrived.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Responding to "How should a woman dress in Istanbul in order to be respectful?", one contributor noted that tight-fitting clothing drew far more unwanted attention in daily life than modest-but-unremarkable outfits did — the specific cut mattered more than the amount of skin shown.*

> *Expat.com (Istanbul guide) — A guide to corporate culture in Istanbul described new arrivals underestimating how much dress and grooming functioned as a stand-in for seniority and respect in meetings, to the point that showing up underdressed to a client meeting could be read as a mild insult to the client, not just a personal style choice.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey — South Africa has repeatedly ranked near the bottom of InterNations' annual expat rankings, with Johannesburg specifically cited for weak marks on safety and affordability — a context that several long-term expats said shaped a more visibly polished, buttoned-up professional presentation in Joburg than the same person might choose to project in a lower-stakes environment.*

> *Expat.com (South Africa forum) — A thread on business etiquette in South Africa advised newcomers to bring at least one genuinely formal outfit regardless of city, since client-facing meetings in finance and law could appear on short notice even at otherwise casual-leaning companies.*

### Conclusion

Pack for the job title and the specific office, not the country. In Turkey, that means erring toward more formal until proven otherwise, with the dial turning down slightly for youth and for private, informal employers. In South Africa, it means finding out which city and which industry you've actually landed in before deciding whether a suit reads as professional or slightly desperate. The honest tip Priya would give a friend packing for either move: buy one good blazer, bring it everywhere, and watch the room for the first two weeks before you decide whether to wear it.
