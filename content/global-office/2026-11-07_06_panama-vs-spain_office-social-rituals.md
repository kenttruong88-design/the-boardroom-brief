**Lunch Is Sacred in Madrid and Negotiable in Panama City, Which Tells You Everything**

🇵🇦 Panama · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ask a newly arrived employee in Panama City what time lunch ends and you will get a shrug; ask the same question in Madrid and you will get a small philosophy lecture. Spain has spent decades defending its two-hour midday break as close to a civilizational achievement, even as Brussels periodically raises an eyebrow at the productivity math. Panama, a country whose Hofstede individualism score sits at just 11 — among the lowest Hofstede Insights has recorded anywhere — treats group warmth as a baseline condition of office life, not a scheduled event. Both countries will tell a newcomer that the work culture is "relaxed." Only one of them means the clock itself is negotiable.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇦 Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every colleague individually each morning, even if it adds ten minutes to your commute to your desk | Skip the greeting round to save time — it reads as cold, not efficient |
| Accept the first lunch invitation without over-negotiating logistics | Treat a group's after-work plan as optional once everyone else has already committed |
| Expect Friday lunches, especially client ones, to run well past an hour | Schedule anything consequential right after a 1pm lunch slot |
| Contribute something small to an office birthday "torta" celebration | Decline the cake on dietary grounds without a graceful, pre-rehearsed excuse |
| Ask about coworkers' families — it comes up constantly and is not small talk | Treat questions about your own family as intrusive; reciprocity is expected |
| Negotiate your starting salary firmly before accepting | Assume a first offer reflects what the role is actually worth locally |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out 2–4pm mentally; almost nothing of substance happens then | Schedule a call for 2:30pm and expect your Spanish counterpart to pick up |
| Join the office's 11am coffee-and-tortilla break when invited | Eat a sandwich alone at your desk — it reads as antisocial, not disciplined |
| Say yes to the Friday caña even if you can only stay twenty minutes | Order a pint and call it the same thing as a caña — it will be noticed |
| Let sobremesa, the post-meal conversation, run its course before excusing yourself | Pay the bill and leave the instant plates are cleared |
| Default to "tú" once a colleague signals it's fine, usually within days | Assume Madrid's pace and Seville's pace are interchangeable |
| Plan around August; entire departments empty out for weeks | Schedule a major deliverable for the last two weeks of August |

Panama's workplaces run on proximity rather than programming. Hofstede Insights puts the country's power distance at 95, among the highest scores in its global dataset, while individualism sits at 11 — a combination that, on paper, suggests a rigid, hierarchical office. In practice, the rigidity applies to who makes decisions, not to who gets a hug in the hallway; a manager can run a tight chain of command and still order lunch for the whole floor without anyone finding it strange. Gallup's global well-being surveys once placed Panama first in the world for respondents who described themselves as "thriving," a result now over a decade old but still cited by HR consultancies operating there as shorthand for why Panamanian offices default to warmth rather than distance.

That warmth has real limits, and they are economic rather than cultural. Panama's National Institute of Statistics and Census (INEC) and ILO figures both point to a regional workweek that runs longer, in raw hours, than Spain's — Central American economies average well above 2,000 hours a year against Spain's roughly 1,633, per OECD data — and a Saturday half-day is still standard at many firms, absorbed into the week rather than resented as an imposition. But on expat and local-worker forums, a recurring theme is that after-work socializing for Panamanian staff is constrained less by preference than by paycheck: entry-level monthly wages that sit around $600 to $800 leave little room for a round of drinks downtown, which is part of why the office birthday cake, not the happy hour, functions as the great social equalizer.

Workforce surveys from firms like Deloitte Global, which track employee engagement across Latin America, consistently note that Panamanian workers rank interpersonal trust in management higher than compensation satisfaction — an ordering that would strike a Northern European HR department as backwards and strikes nobody in Panama City as odd at all. The upshot for a foreign hire is practical: showing up, remembering names, and accepting the cake will do more for your standing in a Panamanian office than any amount of competent, impersonal delivery on deadlines. Competence is assumed; warmth has to be demonstrated daily, on the clock, in person.

Spain's relationship with lunch is a matter of institutional record, not folklore. The Instituto Nacional de Estadística has tracked the slow, contested erosion of the two-hour midday break for years, and national coverage — including reporting by Bloomberg and The Local — has chronicled repeated, mostly unsuccessful attempts by employers and governments to compress it toward a northern-European nine-to-five. Spain's Hofstede individualism score of 51 sits almost exactly at the global midpoint: more collectivist than the Anglophone world, considerably less so than Panama, which helps explain why the Spanish office lunch reads as communal ritual rather than extended-family meal. Colleagues share a table because it is civilized, not because anyone considers them kin.

The lunch itself is defended with an intensity that outlasts any one government's reform attempts, because it is never really about the food. Sobremesa — the conversation that follows the meal, often running twenty or thirty minutes past the last plate — is where a surprising share of actual office relationships get built, which is why cutting it short reads as a minor social offense rather than efficient time management. Set against this, the OECD's 2023 figure of 1,633 average annual hours worked puts Spain near the European middle, not the exhausted bottom some stereotypes imply: Spaniards are not working less so much as distributing the same workload around an immovable midday anchor.

After-work socializing follows a lower-stakes script than Panama's family-inclusive version — a quick caña and a plate of tapas, rarely a full weeknight dinner, and rarely strictly obligatory, though several accounts from workers there suggest that declining twice in a row gets quietly noted. HR commentary cited by outlets like The Local has pointed out that this clipped, optional version of after-work bonding coexists with one of the longer in-office days on the continent — Spaniards often don't leave until 7 or 8pm — because the long lunch, not a short workday, is where the social investment actually happens. A manager who skips the Friday caña is forgiven; one who eats alone at a desk every day is, eventually, quietly reclassified as difficult.

Here is where the two cultures diverge in ways no onboarding packet will flag. Panama's extreme power-distance score suggests a top-down office; in practice, hierarchy dissolves completely at the lunch table, because people simply matter more than rank once the workday meal starts. Spain's far more moderate power-distance score suggests a flatter, more egalitarian workplace; in practice, that flatness evaporates at 2pm, when junior analyst and managing director alike defer equally to the immovable authority of lunch itself. Both countries end up with remarkably classless midday tables — they just arrive there by opposite logic, one through personal warmth, the other through institutional habit.

The after-work picture inverts again. Panama's team events tend to pull in spouses and children without anyone formally asking; Spain's Friday caña is pointedly colleague-only, a boundary that would read as standoffish in Panama City and as entirely unremarkable in Madrid. A newcomer who mistakes Panama's warmth for informality, or Spain's lunch discipline for rigidity, will misjudge both rooms within the first month, usually in opposite directions.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a longtime poster on Panama's expat board noted that many Panamanian colleagues skip after-work drinks entirely, since an entry-level local salary of roughly $600 to $800 a month leaves little room for bar tabs, so the real socializing happens through cheap, informal meetups instead.*

> *Expat.com forum — one new arrival to Panama recalled accepting a first job offer of around $800 a month, then renegotiating once it became clear colleagues respected assertiveness, eventually earning close to three times that after refusing to undersell their own experience.*

> *Reddit (r/AskEurope) — a Spain-based commenter said that a desk lunch of a sandwich and a protein bar, unremarkable elsewhere, would likely get a worker side-eyed or quietly reprimanded in a Spanish office, where a proper seated meal is the unwritten rule.*

> *Reddit (r/AskEurope) — another Spain-based commenter pushed back on the siesta stereotype directly, explaining that a daily nap is now mostly a weekend or tourist-town habit, since most ordinary office jobs simply don't leave room for one.*

> *The Local Spain — an English-speaking transplant described being surprised that sobremesa, the lingering post-lunch conversation, routinely pulls in office colleagues, turning what sounded like idle chatter into the place where real work relationships quietly take shape.*

### Conclusion

If the decision comes down to one variable, it is this: in Spain, belonging is earned through calendar discipline — show up for lunch, respect sobremesa, don't skip too many cañas — and the warmth follows the schedule. In Panama, belonging is closer to a default setting, extended automatically through greetings, shared cake, and family inclusion, though the depth of that inclusion tracks income more closely than it tracks goodwill. Someone who optimizes for predictable boundaries should lean toward Spain, where the rules are rigid but at least transparent. Someone who optimizes for fast personal connection, at the cost of fuzzier hours and a Saturday that isn't entirely their own, should lean toward Panama.

Either way, budget patience for the first six weeks, and do not schedule anything important at 2:15pm in Madrid or skip the office cake in Panama City — both mistakes are forgiven once and remembered twice. The honest version for a friend over a drink: Spain will make you wait for lunch and call it culture; Panama will make you family and call it Tuesday.
