# Ireland Wrote Remote Work Into Law. Mexico Just Let You Show Up and See What Happens.

**🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ireland has a statute for this. Since March 2024, employees there have had a legal right to request remote work, employers must respond in writing within four weeks, and the government's own review found a 94 percent approval rate on requests, with only 7 percent refused outright. Mexico has no such framework — no digital nomad visa, no codified right to request anything, just a temporary resident visa that "functions almost exactly like" a remote work permit if your income originates abroad, tolerated in practice by immigration authorities without being formally defined in law. One country legislated the ambiguity out of remote work. The other left the ambiguity in place and simply let tens of thousands of people work through it anyway.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the Temporary Resident Visa route if you're working remotely for a foreign employer or clients | Assume a "digital nomad visa" exists in Mexican law — it doesn't; you're using an adjacent visa category |
| Keep your income sourced clearly from outside Mexico | Take on Mexican clients or get paid locally while on this visa status — it risks your compliance |
| Set up a dedicated home workspace; noise and interruption management is a common first-month challenge | Assume all US or EU employers are comfortable with staff working from Mexico — many still restrict it |
| Track how long you stay and how "settled" your presence looks to authorities | Treat prolonged, recurring stays as risk-free; authorities have begun issuing informal guidance on this exact pattern |
| Build in flexibility for slower bureaucratic and banking processes as a remote resident | Expect the same day-to-day predictability on paperwork that a codified remote work law would offer |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit remote work requests in writing at least eight weeks before your intended start date | Assume verbal requests count, or that six months' service isn't required first |
| Expect a genuine, high-probability approval — the real-world refusal rate is low | Assume "right to request" means "right to remote work" — employers can still legally refuse with reasons |
| Push for clarity if you're in a rural area — awareness of these rights remains lower there | Assume every employee or employer knows this law well; awareness gaps are real and documented |
| Read your specific employer's hybrid policy carefully — most Irish firms settled into some hybrid model | Expect a fully remote default; most organizations expect meaningful in-office time as part of "hybrid" |
| Use the formal complaints process (Workplace Relations Commission) if a request is mishandled | Expect fast enforcement; case volumes are still low and the system is relatively new |

---

Ireland's remote work regime is the product of deliberate post-pandemic policy. The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 created a formal right to request remote or flexible work, and its first statutory review found the mechanism functioning better than skeptics expected: a 94 percent combined approval rate, with the Workplace Relations Commission logging only 70 individual complaints as of January 2026. The catch is a persistent awareness gap — only 48 percent of employees know the right exists, compared to 65 percent of employers, and usage is notably lower in rural areas. Politically, this was framed as more than convenience: government figures have explicitly tied remote and blended work to reduced commuting, lower transport emissions, and quality-of-life gains, and even as pandemic-era enthusiasm cooled, only around one in six Irish organizations required a full return to pre-Covid, all-in-office patterns.

Mexico took the opposite path: no legislation, no formal visa category, and a regulatory vacuum that the market simply filled. Immigration lawyers describe current practice bluntly — Mexican law "does not expressly regulate" digital nomad activity, and the National Institute of Migration tolerates it without defining it. Industry data shows roughly 69 percent of Mexican tech professionals now prefer at least some remote arrangement, and the country has become one of Latin America's most popular remote-work destinations by sheer accumulated practice rather than policy design. The risk sits quietly underneath the convenience: authorities have begun issuing informal guidance suggesting that longer or more settled stays, or work that starts to resemble local employment, could eventually trigger tax, labor, or immigration obligations nobody has fully mapped out yet.

The reckoning: Ireland gives you a slow, bureaucratic, but genuinely enforceable process — you know your rights, you know the timeline, and you have recourse if it's ignored. Mexico gives you speed and simplicity at entry, but asks you to personally manage an ambiguity that the state hasn't resolved for you, and that ambiguity compounds the longer you stay. An Irish worker moving to Mexico may be unsettled by the total absence of a formal remote work category. A Mexican-based remote worker moving to Ireland may be surprised to learn that "right to request" isn't the same as "right to work remotely" — your employer can still say no, on paper, for cause.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Mexico to work remotely for a US company described the biggest practical hurdle as finding an employer that didn't have a policy objection to staff working abroad in the first place — the legal question turned out to be secondary to the corporate HR question.*

> *Quora — A remote worker asking about long-term stays in Mexico was advised that while the temporary resident visa functioned well in practice, they should keep income clearly foreign-sourced and avoid taking local clients, since that specific pattern was the one drawing informal scrutiny from authorities.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech employee comparing return-to-office policies across companies noted that even firms nominally committed to hybrid work were quietly tightening in-office expectations, and that "hybrid" had become a much less standardized term than it sounded — advice that applies as much to Dublin offices as anywhere else.*

> *Quora — A poster asking how to build a productive home office in Mexico noted that closing doors and setting firm boundaries with household members were the two most underrated fixes, more impactful than any equipment upgrade.*

> *Reddit r/ireland — A worker who'd submitted a formal remote work request under the new legislation said the process itself was straightforward and the approval came within the legal window, but noted that almost none of their colleagues realized this right existed until they mentioned using it.*

### Conclusion

If you want your remote work arrangement legally protected, with a clear process and a real chance of enforcement if things go wrong, Ireland is the safer structural bet — you'll just need patience with the paperwork and the six-month wait. If you want speed, low friction, and don't mind operating in a space the government hasn't fully defined, Mexico remains one of the easiest places in the world to simply start working remotely, provided you keep your income foreign and your stay clean. The honest advice either way: know exactly which category you fall into before you get comfortable. If a friend asked me over a drink: Ireland if you want it in writing, Mexico if you're fine finding out as you go.
