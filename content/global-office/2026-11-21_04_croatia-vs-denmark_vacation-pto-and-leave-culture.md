# Croatia Grants Twenty Days and Dreams of Thirty-One; Denmark Grants Twenty-Five and Vanishes in July

🇭🇷 Croatia · 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Croatian law promises an employee four weeks of paid leave. Danish law promises five. The difference of five working days looks modest until one consults the surveys: 66 percent of Croatian workers told the MojPosao portal in 2024 that they want more leave, with 31 days the most popular figure, while a columnist in the Copenhagen Post reports Danish colleagues, already holding 25, cheerfully asking for more. Eurostat, meanwhile, puts the average Dane at 33.9 hours a week against an EU mean of 35.9. The prospective migrant is therefore choosing between two countries that disagree about what a week is for.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask in writing how many days the contract grants; the 2024 MojPosao average was 24.8 but ranged from 23 to 29 by employer type | Assume a foreign-owned company is the generous option; it averaged 24 days against 29 at Croatian institutions |
| Use carried-over leave before 30 June of the following year | Treat rollover as indefinite; the deadline is fixed |
| Plan bridge days around Thursday and Tuesday holidays, the national pastime known as spajanje | Expect banks and government offices to be open on a public holiday |
| Book summer leave early; most workers take the bulk of their days then | Promise a client a full month off in August without arranging cover |
| Keep your own record of days taken; the Ombudswoman lists denied leave among complaints | Accept cash-in-hand arrangements, which the Ombudswoman describes as illegal |
| Budget for a domestic holiday; 56 percent of Croatians planned one in 2026 | Assume leave means a trip abroad; only 17 percent planned one |

### In Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your collective agreement for a sixth week, which HK says many of its agreements add | Assume 25 days is the ceiling |
| Expect three consecutive weeks between 1 May and 30 September, with three months' notice from the employer | Let the employer schedule you into September without your consent |
| Agree carry-over of up to five days in writing by 31 December | Assume unused days roll over automatically; the employer must otherwise pay them out |
| Plan for the late-July slowdown and closed local shops | Schedule anything urgent for the last two weeks of July |
| Answer the July question of whether you are taking three weeks or four | Skip the summer holiday as a badge of honour |
| Use days as they accrue, at 2.08 per month, under the 2020 Holiday Act | Expect to wait a year before leave becomes usable, as under the old rules |

## Croatia

The Croatian floor is 20 working days after six months of continuous service, according to the leave-law summary maintained by VacationTracker, on top of public holidays. Employers in practice give more. The MojPosao survey, reported by N1, found an average of 24.8 days, but the spread tracks ownership: Croatian institutions give 29 days, state-owned companies 28, foreign-owned private companies 24, and predominantly domestic private companies 23. An expatriate who assumes the multinational is the generous option should note that, by this measure, it is not. Only 29 percent of respondents were satisfied with their allowance, down from 43 percent the year before.

The calendar compensates somewhat. Total Croatia News counted 14 entries on the 2022 public-holiday list and described the linking of holidays to weekends through a single day of leave as something close to a national pastime, while noting that the custom favours office workers and that medical and service staff often work through. Unused leave may be carried over until 30 June of the following year and must be paid out on termination, VacationTracker says. The Croatian Ombudswoman nevertheless lists denied leave and unpaid leave compensation among the complaints workers bring, which suggests that the statute and the practice occasionally part company.

Take-up has a financial ceiling. Alma Career's 2026 survey, reported by Total Croatia News, found that 32 percent of Croatians planned no trip at all this summer, up from 27 percent, and 60 percent of those cited lack of money. Only 3 percent planned 21 days or more of leave, down from 7 percent. Leave is a right; the beach, it appears, is a purchase.

Other absences are better provided for than the annual allowance suggests. EasyExpat's Zagreb guide says employees may take up to seven days of paid leave a year for significant personal reasons such as marriage, childbirth or a death in the family, and caps the full-time week at 40 hours, with overtime limited to 32 hours a month. VacationTracker adds that the employer pays the first 42 days of sick leave, at a minimum of 70 percent of average pay, though it notes most employers pay in full. The Croatian system is not stingy so much as unevenly distributed, and the unevenness is mostly a function of who signs the cheque.

## Denmark

Danish law grants 25 days, and since the 2020 Holiday Act the days accrue and may be used concurrently, at 2.08 days a month, according to HK, the commercial and clerical workers' union. Before the reform a newcomer earned leave in one year and took it in the next, a delay The Local's 2015 guide flagged as a trap for mid-year hires. Employees are entitled to three consecutive weeks between 1 May and 30 September, and an employer who wants to dictate the dates must give three months' notice. Up to five days may be carried over, but only by written agreement made by 31 December. Many HK agreements add a sixth week.

Culture does the rest. Kay Xander Mellish, an American writer in Denmark, reports that the standard July question is whether one is taking three weeks or four, and that skipping the summer holiday marks a person as a weirdo. She also describes an ice cream shop, a bicycle shop and a swimwear boutique closed for weeks at a stretch. Denmark.dk, the official portal, says the working week is 37 hours, that staying late is discouraged, and that business slows in late July. It cites OECD figures putting very long working hours at about 2 percent of Danish employees, against an OECD average of 11 percent.

The edges of the system are also generous. HK says sick days that fall during a holiday and exceed five can be replaced with fresh leave. Denmark.dk reports that the parent who gives birth receives 14 weeks of fully paid leave and the other parent two weeks, followed by nine paid weeks for each parent and up to 13 weeks that can be shared, and that parents may leave work at a reasonable hour for daycare pickups. A newcomer should read this as a description of what the employer expects the calendar to contain, not merely what the statute allows.

The arrangement is not free. Mellish notes that Danes are heavily taxed and that free time is treated as the compensation. The visitor from a country with weaker leave rules will find this either liberating or faintly unsettling, depending on how much of their identity is in the calendar.

## The Reckoning

Expat.com's survey of leave rules notes that the European Union guarantees at least four weeks of paid leave, which is exactly where Croatia's statute sits, and that Denmark shares 25 days with Austria, Sweden and France, behind only Estonia's 28 and Luxembourg's 26. Neither country is an outlier; Croatia is the floor and Denmark is the respectable middle of the upper tier.

On paper, Denmark wins by five days, a better notice regime and a statute that lets leave be used as it is earned. In practice the comparison is closer than the headline suggests, because Croatia's holiday calendar and bridge-day habit add effective time off, and its best employers, the state and large institutions, match or beat the Danish minimum at 28 to 29 days. The Croatian weakness is distribution: which employer one works for matters more than which law applies, and the Ombudswoman's complaint list suggests enforcement is uneven.

Hofstede Insights scores Croatia at 73 for power distance and 80 for uncertainty avoidance, against 18 and 23 for Denmark. That is a reasonable shorthand for the difference in tone: a Croatian manager is more likely to treat leave as something approved within a hierarchy, while a Danish one treats it as a statutory fact about the calendar. Eurostat's figures on hours, 33.9 for Denmark against an EU average of 35.9, fit the pattern, though the Croatian figure was not available in the release consulted.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat blog (How to Live in Denmark) — An American writer reports that July conversations revolve around whether you are taking three weeks or four, that small shops simply close for weeks, and that she skipped vacation herself one year because her daughter's graduation cost roughly DKK 8,500 before extras.*

> *Copenhagen Post — A US-born columnist says the first day back from summer holiday is the most depressing of the year, that colleagues respond by asking for even more time off, and that the polite reply is a joke and a change of subject.*

> *Total Croatia News — A Denver-born marketer working from Osijek for American clients found that time off was hard to fit around client calls, that people were understanding when she could not take all of August, and that some landlords doubted a remote job was a real one.*

> *Total Croatia News — A visitor who spent a month working from Zagreb noticed locals lingering two hours over a double espresso and concluded that the slower pace was good for his mental health.*

## Conclusion

Read the contract, not the country. In Croatia, ask which kind of employer is offering and how many days it grants, and plan around 30 June for any carry-over. In Denmark, check the collective agreement for the sixth week, confirm when your own accrual starts, and learn to make no urgent arrangements in late July. In both, keep your own count of days taken. Where a conversation about time off feels awkward, remember that in Denmark the statute makes it routine and in Croatia the survey data suggest most colleagues would like more days than they have, so the request is unlikely to shock anyone.

The choice comes down to whether you prefer a holiday calendar that rewards ingenuity or one that rewards punctuality. The Croatian will take a Friday and call it a bridge; the Dane will take July and call it a Tuesday.
