**America Shouts Its Salary Range. Singapore Just Raises an Eyebrow.**

**🇺🇸 United States · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In one country, a job listing without a salary band now reads like a restaurant menu without prices: suspicious, possibly a scam, certainly not worth your time. In the other, asking a colleague what they earn remains one of the more efficient ways to end a dinner party early. Nine U.S. states and a growing list of cities now require employers to publish pay ranges before they've even finished pretending to care about your "growth mindset." Singapore, true to form, has written extensive, thoughtful tripartite guidelines on fair hiring — and declined, so far, to mandate that anyone actually say the number out loud.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 United States
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the state-mandated pay range on the listing before you apply — it's often legally required now | Assume the posted range is the real range; the bottom and top are sometimes 80K apart on purpose |
| Counter an offer in writing, politely, with a number attached | Give your current salary first if a recruiter asks; several states have made that question illegal to ask |
| Use public data (Levels.fyi, BLS occupational wage tables) to anchor your ask | Treat the first offer as final; in US corporate culture, not negotiating reads as not caring |
| Negotiate total comp — bonus, equity, PTO — not just base | Discuss salary with coworkers in a tone that suggests you think it's shameful; it's a protected activity, not a secret |
| Time your ask around a signed competing offer if you have one | Expect the transparency law to mean the employer won't still lowball you anyway |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let the recruiter name a figure first, then counter gently | Ask a Singaporean colleague their salary; treat it as roughly equivalent to asking their weight |
| Use your CPF statement and market data from recruiters, not Glassdoor, as your benchmark | Assume the job ad's vague "competitive package" means anything at all |
| Negotiate the whole package — bonus months (the "13th month"), relocation, housing | Push hard in the first meeting; direct confrontation over money reads as aggressive, not confident |
| Ask HR privately and specifically, rather than publicly or in a group interview | Expect a published pay range; it's encouraged in guidance, not required by law |
| Lean on a trusted recruiter as your intermediary for the actual number | Bring up pay before the offer stage; it can read as presumptuous |

America's relationship with wage data is a study in contradictions: a government that collects wages with obsessive, granular precision through the Bureau of Labor Statistics' dozen-odd survey programs, attached to a culture that until recently treated your own paycheck as roughly as discussable as your diagnosis history. That has changed fast. California, Colorado, Washington, New York, Illinois, Maryland, Connecticut, Hawaii, Nevada, Rhode Island and Massachusetts have each passed laws requiring employers to disclose pay ranges in job postings, and federal labor law has protected a worker's right to discuss their own wages with colleagues since long before any of these statutes existed. The result is less a national culture of openness than a patchwork so uneven that the same job can legally be advertised with a number in Denver and without one in Dallas.

What the laws have produced, reliably, is an ecosystem of amateur auditors. Levels.fyi built an entire company on the premise that official pay data was too thin and too slow, and its 2026 acquisition of compensation-benchmarking rival TechPays was framed internally as, in the company's own words, "another step toward global pay transparency" — a mission statement that assumes, correctly, that nobody was going to hand this information over voluntarily. Negotiation in the U.S. is now a semi-public sport: entire blogs exist to document how one engineer raised a single offer's total compensation by 30 percent through a well-timed counter, and refusing to negotiate at all is treated less as modesty than as a rookie mistake.

Singapore runs the opposite experiment. The Ministry of Manpower's Employment Act is detailed on how salary must be paid — frequency, itemised payslips, permitted deductions — and almost silent on what that salary should be or whether anyone else gets to know it. The Tripartite Alliance for Fair & Progressive Employment Practices (TAFEP) publishes thorough guidance on writing fair job advertisements, focused squarely on eliminating age, gender and race discrimination in hiring — and, notably, contains no requirement that the ad include a number. Unlike the European Union's pay-transparency directive or the patchwork of American state laws, Singapore has chosen guidance over mandate, which is either admirably light-touch or a convenient way of keeping wage-setting exactly as discreet as it has always been, depending on who you ask.

That discretion has a cultural backbone, not just a regulatory one. Hofstede Insights scores the United States far higher on individualism than Singapore (roughly 91 versus 20), which tracks neatly onto negotiating style: Americans treat pay as a personal achievement worth advertising and defending, Singaporeans treat it as a private family matter best handled through intermediaries, typically a recruiter, who absorbs the awkwardness on your behalf. The absence of a legal mandate hasn't stopped Singaporean workers from wanting the data — it has just pushed the demand underground, onto recruiter WhatsApp chats and the occasional candid LinkedIn post, rather than into statute books.

The Reckoning, stated plainly: America has legislated the awkward conversation out of existence and replaced it with a spreadsheet; Singapore has kept the awkward conversation and simply agreed, as a society, not to have it. Both approaches solve the same underlying problem — that employers have always known more about market pay than employees have — but they solve it in opposite directions. The American fix assumes more public data will produce fairer outcomes, and mostly it does, at the cost of a listing culture where companies now post deliberately wide, legally-compliant, practically-useless ranges to stay technically honest while telling you nothing. The Singaporean non-fix assumes that direct, confrontational pay disclosure would create more social friction than it's worth, and mostly that's also true, at the cost of leaving the burden of figuring out your own market value entirely on you.

For someone actually relocating, the practical difference lands hardest at the negotiation table itself. In the U.S., walking in with a printed BLS wage table and a Levels.fyi screenshot is normal, even expected. In Singapore, doing the same thing in front of a hiring manager will not get you a higher offer — it will get you a slightly confused hiring manager. The research is quietly validated the long way: wherever disclosure has spread, it narrows gaps between coworkers at the same firm, but does less to raise the overall pile of money being distributed. Neither country has solved underpayment. One has just made it easier to document.

> *Hacker News — a remote worker based in Singapore recounted a U.S. startup offering a fraction of standard Bay Area pay explicitly because of where the candidate lived, prompting them to decline and screen future remote offers far more carefully.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, the honest framing isn't "which country pays better" — it's "which kind of discomfort you'd rather have." In the U.S., the discomfort is upfront and procedural: you'll see the number, and then you'll be expected to fight about it, in writing, with data, possibly against a company that posted a deliberately useless range just to comply with the law. In Singapore, the discomfort is ambient and ongoing: you'll rarely see the number until the offer lands, and you'll spend your first year quietly interrogating recruiters and expat forums to find out if you got a good deal or merely a polite one.

Neither system was built with your negotiating comfort in mind, which is itself the most honest thing either government could tell you. If you want to know your worth before you walk in the door, move somewhere the law makes someone say it out loud. If you'd rather not watch the negotiation happen in public, Singapore will happily let the recruiter carry that burden for you — just don't expect them to tell you what the other candidates got.
