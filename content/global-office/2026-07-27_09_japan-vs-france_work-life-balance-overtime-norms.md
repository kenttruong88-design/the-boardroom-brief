**Sabisu Zangyo or the Right to Disconnect: Overtime in Japan and France**

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇫🇷 France**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

France legislated a right to ignore your work email after hours in 2017. Japan has a specific word — sabisu zangyo, "service overtime" — for the unpaid hours employees log anyway, on top of a legal cap that already permits up to 100 hours of overtime a month at large firms. One in ten Japanese employees now works more than 80 overtime hours monthly, per recent government survey data, putting a fifth of the workforce at documented risk of karoshi, death from overwork. The average French employee works roughly 1,490 hours a year; the average Japanese employee works around 1,611 — and that gap undersells the real difference, because so much of Japan's overtime goes unrecorded by design.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research whether your specific employer is Japanese or foreign-owned before you sign — the overtime culture differs sharply | Assume all Japanese companies work you equally hard; foreign firms in Japan are often meaningfully different |
| Track your actual hours carefully, including unpaid "service overtime" | Assume unpaid overtime doesn't count or won't accumulate against your wellbeing |
| Take the paid leave you're legally entitled to — reforms increasingly require it | Leave vacation days unused assuming that's simply expected of you |
| Watch what your manager and senior colleagues actually do, not just written policy | Trust the official 40-hour-week policy as a reliable predictor of real hours |
| Consider engineering or tech roles if work-life balance is a priority — they trend better than finance or traditional industries | Assume every white-collar role carries the same overtime intensity |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that the 35-hour week is a legal trigger point for overtime pay, not necessarily your real weekly hours | Assume 35 hours is universally the actual workweek — many roles, especially executive (cadre) positions, work more |
| Use your RTT days (time-recovery days) — they exist specifically to compensate hours worked above 35 | Let RTT days accumulate unused; they're a real, expected part of compensation |
| Respect the right to disconnect — avoid sending non-urgent emails outside work hours at companies over 50 employees | Expect immediate after-hours responses from French colleagues; the legal right not to reply is real |
| Expect overtime to come with a real premium — 25% for the first 8 hours, 50% beyond 43 | Work unpaid overtime assuming it's simply expected without compensation |
| Ask directly about cadre (executive) status during hiring — it changes your hours and overtime rules significantly | Assume your contract type doesn't affect your actual work-life balance; it's a major structural variable |

**Japan's** overtime culture persists despite genuine, repeated legal reform. The 2019 Work Style Reform capped large-company overtime at 100 hours a month with penalties for non-compliance, and extended the same hard cap to small companies by 2023 — real regulatory teeth. But the gap between law and practice remains wide: MHLW data shows a portion of overtime, sabisu zangyo, goes entirely unrecorded, performed out of a sense of obligation or loyalty rather than requirement. Karoshi remains a formally recognized cause of death, with compensation claims for overwork-related illness and suicide continuing to rise even as average headline work hours edge down. Sector matters enormously — engineering and tech roles, and especially foreign-owned companies operating in Japan, trend meaningfully better than traditional finance, consulting, or legacy manufacturing firms.

**France's** approach treats overtime as something to be actively priced and limited by statute rather than culturally discouraged. The 35-hour week, in force since 2000, functions as a legal trigger: work beyond it, and overtime premiums kick in immediately — 25 percent for the first eight hours, 50 percent beyond 43. Executive employees (cadres) typically work outside strict hourly tracking but receive RTT days instead, a structural trade of flexibility for guaranteed time off. The 2017 right-to-disconnect law, requiring companies with more than 50 employees to establish rules against after-hours email expectations, formalized something the culture already leaned toward — French workers report far less of the "always-on" pressure common in Japan or the US, and Dropbox's own coverage of the law found genuinely reduced after-hours email volume at compliant firms.

### The Reckoning

The real divergence isn't the number of legal hours — it's the gap between what the law says and what actually happens. France's legal framework and its lived culture are closely aligned: the 35-hour week broadly predicts real weekly hours, and the right to disconnect broadly predicts real after-hours behavior. Japan's legal framework and its lived culture have a much larger gap: a 40-hour statutory week and a 100-hour overtime cap coexist with a widespread unpaid-overtime practice that formal statistics undercount by design. If you're the kind of professional who trusts written policy as a reliable predictor of daily reality, France will reward that trust and Japan will punish it.

There's also a philosophical difference underneath the numbers. France treats rest as an enforceable right the state must actively protect from employer overreach. Japan, even mid-reform, still treats overtime substantially as a matter of individual loyalty and face — declining it, even when legally entitled to, can carry a real social cost that no statute addresses.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Japan, choose your employer with real care — the difference between a foreign-owned tech company and a traditional Japanese firm in the same city can be the difference between a 40-hour week and a genuinely damaging one, and the written policy will not reliably tell you which you're getting. If you're moving to France, trust the 35-hour framework, use your RTT days without guilt, and treat the right to disconnect as a real boundary, not a suggestion. My honest line for a friend over drinks: France wrote work-life balance into law and then mostly lives by it. Japan wrote it into law and is still negotiating with its own culture about whether to actually follow it — don't assume the paperwork tells you which side of that negotiation your specific employer is on.
