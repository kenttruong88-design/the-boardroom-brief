# The Potluck and the Pressure Cooker: Philippines vs. Hong Kong

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Manila, your officemate will notice if you skip the Friday potluck and ask, gently but pointedly, what's wrong. In Hong Kong, your officemate will notice if you leave the office before 9pm and assume, correctly, that you've found a new job. Both cities will tell you their workplace is "like family." Only one of them means it the way you're hoping.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring *pasalubong* (small souvenirs) back for the team after any trip, even a weekend one | Return from a trip empty-handed and expect no comment |
| Accept the invitation to a colleague's birthday *blowout*, even a small cubicle one | Decline food offered at your desk without a reason — it reads as a rebuff |
| Learn names of officemates' kids and ask after them specifically | Treat small talk before meetings as wasted time to be rushed through |
| Join the office group chat and actually respond in it | Go silent in the chat for days — it reads as withdrawal, not focus |
| Say yes to the first few after-work invitations, even tired | Frame your "no" as "I'm too busy," which can land as a status claim |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the mandatory-in-practice team dinner, even once a quarter | Leave a team dinner before the senior staff do |
| Keep a work phone reachable after hours, at least for messages | Assume "flexible hours" means fewer hours rather than looser scheduling |
| Learn the basic choreography of a business-card exchange | Hand over a business card one-handed or without a glance at it |
| Treat the lift ride and lunch queue as informal reporting lines | Discuss internal disagreements loudly in open-plan earshot |
| Book a holiday request well in advance, in writing | Expect an immediate verbal yes to replace the paper trail |

### The Philippines: Family, Formalized

The Philippines workplace runs on what researchers call *pakikisama* — a word that resists tidy translation but means something like "the active maintenance of smooth, agreeable relations," and which Filipino management consultants describe as showing up to the office party you'd rather skip, because not showing up is itself a kind of statement. Hofstede Insights scores the Philippines at 94 out of 100 on power distance, among the highest in the world, meaning hierarchy is respected, even performed — but the same culture scores low on individualism (32), which is where the warmth comes from: officemates are expected to function less like coworkers and more like an extended household, complete with the gossip, generosity, and mild guilt-tripping that implies.

That warmth comes with a cost measured in hours, not sentiment. According to the ILO, Filipino women work some of the longest average hours of any country tracked globally, a statistic the organization has flagged as a "role reversal" worth studying precisely because it runs against the usual global pattern. The Philippine Statistics Authority's labor force data shows overtime as a structural feature of the economy rather than an occasional event, and Filipino HR commentators increasingly describe "overtime culture" as something leadership actively needs to dismantle rather than quietly reward. The social rituals, in other words, are not a break from the long hours — they are how the long hours become bearable. The potluck is the thing that makes the overtime feel like something other than extraction.

The rituals themselves are specific enough to have their own informal vocabulary. A "treat" is whoever got promoted, had a baby, or passed a board exam buying lunch for the floor, no RSVP required because attendance is assumed. Birthdays are marked with a shared cake even in teams of forty, and skipping your own birthday celebration reads as a mild social offense rather than a scheduling preference. None of this is written into any HR handbook; it survives because leaving it out would make the office feel like a foreign country, which, after a transfer to Hong Kong, is exactly what a Filipino employee often reports it starting to feel like.

### Hong Kong: Efficient, Exhausting, Extremely Polite

Hong Kong's office culture inherited British formality and Cantonese business pragmatism in roughly equal measure, and the result is a workplace that is unfailingly polite while being, by most international surveys, one of the most overworked on earth. Multiple cost-of-living and workplace surveys have repeatedly ranked Hong Kong among the cities with the longest average working hours worldwide, and the territory's own government has studied the link between extended hours and employee deaths seriously enough that "shorter working hours" topped a public wish-list survey reported by the South China Morning Post — a genuinely startling thing for a wish-list to contain, in a city this wealthy.

Hofstede's power distance score for Hong Kong sits at a comparatively modest 68 — hierarchy matters, but less theatrically than in Manila — while its long-term orientation score (61) is among the highest Hofstede tracks, reflecting a culture that plans in decades and tolerates short-term discomfort for it. The social rituals reflect this calculus: team dinners happen, but they are transactional in a way Manila's potlucks are not — a chance to read the room, to be seen leaving last, to note who sat near whom. One Hong Kong workplace blog, aimed at employers, describes after-hours socializing less as bonding and more as "building a strong work-life balance" — a phrase doing a great deal of quiet work, given everything else on the page.

The etiquette around all this is unusually codified for a place that prizes informality elsewhere. Business cards are presented and received with both hands, read before being pocketed, never written on in front of the giver. The lift ride down to the lobby is treated as a semi-official extension of the meeting that just ended, and lunch queues outside a popular cha chaan teng function as a genuine, if unscheduled, channel for interdepartmental gossip — more reliable, some employees privately admit, than the meetings that preceded them.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trap: both cultures will describe their workplace as warm, and both will be telling the truth, but they're measuring different things. The Philippines measures warmth in attention — does anyone notice your mood, your kid's birthday, your trip home. Hong Kong measures it in reliability — does everyone show up, on time, for as long as it takes, without making a scene about it. A Filipino employee transferred to Hong Kong can feel the warmth evaporate even as colleagues remain scrupulously, exhaustingly correct. A Hong Kong manager transferred to Manila can feel smothered by an office that wants to know things about their personal life that a performance review never would.

The genuinely counterintuitive finding is that the Philippines, for all its *pakikisama* and family language, is the less individualistic culture on paper (32 vs. Hong Kong's 25) only by a narrow margin — the real gap is in power distance, not collectivism. Manila's hierarchy is loud and ritualized; Hong Kong's is quiet and procedural. Neither is flatter than the other. They just dress the org chart differently.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — One contributor describing their year at a Hong Kong office of a major accounting firm called it a year of "constant long hours," adding — half-reassuring themselves, half warning the thread — that they hoped it was the exception rather than the rule for the role going forward.*

> *expatarrivals.com — Elmer, a web developer from Davao City who relocated to Hong Kong for a telecoms job, described the work culture bluntly as "stressful, in my experience," noting that back home he rarely needed to stay late to finish urgent work, while in Hong Kong he found himself able to "stay at work at 2am if I needed to" — not because anyone demanded it in writing, but because the expectation sat there unspoken.*

> *bringingithome.substack.com — A Filipino writer now based abroad described pasalubong as something she saw "everywhere" growing up — fridge magnets, hard-to-find snacks, handmade coin purses — brought back for coworkers even after a short trip to a nearby city, and noted that she still instinctively looks for a small gift to bring colleagues after any trip, a habit she couldn't quite shake even in a culture that doesn't expect it.*

> *normschriever.com — An American writer who lived in the Philippines for over a year described Cebu's IT Park, dense with call-center workers, as "more alive at 3am than at midday," and observed that the inverted night-shift schedule common to BPO work "saps the constitution and disrupts a semblance of normal life" — a cost of the industry rarely mentioned in its recruitment material.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in Manila, decline food or invitations sparingly and explain why when you must — a flat "no" reads as a closed door. In Hong Kong, show up to the dinner, but don't mistake attendance for intimacy; you are there to be seen being reliable, not to be known.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for the office culture alone, the honest framing is: Manila will know you, and Hong Kong will know your output. Neither is wrong, and both will wear on you in different places — Manila in your calendar, which fills with obligations that are genuinely kind and genuinely relentless; Hong Kong in your evenings, which empty out quietly and without anyone ever quite telling you to stay. The thing I'd actually tell a friend over a drink: take the Manila job if you want colleagues who become something closer to friends, and take the Hong Kong job if you want colleagues who will never once ask about your weekend — and mean it as a mercy, not an insult.
