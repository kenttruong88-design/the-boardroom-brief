# New Zealand and Austria Both Claim to Have Solved Work-Life Balance. The Overtime Numbers Disagree.

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

New Zealand has been crowned the world's best country for work-life balance three years running, a title it has arguably won partly by declining to legislate what "work" actually means after 5 p.m. Austria took the opposite approach, writing overtime rules precise enough that an employee can calculate, to the half-hour, exactly how much unpaid labour they are not supposed to be performing — and then watch Statistics Austria confirm that 42.3 million hours of it happened anyway in 2024. One country regulates almost nothing and polls beautifully. The other regulates almost everything and still logged roughly 170 million overtime hours last year. Two opposing theories of rest, and neither one survived contact with the office.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm in writing whether overtime is paid — it's contractual, not automatic | Assume "salaried" means overtime is baked in and compensated |
| Take your full four weeks of annual leave; colleagues will ask why you haven't | Be the last one at your desk at 6 p.m. — it reads as poor planning, not dedication |
| Expect flexible start and finish times if the work gets done | Email after hours expecting a same-day reply |
| Leave when the office does on a Friday — it's the unwritten script | Skip your smoko or tea breaks; they're a genuine workplace norm |
| Ask directly about time-off-in-lieu policy before you sign | Mistake "flexible hours" for "always available" |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find out which Kollektivvertrag (collective agreement) covers your role — it sets your real hours | Assume overtime pay happens automatically without checking your contract |
| Arrive early; 7:30–8 a.m. starts are common and mornings are taken seriously | Expect business as usual during the July–August "Sommerloch" |
| Use professional titles (Mag., Dr., Ing.) in correspondence | Skip Kaffee und Kuchen with colleagues — declining is a minor social misstep |
| Plan around a generous statutory minimum of 25 paid leave days | Assume small talk equals friendliness; it's reserved for people you actually know |
| Expect the office to empty by mid-afternoon on Fridays | Work through lunch — stepping away properly is the norm, not the exception |

New Zealand's employment law treats overtime roughly the way it treats extroversion: technically permitted, rarely encouraged, and nobody is issuing a certificate for it. The Holidays Act guarantees four weeks of annual leave, and Stats NZ reports average weekly earnings including overtime climbing to NZ$1,712 in the December 2025 quarter (Stats NZ, Labour Market Statistics) — but "including overtime" is doing a great deal of quiet work in that phrase. Overtime pay in New Zealand is a matter of individual employment agreement, not statutory right, so whether extra hours show up on a payslip depends entirely on what got signed on day one.

What New Zealand does have is a reputation, and by several measures it has earned it. The OECD Better Life Index puts full-time workers at 14.9 hours a day of personal care and leisure, just under the OECD average of 15 (OECD Better Life Index), and the country has topped a widely cited global work-life balance ranking for a third consecutive year with a score of 86.87 out of 100. The trophy case, however, is showing cracks: Gallup's most recent wellbeing tracking finds under-25s in New Zealand among the steepest wellbeing decliners globally since 2010 (Gallup World Poll), a statistic that sits awkwardly next to the medal.

The mechanism behind the numbers is cultural rather than legal — flexible hours, early Friday finishes, and a general "she'll be right" assumption that clocking off on time is not a character flaw. It works because New Zealand's small labour market runs on trust rather than supervision. The failure mode is that "flexible" quietly becomes "undocumented," which is how a country with a gold-medal work-life balance ranking still produces unpaid-overtime and wage disputes that never make it into the index.

Austria took the opposite approach: legislate it. The Arbeitszeitgesetz sets a standard working week at 40 hours, most collective agreements bring that down to around 38.5, and overtime is required to carry a 50 percent pay premium or 1.5 hours of compensatory time off, with a hard cap of 60 hours a week including overtime (Statistics Austria). Eurostat's 2025 figures put Austria's average actual weekly working hours at 34.0 — among the shortest in the EU (Eurostat, Statistics Explained). On paper, it is the more tightly regulated system of the two.

On paper. Statistics Austria's own figures show 42.3 million hours of overtime went unpaid in 2024, even as the total volume of overtime logged nationally has fallen by more than half since 2005 — from 354 million hours to roughly 170 million. The law is unambiguous; the compliance is optional. Austria's individualism score on the Hofstede framework sits at 77, just two points behind New Zealand's 79 (Hofstede Insights), a reminder that both cultures ultimately expect the employee, not the collective, to enforce their own boundaries — statute or no statute. Austria's happiness ranking has told a similar story of quiet erosion, slipping from eighth to nineteenth in recent global rankings even as its labour protections stayed exactly the same on paper (Gallup / World Happiness Report).

Put the two head to head and the irony sharpens. New Zealand, which imposes no statutory overtime-pay requirement at all, edges out Austria on the OECD Better Life Index's leisure-time measure — 14.9 hours a day of personal care and leisure versus Austria's 14.5 (OECD Better Life Index). The country with fewer rules is, by that one metric, marginally better rested than the country with a detailed federal Working Time Act.

But marginally better rested is not the same as actually fine. Both countries' overtime economies run substantially on trust that turns out to be misplaced: Austria's legally mandated premium didn't stop 42.3 million hours from going unpaid in a single year, and New Zealand's contractual approach doesn't stop overtime disputes from surfacing regularly enough that Stats NZ still tracks earnings "including overtime" as one blended figure rather than separating what's owed from what's actually paid. Neither country solved overtime. One wrote it into law and got ignored; the other left it to goodwill and got roughly the same result, with better weather.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*A British graphic designer who relocated to Tyrol was struck by how differently overtime was viewed there: staying late at the office read as poor time management rather than dedication, colleagues left when the workday ended, and even senior management treated a full stretch of parental leave as entirely unremarkable, as she described to The Local Austria.*

*A German-to-English translator who has lived in Vienna for two decades advised newcomers not to fight the rhythm of the Austrian year — the "Sommerloch," the quiet stretch across July and August when much of the country effectively downshifts, is better embraced than resisted, he told The Local Austria.*

*One contributor comparing European tech hubs on Quora cautioned that Austria's relaxed reputation doesn't hold everywhere: automotive firms and startups in particular keep overtime alive and well, even where smaller, flatter companies still deliver on the work-life balance promise.*

*A British expat in Tyrol found the unwritten etiquette harder to read than she'd expected — professional titles mattered more than anticipated, colleagues asked pointed questions about her qualifications, and whether Friday drinks with coworkers were even a thing at all turned out to depend entirely on the industry, she told The Local Austria.*

### Conclusion

For anyone relocating for either job, the practical advice is nearly identical despite the two countries' opposite legal philosophies: read the fine print before you sign, not after you're three weekends deep into hours nobody is tracking. In New Zealand, ask directly whether overtime is compensated in the employment agreement, because "flexible" is carrying enormous unstated weight in most job listings. In Austria, ask specifically which collective agreement covers your role and what its real weekly hours are, since the 40-hour legal default is largely theoretical once Kollektivverträge get involved.

Both countries, for all their differences in method, converge on the same trick: an excellent headline statistic sitting on top of a much messier reality. The honest version of the advice is the same wherever you land — negotiate the hours before you fall in love with the scenery, because the scenery was never going to file the timesheet for you.
