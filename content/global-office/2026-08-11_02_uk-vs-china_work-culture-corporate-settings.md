# The Pub at 5pm vs. The Office at 9pm: Corporate Life in the UK and China

**🇬🇧 UK · 🇨🇳 China**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The British office empties by 5:30, half of it heading to the nearest pub on the theory that Friday starts on Wednesday. The Chinese office, particularly in tech, may still be full at 9pm, not because anyone loves the work, but because being first to leave carries a social cost few are willing to pay. Both offices consider themselves reasonably normal. Neither is wrong, exactly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to read understatement — "not ideal" can mean "a disaster" | Take polite phrasing at face value; ask a trusted colleague to translate the subtext |
| Join the Friday pub trip at least occasionally | Skip every social event and expect to be read as merely busy rather than aloof |
| Match the dry, self-deprecating humour rather than overselling your achievements | Lead with confident self-promotion in the American style — it can land as arrogance |
| Use your full holiday allowance — around 28 days statutory minimum including bank holidays | Treat unused leave as a badge of dedication; nobody is impressed |
| Speak up in meetings regardless of seniority | Assume rank determines whose opinion counts most in the room |

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest time in guanxi — relationships built outside formal meetings | Expect decisions to move purely on merit or the written proposal alone |
| Defer visibly to seniority in group settings | Publicly contradict a senior colleague or manager, even if you're right |
| Expect the workday to run later than contracted hours, especially in tech | Be the first to leave without a very good, visible reason |
| Learn who has real influence versus who has the title | Assume the org chart reflects where decisions actually get made |
| Treat group dinners and toasts as extensions of the working relationship | Decline every informal gathering — it reads as disengagement, not boundaries |

### UK: Understatement as Operating System

British corporate culture runs on indirection dressed up as politeness. According to the Office for National Statistics, full-time employees worked an average of 36.7 hours a week in the final quarter of 2025, the highest quarterly figure since 2019, and roughly 60 percent of the workforce sits in the 31-to-45-hour band — solidly conventional by European standards. What distinguishes the culture isn't the hours; it's the communication style layered on top of them. Feedback arrives softened, criticism gets phrased as a mild observation, and Hofstede Insights scores the UK at 89 on individualism, among the highest in the world, alongside a low power-distance score of 35 — meaning juniors are structurally expected to speak up, and hierarchy is treated as something to work around rather than defer to.

The social architecture matters as much as the org chart. Fridays regularly end with the office decamping to a pub, an informal ritual that does real relationship-building work precisely because it isn't mandatory. Holiday entitlement — a statutory minimum of 28 days including bank holidays — is treated as a right to be used, not a signal of insufficient commitment if claimed in full.

### China: Hierarchy, Relationships, and the Weight of Hours

Chinese corporate culture, particularly in the technology sector, has spent the past several years publicly negotiating with its own overtime norms. The informal "996" system — 9am to 9pm, six days a week — became notorious enough that Beijing courts and state media pushed back on it as illegal starting around 2021, yet reporting into 2025 still put average tech-sector weekly hours at 56.8, against a legal cap of 44, with 72 percent of surveyed tech workers reporting they'd worked a 996-adjacent schedule within the past year. Hofstede Insights puts China at 80 on power distance and just 20 on individualism — a near-mirror inversion of the UK's scores — which tracks with how decisions actually move: through guanxi, the web of personal relationships and reciprocal obligation that often outweighs the formal org chart, and through visible deference to seniority in group settings.

For a foreign hire, the practical consequence is that competence and a strong proposal are necessary but insufficient. Trust gets built through repeated informal contact — dinners, toasts, the unglamorous accumulation of relationship capital — and being seen to leave before your manager, even with your work finished, can register as a signal you don't intend to send.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, the UK optimises for individual voice and treats hierarchy as a soft suggestion; China optimises for group cohesion and treats hierarchy as load-bearing infrastructure. The counterintuitive part is that the UK's famous politeness can be harder to navigate than China's more overt deference — British indirection hides disagreement inside soft language, while Chinese hierarchy at least tells you plainly, through visible seniority cues, where the power sits.

Hours worked tell a similarly inverted story. The UK's shorter, more clearly bounded week coexists with genuine ambiguity about what's actually being communicated in a meeting; China's longer, more exhausting week coexists with far less ambiguity about who's in charge and what's expected of you physically, even as what's expected of you socially — the guanxi-building, the deference rituals — adds an entirely separate, unwritten workload.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American who'd worked in a London office for several years said the hardest skill to learn wasn't any specific rule but recalibrating for understatement: a colleague's "slight concern" about a project turned out to mean the client was ready to walk, and nobody had said so directly in the meeting.*

> *teamblind.com — A foreign engineer who spent time at the US office of a major Chinese tech company described the peer pressure around hours as more powerful than any written policy — leaving on time was technically allowed and socially costly at once, and the discomfort of being the first to go outlasted any formal work-life-balance messaging from HR.*

> *Reddit — A UK-based poster reflecting on office culture said the thing that took longest to adjust to wasn't the workload but realising Friday afternoon pub trips were where real decisions and alliances actually got discussed, not the Monday meeting.*

> *Reddit — Someone recounting a stint at a Chinese firm described being caught off guard by how much unofficial influence sat with people who had unremarkable titles — the org chart was, in their words, more of a suggestion than a map, and figuring out who actually held sway took months of paying attention to who got deferred to in the room.*

### Conclusion

The practical question for anyone weighing these two isn't which culture works harder — it's which kind of ambiguity you'd rather manage. The UK will hand you clearly bounded hours and then make you decode what people actually mean; China will hand you a much heavier time commitment and a clearer, if more demanding, map of where deference is owed. Neither rewards someone who shows up expecting the other country's rules to apply. If a friend asked me over a drink which is the bigger adjustment, I'd say the UK is the harder culture to read and China is the harder culture to keep up with — plan your first three months accordingly.
