# Iceland Hands Out Six Months Each, Norway Hands Out a Year and a Kindergarten

🇮🇸 Iceland · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two Nordic countries, two of the most generous parental leave systems on earth, and two entirely different answers to the question of what happens on the morning after. In Norway, the answer is a kindergarten place at a state-capped price of NOK 1,200 a month. In Iceland, according to a published study of the system, children have no legal right to a childcare place when paid leave ends. Anyone planning a family abroad should read the end of the leave before the beginning.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply through the Maternity/Paternity Leave Fund and plan around 80 percent pay | Assume full salary; the monthly ceiling is ISK 600,000 before tax |
| Use your own six months and decide deliberately about transferring up to six weeks | Forget that each period must be at least two weeks long |
| Propose a leave pattern to your employer; if they disagree, they must counter within a week | Take silence as agreement or refusal |
| Put your child on a preschool or day-parent list long before you need it | Assume a place will exist the week your leave ends |
| Plan around the mother's compulsory first two weeks after birth | Forget that leave may start up to a month before the due date |
| Remember that parental rights expire when the child turns 24 months | Count on stretching leave beyond that |

### Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check that you have work income in six of the last ten months before the birth | Assume eligibility just because you are employed on paper |
| Choose between 49 weeks at 100 percent and about 61 weeks at 80 percent with a calculator | Pick the longer option and forget the monthly shortfall |
| Ask your employer in writing whether they top up income above the state ceiling | Assume they will; none is required to |
| Use the father's quota; it is not transferable and is lost if unused | Treat it as optional for the partner's career health |
| Use all leave before the child turns three | Let the final weeks lapse |
| Apply for a kindergarten place through your municipality and check its deadline | Rely on a 2014 blog post for the dates |

## Iceland: Six Months Each, Then a Quiet Panic

Iceland's rules are admirably symmetrical. According to the government portal island.is, each parent has an independent right to six months of leave, and each may transfer up to six weeks to the other, giving a couple twelve months in all. Leave can be taken in a single block or in separate periods of at least two weeks, and the right lapses when the child reaches 24 months. Work in Iceland, the national relocation portal, states the payment: 80 percent of average income up to a ceiling of ISK 600,000 a month before tax. Employment contracts and acquired rights remain intact throughout, and a parent may separately take up to 13 consecutive weeks of unpaid leave to care for a child up to the age of eight, which is generous in principle and unpaid in practice. If an employer cannot agree to the pattern a parent proposes, the law lets the employee take the leave in one block from a chosen date, a rare example of a workplace dispute with a pre-written ending.

The ceiling is where the story becomes less Nordic-utopian. Reporting in the Nordic Labour Journal in September 2022 noted that after a post-2008 cut to the ceiling, fathers with higher salaries took less leave, and that higher earners generally receive well under 80 percent of their pay. The same article quotes a father who stayed home with his son because no kindergarten place was available, and notes that interviewees regard the gap between the end of leave and public childcare as the bigger problem. A paper in the Journal of Family Research reaches a similar conclusion in academic prose: Icelandic children have no legal right to early education once paid leave ends, so many go into family day care while a public place is awaited.

Help is arriving, slowly. The City of Reykjavik announced in February 2026 a scheme of 36 free preschool hours a week, with 38 hours costing 7,000 krona a month plus food, which the city says can save a family up to 168,000 krona a year. The announcement did not state the ages covered, and a free hour is of limited use to a family without a place. The wider equality picture is similarly mixed. A Monash University analysis published in December 2025 notes that Iceland has topped the World Economic Forum gender gap index for fifteen consecutive years, yet Statistics Iceland's unadjusted gender pay gap rose to 10.4 percent in 2024 from 9.3 percent in 2023. The same analysis reports that 68 percent of Icelandic mothers worked full-time in 2023, against 96 percent of fathers, and that about 97 percent of children attend preschool, which has not stopped mothers reporting guilt over late pickups. Fathers, the author notes, report no equivalent. The leave is shared; the conscience, it appears, is not.

## Norway: A Longer Year, a Cheaper Kindergarten, an Honest Ceiling

Norway offers more weeks and more arithmetic. For births from 1 July 2024, parents share 49 weeks at 100 percent of earnings or 61 weeks at 80 percent, according to the guidance summarised by Working With Norwegians. Each parent has a 15-week quota, or 19 weeks at the lower rate, which cannot be transferred. The remaining 16 weeks are shared, and the whole leave must be used before the child turns three. Income is covered up to six times the National Insurance basic amount, NOK 819,294 from May 2026, and anything above that is the employer's choice to top up. The Nordic Council of Ministers notes that collective agreements typically have employers pay the difference, which is a reason to read your agreement and not your optimism.

The quota behaves as designed. Statistics Norway reports that 67.5 percent of fathers took the full quota or more in 2024, and fewer than 20 percent used none, a group that includes fathers not entitled to benefit. The Nordic Council's report adds that about nine in ten eligible fathers use all or part of it. Nor is the structure rigid in the other direction. A father may take two weeks of his quota shortly before the birth, the mother takes the first six weeks after it, and the 80 percent option buys roughly three extra months at home before kindergarten begins, according to Working With Norwegians. A mother who does not qualify for the benefit receives a tax-free lump sum of NOK 92,648 per child. It is a small consolation prize for having failed the six-of-ten-months test. Then comes childcare. Statistics Norway says the national maximum for a full-time kindergarten place fell from NOK 2,000 to NOK 1,200 a month in 2026, the average household paid NOK 997 in January, and kindergarten was free in 34 municipalities. The average annual cost, including meals, was 31.3 percent lower than a year earlier. The Local notes that sibling discounts and income-based caps apply on top, with the second child at least 30 percent cheaper and the third free, and that households earning under NOK 692,465 a year can apply for up to 20 free hours a week for children aged two to five. Applying is, naturally, a form.

None of this has made Norwegians prolific. Statistics Norway's fertility rate rose to 1.48 in 2025 from 1.44, the third consecutive increase, but 58 percent of women turning 30 that year had no children, against 43 percent a decade earlier. Generous leave and cheap kindergarten, it turns out, do not fully compete with housing, uncertainty and the opportunity cost of timing. An NHH analysis in 2023 adds the familiar caveat that the pay gap widens markedly after women have children, and that sharing parental duties more evenly can narrow it.

## The Reckoning

On the numbers, Iceland is the more equal on paper and Norway the more continuous in practice. Iceland reserves six months for each parent and then leaves a gap. Norway reserves fifteen weeks for each, lets the couple share the rest, and provides a subsidised place for a child over one. The Icelandic scheme produces a fairer first year and a more improvised second. The Norwegian scheme produces a slightly less symmetrical first year and a considerably calmer second. Hofstede's framework, as summarised by James Madison University's international student office, classes Norway as low in masculinity, meaning a culture that rates quality of life and caring above competition, which is a tidy academic way of saying the leave policy is not an accident.

For a foreign worker the differences are practical. In Iceland, the main risk is financial and logistical: the ceiling, and the wait. In Norway, the main risk is eligibility and employer generosity: six of ten months of income, and whether your employer tops up the part above the ceiling. Neither country is hostile to working parents. Both simply bill them at different stages.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reykjavik Grapevine, paraphrased experience: A journalist-parent spent two months calling nineteen daycare centres to find a place for her five-month-old before her return to work, and was told only that she could join waiting lists. Parents, she said, were registering children before they were born. This was 2014, though later reporting suggests the pressure persists.*

> *Nordic Labour Journal, paraphrased experience: An Icelandic father interviewed in 2022 stayed home with his small son after leave ended because no kindergarten place was available, and said the transition from leave to childcare was a greater difficulty than the leave itself.*

> *Slate, paraphrased experience: An Oslo marketing professional on leave in 2014 described taking about eleven months at reduced pay with her first child, her partner taking ten weeks when she returned, and a second child who could not secure a daycare place until almost two. She found employers supportive, but called the September birth-date cutoff for daycare places a recurring frustration.*

> *ScienceNorway, paraphrased experience: Eight fathers who had moved to Norway from Central and Southern Europe told researchers they embraced the father's quota, saw it as settled law they did not have to negotiate with employers, and valued learning childcare directly, though one said he would not have taken leave without the earmark.*

## Conclusion

If you want the most symmetrical first six months, choose Iceland, and build a childcare plan before you conceive one. If you want the smoothest handover from leave to nursery, choose Norway, and read your employment agreement for the top-up above the ceiling. In both countries, take the leave: the NHH analysis suggests that sharing parental duties more evenly narrows the pay gap that otherwise opens after the first child.

Neither country makes children free. They merely make the first year defensible and the second year somebody else's administrative problem, which is, by international standards, a triumph.
