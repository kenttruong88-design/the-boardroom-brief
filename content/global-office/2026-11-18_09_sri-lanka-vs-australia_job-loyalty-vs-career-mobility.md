# Sri Lanka Rewards Staying Put; Australia Rewards Leaving Politely

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Australia, roughly one working adult in ten changes jobs in a given year, and nobody sends flowers or a concerned email to the family. In Sri Lanka, a manager who changes employers twice in five years is treated as a cautionary tale, while a manager who leaves the country entirely is treated as a statistic, and a record-setting one at that. Two economies, one question: is loyalty a virtue or merely a policy that employers would like you to adopt?

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan to stay two to three years in a role before moving, and have a clean story for each exit | Present a CV with several short stints and expect it to pass unremarked |
| Deliver criticism and disagreement privately, one to one | Correct a colleague or superior in front of the room |
| Treat your tenure as a credential, especially in senior or professional roles | Assume a competing offer will be read as ambition rather than disloyalty |
| Ask what progression looks like before joining, since pay rises often come with a move rather than a review | Count on annual reviews alone to keep your salary in line with the market |
| Check whether partner or competitor firms pay more before accepting a counter-offer | Accept a counter-offer without asking what has actually changed |
| Keep relationships warm with former managers, since the market is small | Burn a bridge on the way out, however satisfying it looks in draft |

### In Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a move after a few years as a normal, unremarkable step | Stay in a stalled role out of a sense of obligation the employer does not share |
| Answer "how are you" with a short, cheerful phrase | Deliver a full medical and emotional bulletin to a manager in passing |
| Speak plainly and directly, even to senior colleagues | Expect hierarchy to be performed through titles and deference |
| Give your resignation professionally and work out your notice in good faith | Disappear mid-notice; your next referee may be the same person |
| Negotiate pay on the basis of market rates and your own results | Wait quietly to be noticed for loyalty |
| Learn local shorthand and humour, and accept being teased as a sign of acceptance | Mistake informality for a lack of seriousness |

## Sri Lanka

Sri Lankan working life has a stability premium that predates the recent economic crisis and was sharpened by it. The Sunday Observer's career advice columns, a decent barometer of received wisdom, put the expected tenure for senior executives at five to ten years and for doctors and lawyers at five to seven. Changing jobs more than once every two to three years, the paper notes, counts as job hopping, and hiring managers treat hoppers as a red flag because the training spend may never be recouped. The writer, who reports 33 years at two organisations, concedes that conditions have changed, which is a polite way of saying the advice is losing an argument with reality.

Reality, in this case, is the exchange rate. According to the Sri Lanka Bureau of Foreign Employment, a record 312,836 people left for work abroad in 2023, beating the previous record of 310,953 set in 2022, and the government has set a target of 340,000 for 2025. The Central Bank has described the outflow as a downside risk to growth, with officials warning of a shortage of skilled middle-level staff. A senior banker, quoted in the same coverage, said the best of his staff had already gone. Earlier, the Daily Mirror reported 161,394 new passports issued by late March 2022, equal to 42 per cent of the previous year's total, with the chief executive of a major conglomerate calling retention "a huge problem".

So the loyalty ethic coexists with an exit rate that suggests it is applied selectively. According to the Sunday Times, nearly 4,000 IT professionals left in 2020 alone, and a planned 15 per cent cut to health sector salaries in 2023 did little for the case against leaving. The same coverage lists the reasons without much ceremony: pay that fails to cover the cost of living, sharply higher income tax for top earners, heavier workloads where colleagues have already left, and better-paid jobs overseas as documented by the IOM and the Asian Development Bank. A ministry official admitted there was no comprehensive data on how many skilled workers had gone, which makes the loyalty debate somewhat academic. The practical reading for a newcomer: Sri Lankan employers still prefer long tenure, but they are in no position to enforce it, and the most mobile workers are the ones leaving the country rather than the company. Hofstede Insights data for Sri Lanka could not be retrieved for this piece, which seems an appropriate level of transparency for a country where the numbers are still being collected.

## Australia

Australia treats job changes as routine administration. The Australian Bureau of Statistics reports that 9.5 per cent of employed people, about 1.3 million, changed jobs in the year to February 2023, the highest rate in a decade. Of 13.8 million employed people, 56 per cent had been in their current job for under five years and 21 per cent for under one year, while only 10 per cent had passed the 20-year mark. Among the 2.3 million who left or lost a job, the most common reason, at 32.3 per cent, was wanting a better job or simply a change. Retrenchment accounted for 7.9 per cent, and the retrenchment rate of 1.4 per cent was the lowest on record.

None of this is hostile to loyalty; it simply refuses to build an economy on it. According to Hofstede Insights figures as compiled by one management reference, Australia scores 90 on individualism and 36 on power distance, meaning the workplace expects you to manage your own career and to address your manager by first name while doing so. Poor work arrangements, pay or hours drove another 11.9 per cent of departures, a reminder that Australians leave for specific, itemisable grievances and do not take it personally. Expats report the flip side: one found the blunt, casual speech of colleagues faintly unprofessional at first, and another noted an undercurrent of status-seeking beneath the beach-and-barbecue branding.

Loyalty has not vanished; it has simply been made optional. One in ten Australian workers has stayed 20 years or more, and nobody asks them to apologise for it. The difference is that staying is judged on results rather than on the calendar, and a long-serving employee is expected to keep earning the title. Australian employers, for their part, have little to say about the person who leaves for a better offer, because the ABS suggests it happens to roughly everyone sooner or later. For a newcomer, the lesson is that mobility is both permitted and expected. A move every few years is the market working as designed, and an employer who never offers a rise is not a loyalty test but a recruiting opportunity for someone else.

## The Reckoning

Sri Lanka and Australia disagree less about loyalty than about who pays for it. In Sri Lanka, tenure is a signal that substitutes for the thing employers cannot always offer, which is predictable progression and pay that keeps pace with inflation. The loyal employee is rewarded with respect and a reference; the mobile one is rewarded with a better salary and a quizzical look. In Australia, the market itself provides the signal, with one in five workers under a year in their current job, so staying put is a choice rather than a credential.

The difference matters most in the first two years abroad. A Sri Lankan professional arriving in Australia will find a CV with three employers in six years unremarkable, and may find the habit of quiet endurance read as a lack of ambition. An Australian arriving in Sri Lanka should expect questions about why they left, and should have an answer that sounds like growth rather than escape. There is also the matter of how departure is delivered. Sri Lankan norms favour indirectness and private conversation, so a resignation that arrives as a surprise in a meeting will be remembered long after the handover document is forgotten. Australian norms favour a plain statement and a quick, friendly exit. Neither is wrong; they are simply different dialects of the same polite goodbye. In both places, the safest course is to stay long enough to deliver something and leave on terms that survive a reference check.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on loyalty alone, Australia is the easier place to change jobs and Sri Lanka the harder place to explain why you did. Weigh your own tolerance for each: a market that treats your tenure as a credential, or one that treats your résumé as a living document. Either way, ask early what progression and pay review look like in writing, since both cultures are happy to leave this vague until you leave.

Loyalty, in the end, is a lovely quality in an employee, provided the employer is also willing to show it in the payroll.
