**In Seoul, Your Job Title Changes How You're Allowed to Speak. In London, It Just Changes Your Parking Space.**

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇬🇧 UK**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

South Korean employees clocked 1,833 hours in 2025 — down from 1,865 the year before, a decline Korean officials are treating as a triumph of work-life balance — while their British counterparts worked 1,533, a gap of roughly seven and a half standard work weeks, according to OECD figures. Both countries are simultaneously hosting a quiet civil war between a generation that built its identity on loyalty, seniority, and showing up, and a generation that built its identity on boundaries, feedback, and leaving on time. The war looks different in each country only because the starting hierarchy was so different to begin with.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the title system fast — colleagues are addressed by rank ("Team Leader Kim"), not first names | Use casual speech (banmal) with anyone senior to you unless explicitly invited to |
| Attend your first team dinner (hoesik), even if you leave before it gets long | Skip the first hoesik without a very good, pre-cleared excuse |
| Receive documents, business cards, and drinks with both hands from anyone senior | Pour your own drink at a work dinner — let seniors pour, and reciprocate for them |
| Wait to be told before openly disagreeing with a manager in a group setting | Challenge a decision in front of the team — raise concerns privately instead |
| Check whether your company has adopted the 4-day or flexible pilot schemes before assuming standard hours | Assume the legal 52-hour workweek cap means nobody works past 6pm in practice |
| Note who sits where and enters the elevator first — seating and order reflect rank | Sit in a senior colleague's usual seat, even in an empty meeting room |

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open meetings with a bit of small talk before getting to business | Launch straight into the agenda — it reads as brusque |
| Learn to hear disagreement inside polite phrasing ("interesting, though have you considered…") | Take "that's quite brave" or "not bad" at face value — it's rarely a compliment |
| Get hybrid-schedule expectations confirmed in writing in your first week | Assume a "flexible" policy means no informal face-time expectations exist |
| Use humor and light self-deprecation — it's a genuine social currency here | Be too direct or blunt in group settings; it can read as aggressive |
| Ask directly for feedback if you're early-career — it's not offered as often as you'd expect | Wait passively for a formal review cycle to find out how you're doing |
| Show up for the optional pub trip occasionally, even briefly | Assume flat-sounding job titles mean flat actual authority |

### South Korea: Hierarchy on a Slow Leak

South Korea's Hofstede power distance score sits at 60 against an individualism score of just 18 — a profile that describes, reasonably accurately, a workplace where your rank determines who speaks first, who eats first, and which verb endings you're permitted to use. The MZ generation (millennials and Gen Z, treated in Korean discourse as one bloc) has spent the last several years pushing back against exactly that structure. According to the Korean Chamber of Commerce and Industry's 2020 survey of 13,000 office workers, 63.9% reported experiencing a generational gap at work directly. More recent academic work is blunter about the consequences: a 2024 study of over 9,500 Korean employees found hierarchical culture measurably eroded organizational commitment among MZ workers, and a 2025 study of more than 9,000 employees linked that same hierarchical culture to higher turnover intention specifically among the young.

The most visible casualty has been hoesik, the after-work dinner-and-drinks ritual once treated as mandatory loyalty theater. A Korea Institute of Public Administration survey found that both MZ workers and their older colleagues now prefer daytime team lunches over evening drinking sessions — a rare point of generational consensus, arrived at for different reasons (younger staff want their evenings back; older staff, it turns out, would also quite like to go home). Companies are responding unevenly: Cafe24 has rolled out a full four-day week, and Severance Hospital and Gyeonggi Province launched four-day pilots in 2025, even as the country as a whole still logs 97 more hours per year than the OECD average. The kkondae — the officious senior colleague who treats deference as owed rather than earned — has become enough of a cultural fixture to earn its own Wikipedia entry and its own academic literature on workplace bullying. Change is real. It is also, measurably, slower than the complaints about it.

### UK: Flat Structure, Fault Lines Anyway

The UK's Hofstede profile is close to the inverse of Korea's: a power distance score of 35 and an individualism score of 89, describing a culture that formally dislikes hierarchy and prizes the individual over the group. On paper, this should make the UK's generational friction milder than Korea's. It hasn't. According to HR Magazine, 45% of UK Gen Z workers report experiencing a generational divide at work, compared with just 23% of workers aged 50 and over — a perception gap nearly double in size, even inside a supposedly flat structure. A separate industry survey found 45% of hiring managers name Gen Z the hardest generation to manage, citing a feedback-frequency gap, differing attitudes to authority, and mismatched expectations around how fast promotion should happen.

The flashpoint is less about titles than about presence. Deloitte UK found two-thirds of British Gen Z and millennial workers favor remote or hybrid arrangements, and over three-quarters of Gen Z said they'd consider quitting if forced back to the office full-time — while, according to People Management, only one in ten Gen Z workers actually want to be in the office five days a week. And yet other workplace surveys report Gen Z employees under 24 are physically in the office more days per week on average than the managers nominally pushing return-to-office mandates, a statistic that flatters no generation's self-image equally. The UK's generational conflict isn't fought over honorifics; it's fought over the calendar.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: the country with the steeper formal hierarchy (Korea) is having a more coherent generational negotiation — hoesik declining, work hours falling, four-day pilots launching, everyone at least agreeing on what's being negotiated. The country with the flatter formal hierarchy (UK) is having a messier one, because when there's no explicit rank to blame, the friction gets displaced onto vaguer terrain — feedback style, meeting tone, who's "committed" versus who has "boundaries" — where nobody can quite agree on the rules to begin with.

The practical upshot for a relocating professional: in Korea, learn the explicit rules and you will mostly know where you stand, even as those rules visibly erode around you. In the UK, there are fewer explicit rules to learn and more unwritten ones to infer, which is a harder skill to pack in a suitcase.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One expat working under Korea's ranking system described raising concerns with a manager and being shut down outright, told there was "no right to discussion," just to execute instructions even when they seemed clearly mistaken.*

> *Quora — Another contributor noted that the loud, authoritarian boss stereotype familiar from K-dramas is far less common at tech startups with younger leadership, where the atmosphere runs noticeably more relaxed than at traditional chaebol-style employers.*

> *Expat.com — A guide to Seoul corporate culture flagged the details newcomers miss entirely: who enters the elevator first, where you're permitted to sit in a meeting room, and that hoesik dinners, while technically declinable, are "very difficult to refuse" if you want to be treated as part of the team.*

> *Quora — On British office etiquette, one answer warned that UK colleagues often default to relentless surface pleasantness toward management, which can read to outsiders as fake friendliness rather than the genuine hedging and indirect disagreement it usually is.*

> *Quora — Responding to a question on bridging the UK's generational divide, one contributor argued the core friction isn't values so much as tone — older colleagues read Gen Z's directness as entitlement, while younger staff read older colleagues' vagueness as evasion, and both sides mostly just want to be managed clearly.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is easier, exactly — they're difficult in different grammatical registers. Korea will hand you an explicit rulebook (titles, seating, drinking order) that is currently being rewritten in real time by the people it governs; the discomfort is legible, even when it's uncomfortable. The UK will hand you no rulebook at all, just a set of politely-worded signals you're expected to decode correctly on the first attempt, with generational battle lines drawn over Slack tone and office days rather than anything as clarifying as rank. If a friend asked me which to pick: go to Korea if you'd rather fight a system you can see, and go to the UK if you'd rather fight one that's pretending not to be there.
