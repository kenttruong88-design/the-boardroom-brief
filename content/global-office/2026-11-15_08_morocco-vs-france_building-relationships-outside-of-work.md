# Morocco Will Adopt You in Thirty Seconds; France Would Like to See Some Paperwork First

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Expat.com guide to Casablanca warns that you can become someone's brother, sister or friend after knowing them for about thirty seconds, and adds, with admirable concern, "so be careful." The Connexion, a newspaper serving English speakers in France, makes a roughly opposite point in 2026: French friendships "are not formed quickly, but they are taken seriously." One country issues friendship on arrival and sorts out the consequences afterward. The other conducts a lengthy review and then, if you pass, refuses to let you leave.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept all three glasses of mint tea | Refuse tea; it is described as a serious breach of etiquette |
| Remove your shoes when invited into a home | Walk in as though it were a hotel lobby |
| Bring pastries, flowers or quality gifts from your home country | Bring alcohol unless you know your host drinks |
| Match your social life to your status and circle, as the Casablanca guides advise | Assume a club or school is open to anyone; fees and co-optation apply |
| Respect Ramadan by not eating, drinking or smoking in public by day | Schedule a long lunch with local colleagues that month |
| Be warm and patient before raising business or favours | Treat a fast friendship as an unlimited loan facility |

### France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan invitations ahead; French invitations are intentional | Text at 6pm suggesting dinner at 7pm |
| Join structured groups such as associations, clubs or the village fête committee | Wait for friendship to arrive unassisted |
| Use formal forms of address until familiarity develops | Use tu on the first evening to signal openness |
| Keep conversation on ideas and practical matters at first | Open with your divorce, your salary and your childhood |
| Practise French everywhere, even badly | Retreat into English whenever it gets difficult |
| Pick one French friend and invest in that person | Treat polite warmth as a binding offer of friendship |

## Morocco

There is no statistical agency measuring how quickly Moroccans befriend strangers, which is a pity, because the evidence is anecdotal and consistent. Expat.com's Casablanca guide describes a very large, varied city where adapting "can look tricky at first," yet where friendships form quickly. Cyborlink's business etiquette profile says Moroccans invest heavily in rapport, expect several meetings over tea before business comes up, and that the mint tea comes in three rounds.

The catch is the same guide's description of Casablanca as "a city of extremes." Social circles tend to follow income and status. International schools are a social marker, with fees of roughly 20,000 to 20,500 dirhams a year for schooling alone, and a sports club such as the CAC requires co-opted registration and an entrance fee. A second Expat.com guide advises joining the social life that fits your status, because stepping outside it may be frowned upon, and cautions against people who regard you mainly as a source of money. Hospitality is real. So, regrettably, is the price list. The Casablanca guide's own advice, to register with your consular list and national associations on arrival, reads less like a social tip than a pragmatic admission that the existing networks are where the doors are.

The home is where Moroccan friendship is actually conducted. Cyborlink advises removing shoes at the door, accepting all food offered, and using the right hand if eating from a shared dish. Gifts of pastries or flowers are appropriate, though alcohol is not unless you know the host drinks. The same profile notes that Moroccans may arrive late while foreign visitors should not, a double standard that is best understood as a form of hospitality in which you are the one holding the clock. Ramadan reshapes the calendar entirely: no eating, drinking or smoking in public during daylight hours, and shortened business hours, which makes it a good month to discover the evening.

## France

The Connexion's columnist is precise about the mechanics. Invitations "tend to be intentional, rather than on the spur of the moment." Personal questions about relationships, jobs or struggles can seem intrusive, so early conversation stays general, intellectual or practical. The French also draw a clearer line between courtesy and friendship, so a warm exchange with a shopkeeper may be exactly that. Many French people keep friends from school or university for decades, and expats, being temporary by nature, are not always considered a sound investment. Even fluent speakers, the columnist adds, often struggle with humour, irony and emotional nuance, which is the linguistic equivalent of passing every exam except the one that matters.

The Local France offers the rural remedy: introduce yourself to neighbours with a treat, host an apéro, walk the village and greet everyone, volunteer for the Comité des Fêtes, and watch the local rugby match for the bar afterward. Another reader learned French and found neighbours delighted when she used new words correctly, while another reported that buying a drink at the local bar worked every time. Readers quoted there report that it works; one said locals invite you in for wine at the kitchen table so readily she sometimes sneaks past a neighbour's house.

## The Reckoning

On speed, Morocco wins without a contest. A Moroccan will have you in a living room, in front of a tray of tea, before a French acquaintance has finished deciding whether to use vous. But speed and depth are different currencies. Morocco's early warmth is real, yet it sits inside a class structure that decides which club, school and circle will admit you. France's early coolness is also real, yet once the door opens, the Connexion says the friendship is loyal and enduring.

Hofstede Insights scores France at 68 on power distance and 74 on individualism, which fits the picture of a society that is hierarchical in manner yet protective of private life. Morocco's 70 on power distance, from the Cyborlink profile, is a near match, but a Moroccan living room does not share France's reluctance to merge public and private. The French are not cold. They are filing.

For the expat who needs company by March, Morocco is kinder. For the one who wants a friend to ask about a hospital in ten years, France is the better bet, provided you can tolerate a long audition and a language test conducted continuously. The expat forums confirm the pattern in their own anxious way. Residents in France with eight, ten and twenty years behind them describe everything from a guarded village to a welcoming Basque Country, which suggests that region and city size matter nearly as much as nationality. In Casablanca, by contrast, the threads read as a long queue of people, returnees included, politely asking whether anyone would like to meet for coffee. Neither country is unfriendly. They simply charge different fees: Morocco in money and status, France in time and grammar. The sensible expat budgets for both, then discovers that the currency they brought is the wrong one for at least half of the transactions.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, France — paraphrased experience: A newcomer to a southern village found it hard to make friends at first, then stopped seeking other English speakers and befriended neighbours instead. Now they share tools, attend village events and help each other in emergencies. A quiet, unobtrusive presence earned more trust than enthusiasm.*

> *Expat.com forum, France — paraphrased experience: After eight years, a resident describes locals as guarded in everyday conversation, with friendships forming slowly but lasting once established. People who are outgoing or want emotional openness may struggle in small towns; larger cities are easier.*

> *British Expats forum — paraphrased experience: A Paris resident of more than five years says close friends have moved away, working from home leaves no daily colleagues, and building a circle beyond casual drinks is harder than at university. Replies suggest town hall clubs, charity volunteering and professional associations.*

> *Expat.com forum, Morocco — paraphrased experience: A man who returned to Casablanca after 25 years in the United States wants English-speaking friends and people with similar experiences, and finds several other returnees with the same wish.*

> *Expat.com forum, Morocco — paraphrased experience: A Dutch expat, eight months into life in Casablanca, likes the city and offers to meet newcomers for coffee or a chat in English, while another resident jokes that it is hot, congested and dusty.*

## Conclusion

If you want friends quickly, choose Morocco, accept the tea, and keep an eye on who is asking for what. If you want fewer friends who are harder to win and more durable once won, choose France, join an association, and learn the subjunctive as an act of social strategy. In both countries the reliable method is the same: show up regularly, in a place with a purpose, and be patient.

Either way, bring a hostess gift. In Morocco it is a courtesy and in France it is an application fee.
