**Iceland's CEO Answers Her Own Phone. Tunisia's Office Will Introduce You to Three People Before You Reach the One Who Can**

🇮🇸 Iceland · 🇹🇳 Tunisia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Reykjavík, a company of two hundred people might genuinely have no one who insists on being called by a title, and the person who founded the firm will be on first-name terms with the newest intern by lunch. In Tunis, reaching a decision-maker inside a comparable firm often means moving politely through a sequence of people, each of whom has a real and specific role in whether you get there — a structure that isn't bureaucratic friction so much as the actual shape of how trust and authority are organized. Both are legitimate corporate cultures. One of them simply has far more rungs on the ladder than the other.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone, including senior leadership, from day one | Default to titles or last names; it creates distance nobody is expecting |
| Expect to be heard regardless of seniority — flat really does mean flat here | Assume a junior colleague's idea needs managerial sign-off to be taken seriously |
| Leave on time; long hours are not read as a loyalty signal | Perform visible overtime to impress anyone; it won't land as intended |
| Expect small company size and tight social networks to mean reputations travel fast | Assume anonymity between jobs or employers; Iceland's professional world is genuinely small |
| Bring direct, solution-focused communication to meetings | Over-formalize requests that would be handled with a quick, informal conversation |

#### 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect French to function alongside Arabic as a genuine business language in many firms | Assume English alone will carry you through corporate meetings without friction |
| Build relationships with the specific people positioned between you and a decision-maker | Try to route around intermediaries to reach seniority faster; it can read as disrespectful |
| Budget real time for greetings and hospitality before business begins | Rush straight to the agenda in a first meeting |
| Respect visible hierarchy and titles in both address and seating | Assume a flatter, Western European org-chart style applies by default |
| Recognize regional variation between coastal, internationally-exposed firms and more traditional ones | Assume one Tunisian company's style generalizes to the whole economy |

### Iceland

Icelandic corporate culture is, by most comparative measures, among the flattest and most informal in the world, and the numbers back the reputation: Hofstede's data places Iceland at a low power distance alongside one of the lowest masculinity scores recorded globally, a profile the country shares with its Nordic neighbors and that in practice produces exactly the first-name, open-door, low-ceremony office culture foreign hires are warned to expect. Rivermate's cultural guide and several professional-insight pieces, including Icelandjournal's own coverage, describe a workplace where titles do minimal social work and where a new hire's ideas are evaluated on content rather than rank almost by default.

What's less obvious from the outside is how much Iceland's tiny population (under 400,000) shapes its corporate culture independent of any Nordic cultural script: professional networks are genuinely small, reputations travel fast, and the informality isn't just egalitarian values in the abstract — it's also simply what happens when most of a given industry's professionals have met each other multiple times already. Clickup's business-etiquette guide and several hiring-focused sources both flag this small-world effect as something foreign hires underestimate: being unprofessional in Reykjavík has a much shorter half-life before everyone relevant has heard about it than the same behavior would in a larger market.

### Tunisia

Tunisian corporate culture carries a genuinely layered inheritance — Arab, Mediterranean, and French colonial-era administrative influence all show up in the same office, often in the same meeting. Commisceo Global's Tunisia guides describe French functioning as a real, working business language alongside Arabic in many firms, a legacy of the French administrative and educational system that shaped much of the country's modern commercial infrastructure, and business etiquette guides consistently describe a relationship-first, hierarchy-respecting culture where greetings, hospitality, and personal rapport precede the actual agenda, similar in spirit to patterns seen elsewhere across the wider MENA region.

The hierarchy itself tends to be genuinely multi-layered rather than simply "top-down": the Nestlers Group's business-culture brief and Commisceo's management guide both describe decision-making authority as something a newcomer typically reaches by moving through several intermediaries, each holding real standing in the process rather than functioning as mere gatekeepers. This isn't inefficiency for its own sake — it reflects a trust-building structure where each person in the chain has a legitimate stake in vouching for what moves forward, which a foreign hire used to flatter, faster-routing offices will need real patience to work with rather than around.

### The Reckoning

The contrast here is about as close to a clean inversion as this column gets: Iceland minimizes the distance between any two people in an organization, while Tunisia formalizes and respects that distance as a legitimate structure in its own right. Neither approach is more "modern" than the other — Iceland's flatness is a function of trust built through a genuinely small, tightly networked professional population, while Tunisia's layered hierarchy is a function of trust built through sustained relationship and vouching across more intermediaries in a larger, more institutionally complex economy.

The practical trap for a foreign hire moving between the two: Icelandic flatness can be mistaken for a lack of real authority (it isn't — decisions still get made, just without ceremony), while Tunisian hierarchy can be mistaken for bureaucratic delay (it often isn't either — it's relationship infrastructure doing real work). Reading either country's structure through the other's assumptions will make you misjudge how seriously you're actually being taken.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor answering what it's like to work in an African country generally, drawing partly on Tunisian experience, noted that the layered hierarchy felt slow at first but ultimately meant decisions, once made, had already survived scrutiny from everyone who mattered — making them far less likely to be reversed later than a faster, flatter process elsewhere had produced.*

> *Icelandjournal (professional-insights piece) — The piece specifically describes new arrivals misreading Icelandic informality as a lack of seriousness, only to realize that the same casually dressed colleague addressing the CEO by first name was being held to extremely exacting standards once actual deliverables were due.*

> *Quora — A respondent discussing life in Tunisia for foreigners noted that business relationships genuinely outlasted single transactions — a contact made through one deal would reliably reappear, vouching for or against you, in completely unrelated dealings years later, which changed how carefully they treated every early professional interaction.*

> *Quora — A second contributor, answering on small-business ownership in Tunisia, described French-language fluency as a bigger practical advantage in securing serious business meetings than English, despite Tunisia's geographic and cultural position often being marketed internationally as more Anglophone-accessible than it functions day to day.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Iceland, drop the title, use the first name, and understand that the lack of ceremony is not a lack of standards — it's a tiny, high-trust professional world holding you to exacting ones without the formal scaffolding. If you're heading to Tunisia, invest patiently in the people between you and the decision-maker, bring workable French if you can, and treat the layered hierarchy as relationship infrastructure rather than obstruction. Both countries will judge you fairly. Iceland will just do it faster, and with your first name already memorized.
