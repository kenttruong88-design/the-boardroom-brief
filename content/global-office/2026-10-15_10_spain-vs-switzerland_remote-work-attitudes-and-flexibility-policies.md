**Presentismo vs. the Spreadsheet: Spain and Switzerland Can't Agree on What "Flexible" Means**

**🇪🇸 Spain · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Madrid, you can finish your work by 4pm and still be expected to stay at your desk until your boss leaves, a habit with its own name: presentismo. In Zurich, a new government regulation now requires your employer to put your remote-work "home base" in writing, down to which office you're officially attached to even when you never set foot in it. Spain's flexibility problem is cultural. Switzerland's flexibility problem is bureaucratic. Both will test your patience in completely different ways.

**Spain: The Law Caught Up to Remote Work Faster Than the Culture Did**

Spain's legal framework around remote work is genuinely detailed. Ley 10/2021 requires a formal written agreement once an employee works remotely for 30% or more of their time across a rolling three-month window, and a newer Royal Decree 723/2026 — published in the BOE on 15 September 2026 and in force from 5 October 2026 — goes further, requiring employers to state in writing which physical "work centre" a remote employee is formally attached to, and to disclose any algorithmic systems used to set working conditions. On paper, that's one of the more worker-protective remote-work regimes in Europe.

What the law hasn't shifted much is actual adoption or office culture. InfoJobs' September 2026 report, covered by RemoteWorkEurope.eu, found 25% of surveyed workers telework fully or partly (19% hybrid, 6% fully remote) — statistically unchanged from 2025's 24%, and remote-eligible job postings actually declined slightly year over year even as their overall share held steady. Worker appetite for flexibility, though, is sharp and specific: 35% of workers would job-hunt if required back in the office more, rising to 39% for a fully on-site return, and the split by current arrangement is stark — 49% of fully remote workers would job-hunt versus 31% of hybrid workers, suggesting the people with the most flexibility are also the most determined to protect it. The Local's long-running feature on Spanish workplace phrases captures the cultural layer underneath all of this: "presentismo," the habit of staying visibly at your desk even once your actual work is finished, remains real enough that one listed non-phrase — "If you have completed your jobs why don't you pop off early while I finish up?" — is presented as something you'll essentially never hear a Spanish manager say. A long lunch block (roughly 2-5pm, per the same piece) is a genuine daily fixture, not a relic, even though the siesta-at-home version has mostly disappeared.

**Switzerland: The Policy Is Generous, the Paperwork Is Catching Up**

Switzerland's remote-work story runs in the opposite direction from Spain's: adoption is rising briskly, and the regulatory apparatus is scrambling to formalise what's already happening informally. Indeed's labour-market data, reported by iamexpat.ch, found 14% of advertised Swiss jobs offered remote or hybrid arrangements in Q2 2025 — a record high that puts Switzerland third among major Western economies for remote-job availability, behind only the UK and Germany — with flexible job listings up more than fivefold since before the pandemic. Indeed's Virginia Sondergeld noted Switzerland is "bucking a broader Western trend" of companies like Disney, Apple, and Amazon tightening return-to-office rules, and suggested Switzerland could become a leading European hub for flexible work if the momentum continues. An earlier Adecco survey found 49% of Swiss workers want to keep some home-working days permanently, close to the 53% global average, though 58% said they were also eager to see colleagues in person again — a reminder that Swiss remote-work enthusiasm isn't the same thing as a wish to never return to an office.

The practical reality inside individual companies is messier and more conditional than the headline numbers suggest. A Blind thread specifically about Google's Zurich office — not Swiss remote-work culture broadly, a scope worth being honest about — found employees describing a real but bounded hybrid model: roughly two to three office days a week depending on team, with one commenter specifically warning that "in-office requirements have a way of creeping back over time" and that fully remote status largely applies only to people grandfathered in before policies tightened, not to new hires. Swiss rail infrastructure does genuinely support the arrangement for people who do get it — several commenters described commuting comfortably from towns like Zug and Aarau rather than living in Zurich itself. Hofstede Insights scores Switzerland at a comparatively low 34 on power distance (with a documented "German-speaking region emphasis" in how the original study collected its data), which helps explain why Swiss employees feel able to ask pointed, direct questions about hybrid policy before accepting a role rather than simply accepting whatever's offered — a cultural trait that shows up clearly in how openly Blind's commenters discuss and compare notes on exactly which teams get which deal.

**The Reckoning**

The real contrast isn't which country is more flexible on paper — Spain's legal framework arguably protects remote workers more explicitly than Switzerland's does. It's which gap is actually widening. In Spain, the law has moved further and faster than either adoption or office culture; a worker can have ironclad legal rights to a written remote agreement and still face real, unwritten pressure to stay visibly late at a desk. In Switzerland, adoption and job-market supply have moved further and faster than the old guarantees; a worker can land one of a rapidly growing number of flexible roles and still discover, a few years and one internal transfer later, that the grandfathered arrangement quietly expired.

Put differently: Spain's remote-work problem is that the culture hasn't caught up to the law. Switzerland's remote-work problem is that the law — and company policy — hasn't quite caught up to how fast the culture and job market have already moved.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TheLocal.es — Among the "phrases you'll only hear at work in Spain," the outlet's list of their inverse — things a Spanish manager will essentially never say — included "If you have completed your jobs why don't you pop off early while I finish up?", framed as the clearest possible illustration of presentismo, the pressure to be visibly present regardless of actual workload.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — Responding to a Booking.com employee's question about Google Zurich's WFH policy, one Google employee reported hybrid work with roughly 4 remote weeks a year, while another cautioned directly: "in-office requirements have a way of creeping back over time," especially for newcomers who shouldn't assume current flexibility will hold.*

> *dragosroua.com — A Romanian remote worker based in Spain described the cultural trade-off bluntly: "the 'dolce far niente' atmosphere, especially during siesta, can make work hard to finish," adding that choosing the beach "instead of finishing that project can be simply impossible to resist" — and that newcomers "will need to be way more organized in Spain" than they might expect.*

> *SwissInfo.ch (Adecco survey) — The coverage noted directly that "remote working is not the ideal solution for everyone," citing that 35% of surveyed Swiss workers reported anxiety about returning to the office even as 58% said they were eager to see colleagues in person again — a genuinely two-sided result rather than a clean verdict either way.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for a genuinely flexible work life, the honest framing isn't "which country has better remote-work law," it's "which kind of gap you're willing to manage personally." Spain hands you real legal protection and then asks you to navigate an office culture that may still quietly expect you at your desk past 7pm regardless of what the law or your actual task list says. Switzerland hands you a fast-growing, genuinely competitive market for flexible roles and then asks you to keep a close eye on whether this year's hybrid policy is the one you'll still have in two years, especially after any internal move.

My honest line for a friend: in Spain, get the written remote agreement the law entitles you to, but still watch what time your actual colleagues leave before assuming your contract settles the question. In Switzerland, take the flexible role and enjoy how genuinely available they've become — just don't build your five-year plan around an arrangement nobody there seems fully confident will survive the next reorg.
