# The Right to Disconnect vs. the Struggle to Connect: Zambia and Germany on Working From Home

🇿🇲 Zambia · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Germany has spent the better part of a decade legislating, litigating, and lightly agonizing over whether employees have a legal right to ignore their inbox after 6pm. Zambia, where 76.3% of employed people work informally and 99% of internet subscriptions are mobile, has a more pressing question: whether the inbox will load at all. Both countries are, technically, having a "remote work conversation." They are not having the same one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇲 Zambia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a backup mobile data bundle for "remote" days | Assume fibre will survive the next load-shedding schedule |
| Treat a 9am call as a loose opening bid, not a start time | Stack back-to-back video calls assuming the first one starts on time |
| Expect "remote work" to mean informal, mobile-first self-employment for most people | Assume a written WFH policy exists at most employers |
| Secure a job offer before applying for a work permit | Move to Lusaka assuming you'll find formal work once you land |
| Build trust before requesting flexibility | Open salary or schedule negotiations by asking to skip the office |
| Dress the part, even on camera | Treat video calls as an excuse for pajamas |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect Homeoffice days to be negotiated case by case | Expect a legal right to work from home — it does not exist |
| Track your hours precisely | Answer evening emails hoping it reads as dedication — courts don't require it either way |
| Ask whether a Betriebsrat (works council) sets WFH rules | Skip the works council; they often decide more than HR does |
| Target industries that normalize remote work, like IT or consulting | Expect it in hospitality, retail, or manufacturing |
| Accept two or three office days as the new hybrid baseline | Assume full remote is standard just because Germany ranks above the EU average |
| Respect the cultural and legal quiet on Sundays and holidays | Schedule a "quick" Sunday check-in |

---

In Zambia, the formal labor market that remote-work policy would even apply to is the minority case. ZAMSTATS's 2022 Labour Force Survey put informal employment at 76.3% of everyone working, and the country's unemployment rate, recently reported at 9.9%, masks a youth unemployment rate of 24.7% and a youth NEET rate above 50% ([Equity Axis](https://equityaxis.net/post/19028/2026/4/zambia-s-unemployment-falls-but-the-9-9-rate-masks-a-deeper-working-poverty-crisis)). For most working Zambians, "flexibility" was never a perk management granted; it was the default condition of market trading, farming, or driving, long before anyone called it a lifestyle.

Where formal, salaried remote work does exist, it runs into infrastructure rather than ideology. Ninety-nine percent of Zambia's internet subscriptions are mobile, and Lusaka's connectivity drops at least once a week because of unstable electricity supply, with rural areas worse off; three-quarters of surveyed small businesses cite the cost of internet and devices as a binding constraint, a cost inflated by the country's landlocked geography, a weak kwacha, and a small pool of internet service providers ([International Trade Centre](https://intracen.org/news-and-events/news/digital-connectivity-a-case-study-from-lusaka)). Tech hubs such as BongoHive pivoted staff to remote work before Zambia's first confirmed COVID case and ran informal "Hive Connect" sessions to fight isolation, but the same account notes that organizations with no prior investment in technology struggled simply to choose a collaboration tool ([BongoHive, via Medium](https://medium.com/bongohive/remote-work-is-the-new-normal-2fd300b3a42f)). World Economics puts Zambia's informal economy at roughly 38% of GDP, which is less a side channel than the main road; a formal, HR-departmentalized "remote work policy" is, for most of the labor force, a solution to a problem they do not structurally have. As one South African expat told Expat Arrivals, Zambian workplaces generally run on a looser relationship with time than visitors expect — a 2pm meeting is an estimate, not a contract, deadlines are social suggestions rather than enforceable facts, and office supplies tend to migrate home without anyone calling it theft ([Expat Arrivals](https://expatarrivals.com/article/interview-with-norman-a-south-african-expat-living-in-zambia)). None of that is a verdict on work ethic; it is a description of where the real flexibility in Zambian work culture already sits — in time and in ownership, rather than in location.

Germany's problem is the opposite kind of abundance: formal policy with no formal right attached. Destatis reports that 25% of German employees worked from home at least occasionally in 2025, edging above the EU average of 23%, though the practice skews hard by sector (74% in IT and consulting, 6% in hospitality) and by firm size (35% at large companies, 19% at small ones) ([Destatis, via der Betrieb](https://der-betrieb.de/meldungen/anteil-gestiegen-25-der-erwerbstaetigen-arbeiteten-2025-im-homeoffice/)). Yet there is still no statutory right to work from home, and no statutory right to disconnect either — only the Working Time Act's hour limits and rest periods, enforced case by case, with a 2023 Federal Labour Court ruling explicitly declining to grant an unrestricted right to ignore after-hours messages ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/germany-the-right-to-disconnect-qa)). Expats experience the upside of this ambiguity rather than the downside: InterNations' *Future of Working Abroad* report found 83% of expats in Germany can work remotely, against a 78% global average, and 74% rate their work-life balance favorably ([InterNations, via iamexpat.de](https://www.iamexpat.de/career/employment-news/good-hours-and-job-satisfaction-expats-what-its-work-germany)). A 2020 Hacker News commenter recalled German firms as "highly conservative" about staff working outside the office; the Destatis numbers suggest that conservatism has since been negotiated down to a norm of two or three office days a week, not abolished. The country also now advertises more remote-friendly jobs than most peers: roughly 15% of German job postings are fully or partly remote, ahead of the UK, Canada, France, Italy, and the Netherlands, and about three times the pre-pandemic share, according to Indeed data reported by The Local ([The Local](https://www.thelocal.de/20250424/germany-among-top-countries-for-remote-and-hybrid-work)). The gap between that generous labor-market signal and the absence of a hard legal right is precisely the German compromise: employers are free to be generous, and equally free not to be, and the law stays studiously neutral about which.

What makes the comparison genuinely interesting is how little the two countries' instincts overlap. Hofstede's data has no standalone Zambia profile — Zambia is folded into an "East Africa" cluster (with Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia) scoring low on individualism and high on power distance relative to Germany's famously low-power-distance, high-individualism profile ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com)). In practice, that gap shows up as two different failure modes. Zambian flexibility is temporal and informal, built on relationships and patience, but it is easily broken by a dead router or a denied work permit, as one hopeful mover to Lusaka learned when discovering she could not get a permit without a job offer, nor a job offer without a permit ([expat.com forum](https://www.expat.com/en/forum/africa/zambia/907807-obtaining-a-job.html)). German flexibility is contractual and procedural, built on works councils and the Working Time Act, but it is easily capped by a manager who simply prefers desks occupied, since no statute says otherwise — witnessed on Blind, where Amazon Berlin staff described going into the office roughly three days a week less because policy demanded it than because that was simply what the team did ([Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/back-to-office-in-amazon-berlin-dzsqtjee)).

Put differently: Zambia trusts people, loosely, and distrusts infrastructure, correctly. Germany trusts infrastructure and distrusts the absence of a rule. Neither country has actually solved remote work. They have each solved a different half of it, and each half happens to be the one the other country is still missing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *expat.com forum — a would-be mover to Lusaka spent roughly four years trying to land a job without a work permit, and a permit without a job, before admitting the catch-22 had simply not resolved itself.*

> *Blind — an Amazon employee in Berlin described a team that technically could work from home but mostly chose to show up three days a week anyway, because that was what everyone else was doing.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter recalling pre-2020 Germany described employers as reflexively resistant to the idea of staff working outside the office, treating presence itself as the metric that mattered.*

> *Medium (BongoHive) — a Zambian tech-hub account described organizations that had never invested in basic collaboration tools suddenly needing to choose one overnight, with predictable confusion and slower-than-hoped adoption.*

> *Expat Arrivals — a South African expat in Zambia described arriving expecting punctuality and instead learning to treat a 2pm meeting as an estimate, with lateness rarely acknowledged, let alone apologized for.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will hand a new arrival the remote-work life depicted in a laptop-on-a-beach advertisement. Zambia offers a workforce that has never needed anyone's permission to be flexible about time, paired with infrastructure that will test anyone's patience about reliability. Germany offers a workforce with genuinely strong, Destatis-measured work-life balance, wrapped in a legal framework that still, after years of debate, cannot quite commit to calling any of it a right.

The honest advice for someone choosing between them is to decide which kind of uncertainty they can live with: a country where the schedule is negotiable but the server isn't guaranteed, or one where the server is guaranteed but the schedule still answers to an unwritten rule about how many days a week your desk should look occupied. Pack accordingly: a second SIM card for Lusaka, a clean argument for your works council in Berlin, and, in either city, the understanding that "flexible" is doing a great deal of unpaid labor as a word.
