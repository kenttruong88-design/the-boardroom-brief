# Egypt Will Interrupt Your Meeting; the United Arab Emirates Will Agree With It Politely

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, one language family, and roughly opposite theories of what a meeting is for. In Egypt, by most accounts, the meeting is a lively negotiation in which several people speak at once and nobody takes offence. In the United Arab Emirates, it is a courteous ceremony in which the most senior person says little, the junior ones say less, and a conversation about family can occupy the first half hour before anyone mentions the quarterly numbers. Anyone relocating to either should expect the agenda to be treated as a suggestion, though in different ways.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Allow time for tea, coffee and personal chat before the agenda begins | Open with slide one and an apology for the schedule |
| Use titles and wait for your counterpart to offer first names | Assume first names are on offer at the first meeting |
| Arrive on time even though the meeting may start late | Announce that the meeting has started late |
| Treat interruptions as engagement, and join in | Wait politely for a gap that will never arrive |
| Schedule around prayer times and Ramadan hours | Book a call at the call to prayer, or eat in public in daylight during Ramadan |
| Confirm important answers in writing, since a pleasant answer may not be a commitment | Take an agreeable reply to an email as final |

### United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget at least 30 minutes of small talk before business | Rush a meeting, which is read as rude |
| Greet the most senior person first and work down | Assume the person asking the most questions is the decision-maker |
| Offer a range of options and let the other side choose | Demand an immediate yes or no |
| Follow up after a meeting that went well | Treat a warm meeting as a signed deal |
| Check reduced Ramadan hours before scheduling anything long | Plan a full afternoon of workshops during Ramadan |
| Keep presentations short, since meeting fatigue is now a local complaint | Take a non-urgent phone call in the middle of a meeting |

## Egypt

According to the Babel Group, Egyptians are relaxed about time, though visitors are still advised to arrive on schedule. Meetings tend to open with socialising, and the discussion that follows is long and full of back-and-forth. Interruptions are common and, Babel notes, do not signal disinterest. ExpatExchange adds that meetings may start later than scheduled and that plans are made on the understanding that they may not come to fruition. A foreign colleague writing in Identity Magazine described Cairo meetings as loud and animated, with people talking over one another, and concluded that she could not politely wait for her turn. The skill, in other words, is not patience but timing, which is what patience looks like when it has been asked to compete.

The table is lively, but the chair is not negotiable. Babel observes that most Egyptian companies are hierarchical, so decisions are deliberate and slow, and titles matter. Zawya's reporting on workplace behaviour adds an uncomfortable corollary: one human resources head blamed centralised decision-making and thin management layers for problems that go unaddressed, while a bank HR director said Egyptian labour law lacks clear rules on workplace conduct and bullying. Directness is a moving target. Babel calls Egyptian communication more direct than that of other Arabic-speaking cultures, yet the Identity Magazine writer found that English-language answers were often evasive out of politeness, so that an assurance that nobody minded was frequently untrue.

The calendar is the third variable. Egyptian Streets reported in 2021 that many advertising firms let staff set their own start and end times, while clients may expect weekend meetings and staff are expected to attend. Its September 2026 follow-up observed that answering email from bed and joining meetings from the kitchen have become a baseline expectation, in a year when inflation reached 14.9 percent in July. The flexibility, it turns out, runs in one direction. Zawya's case studies include a senior accountant who worked from 9am to 11pm during Ramadan, and a young financial analyst whose new managing partner treated long hours and weekends as a given, though these are individual accounts rather than survey data. Newcomers should assume the meeting will be shorter on formality, longer on duration, and entirely unrelated to the time printed on the invitation.

## United Arab Emirates

Expatica advises visitors to expect at least 30 minutes of small talk before business begins, and notes that rushing is considered rude. Formal sessions are those in which the most senior people present, discuss and negotiate, and decisions rest with them, which can take a long time. The Gulf News business columnist Hanan Nagi warned in 2018 that customers may expect suppliers to anticipate their needs rather than state them, and that an unhappy client may not voice bad news openly. Cyborlink's etiquette profile is blunter: yes often means possibly, and the real decision-maker may stay quiet and observe while someone less important asks the questions. Expat.com adds that Arabs typically do not refuse directly, so a meeting may seem positive even when it is not, and recommends following up. Cyborlink also warns of frequent phone calls and visits from friends and family during meetings, although Expat.com advises against taking calls yourself unless they are urgent. A newcomer thus has to learn to read the silence, which is harder than reading the agenda and considerably less documented.

The calendar is more regulated than Egypt's. According to Aletihad, the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation announced a two-hour daily reduction in private sector working hours for Ramadan 2024, while federal staff worked 9am to 2:30pm Monday to Thursday and until noon on Friday, with up to 70 percent permitted to work remotely on Fridays. Expat.com notes that the standard week has run Monday to Friday, with Saturday and Sunday off, since January 2022, and that some employers let staff leave for Friday prayers or finish early. A meeting scheduled at 4pm on a Friday is therefore an act of optimism.

The paradox is that the region associated with unhurried meetings is now rethinking them. Gulf News reported in April 2025 that a Dubai athleisure brand prints the question, could this have been a Slack message, on its meeting-room doors, that Emaar's chief executive Mohamed Alabbar has doubted that meetings produce positive decisions, and that one Dubai investment firm partner put the cost of a meeting at between Dh2,000 and Dh6,000 per hour of attendee time. A wellness chain with more than 70 clinics runs 15 to 30 minute meetings and a zero-presentation rule. The deference, meanwhile, persists. Justin Thomas wrote in The National that in high power-distance workplaces staff may hold back ideas and dissent to avoid offending superiors, which is efficient for the superior and expensive for everyone else.

## The Reckoning

According to Hofstede Insights, the UAE scores 90 on power distance and Egypt 70, which is a polite way of saying that both countries respect rank and the UAE would like you to notice. The practical difference is where the hierarchy shows. In Egypt it hides behind noise: the room is crowded, people interrupt, and the decision is made somewhere else by someone who was not necessarily talking. In the UAE it sits in plain view and says little, so the person you are addressing is rarely the person you are persuading. Egypt's meeting rewards the one who speaks up; the Emirati meeting rewards the one who can tell who has not.

The hours tell the same story. Egypt asks for more availability and offers more informality, and the Emirati calendar is better regulated, with defined Ramadan reductions and a weekend that matches the Western one. Both punish the person who assumes an agreeable face means an agreed outcome. If you value being heard, Cairo is easier to join and harder to leave. If you value predictability, the Gulf offers a schedule and withholds the verdict.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A recent arrival in Egypt, working in a call centre after living in New York, found colleagues friendly and generous with food, and teamwork strong, with people covering for a teammate running late. The constant personal questions felt like being interviewed, and snapping fingers to get attention was irritating. The advice offered was to avoid strong political or religious opinions, and to keep management on side, since a manager who dislikes you may pressure you to resign rather than fire you.*

> *Identity Magazine — A foreign colleague in a Cairo office recalled meetings that were loud and animated, where waiting for a turn to speak simply did not work. She learned that people avoided direct answers in English, so her first conclusion that they did not mind was often wrong, and that morning tea and chat with colleagues eventually became the part of the day she liked best.*

> *Zawya — A business development manager at a small Egyptian firm described a meeting in which an executive from another team berated her and the owner. Her own manager laughed, she walked out, and the owner later conceded the executive had misunderstood her.*

> *Hacker News — A developer who had worked in several Gulf cities said Dubai is fast-paced and diverse, with English widely used and work expected to be flawless, while in Riyadh urgent tasks need top-level sign-off and formal one-on-one meetings can run long. The advice was to explain complex ideas to clients with diagrams and flowcharts, and keeping written confirmation of any verbal changes.*

> *Reddit — A newcomer to a Dubai tech firm, promised there would be no micromanagement, reported frequent unnecessary check-ins within weeks and asked whether this is typical. One reply argued it is not a UAE trait but a symptom of weak managers and connections-based promotion, adding that companies in three industries claimed to avoid micromanagement while still demanding daily check-ins.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is a trap for the adaptable. In Egypt, arrive on time, expect the start to slip, speak early and do not read interruption as hostility. In the Emirates, allow for the long preamble, work out who actually decides, offer options instead of demanding answers, and follow up in writing, because the warmest meeting may not be the one that settled anything. In both, confirm commitments after the fact, and ask a local colleague who really runs the room before you spend an hour pitching to the wrong person.

On balance, one country will interrupt you to your face, the other will agree with you to your face, and in both the decision will have been made by someone who was not in the room. Choose your frustration accordingly.
