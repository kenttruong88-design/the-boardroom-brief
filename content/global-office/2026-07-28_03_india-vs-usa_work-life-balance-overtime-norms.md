**Nobody Wins the Overtime Olympics, But These Two Keep Competing Anyway**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇺🇸 USA**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

America ranks 29th out of 41 countries on the OECD's work-life balance measures, with 40% of workers logging 41–50 hours a week and 765 million vacation days going unused annually. India's Factories Act caps the standard week at 48 hours, but IT workers in Karnataka — under 4% of the state's workforce — account for a startling 20% of all transplant patients, largely from organ failure linked to overwork. Both countries have built entire cultural mythologies around hustle. Only one of them writes it into an actual union contract's absence; the other just assumes you'll pick up the phone at 9pm and calls it teamwork.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

### India: The Number on Paper Isn't the Number in Practice

India's legal working-time framework looks moderate on its face. The Factories Act, 1948 caps most roles at 9 hours a day and 48 hours a week, and the national average sits at 45.69 hours weekly, according to labor-law trackers. The ILO's global guidance recommends a 40-hour ceiling and notes productivity measurably declines past 48 hours a week — numbers India's formal statutes broadly respect.

The gap between statute and practice is where the real story lives, particularly in IT and services. Accounts from workers in the sector describe a pattern where the 9-hour day on paper stretches toward 11–12 hours in reality, driven by evening client calls and after-hours pings from managers, with no overtime premium attached because the extra hours are never formally logged as overtime at all. The health data is not abstract: IT employees comprise a small fraction of Karnataka's workforce but a disproportionate share of transplant patients, with a large share showing metabolic dysfunction linked to sedentary, high-stress overwork. The picture is not uniform — several accounts note that product companies and multinational tech offices increasingly offer genuinely better balance, while traditional Indian startups and older-generation leadership retain a harder-driving culture — but the baseline expectation of availability outside contracted hours remains a defining feature for much of the sector.

### USA: No Ceiling, Plenty of Guilt

The US has no federal cap on weekly hours for most salaried employees and no federal mandate for any paid vacation at all — the 10 to 14 days many private-sector workers receive after a year, per BLS data, is a matter of employer policy, not law. What the data shows is a workforce that under-uses even the generous end of that voluntary allotment: over a third of Americans report feeling guilty taking time off, more than double the rate in Europe, and more than half stay connected to work — checking email, taking calls — during time meant to be off.

The OECD's Better Life Index places the US 29th of 41 countries on work-life balance measures, a ranking that sits oddly against the country's high compensation and career-mobility appeal. The mechanism isn't legal compulsion, the way it partly is in Indian IT's client-hour culture — it's social and reputational. Unlimited PTO policies, increasingly common, paradoxically correlate with people taking less time off, not more, because the absence of a hard cap removes the "use it or lose it" pressure that drove utilization under fixed-day systems.

### The Reckoning

The two systems arrive at similar overwork outcomes through opposite mechanisms. India's overtime culture, at least in IT and services, is driven by direct managerial demand and economic precarity — workers describe staying in "survival mode" during layoff cycles, taking unpaid extra hours because refusing feels like a fireable offense. America's overwork is more diffuse and self-administered: no one calls at 9pm demanding a report, but internalized guilt and unclear informal norms around "unlimited" leave produce comparable underuse of time off.

Hofstede's power distance score for India (77) dwarfs that of the US (40), which tracks with why an Indian employee describes saying no to a boss as fundamentally risky in a way an American employee generally would not — even though the American ultimately uses less of their own nominal freedom than the hierarchy might predict.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to India for a services or traditional IT role, budget for the evening call before you sign, and specifically vet whether your prospective employer is a "product company" or a "boomer CEO" shop, because the difference in daily experience is enormous. If you're moving to the US, understand that your restraint is now entirely on you — nobody will stop you from working through your vacation, and the informal social pressure not to use "unlimited" leave is real even without a manager saying a word. The honest version, over a drink: India will tell you no when the client needs you; America will just let you tell yourself no, forever, and call it flexibility.
