# Italy Keeps Its Young Cheap and Czechia Keeps Them Quiet

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Italian workers under 30 earn 36.4 percent less per hour than those over 50, according to figures reported by Il Sole 24 Ore, while Italy's median age has reached 49. In Czechia, 62 percent of Gen Z respondents told Deloitte their employer had recently introduced a return-to-office policy, and nearly half of employees do not give their boss critical feedback directly, per a 2026 survey reported by Expats.cz. Both countries have a generational divide at work. In Italy it shows up on the payslip. In Czechia it shows up in the meeting, specifically in what nobody says during it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about contract type before anything else, since 31.3% of employees aged 15 to 29 are on fixed-term contracts | Assume a permanent contract will follow, since the youth fixed-term share is roughly triple the 10.8% seen across all employees |
| Ask for a salary range in writing, because advertised pay is often unspecified or lower than elsewhere in Europe | Expect pay to climb with your degree, since reports describe employers as unmeritocratic on this point |
| Learn Italian to a working level, since outside international roles colleagues and customers expect it | Plan on English alone and a few months of patience |
| Build personal contacts, which expats say play a major role in finding work | Rely on cold applications as your only strategy |
| Present free time and flexibility as real benefits, since young Italians rank extra free time the top perk (38%) | Treat flexibility as a favour, given that roughly eight in ten young workers told Deloitte they would consider leaving over mandatory full-time on-site work |
| Benchmark your pay against seniority, knowing under-30s earn 36.4% less per hour than over-50s | Mistake a low offer for a personal judgement, when it is a structural one |

### Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Offer flexible hours, which 95% of Czech white-collar workers want according to Grafton | Assume remote work, since 59% of Gen Z and 63% of millennials are fully on-site |
| Build channels for candid feedback, because nearly half of employees do not give bosses critical feedback directly | Read a nod in a meeting as agreement |
| Make the job match the advert, since Gen Z candidates cite the gap between advertised and actual duties as a reason to withdraw | Oversell the role and hope the workload will not be noticed |
| Keep recruitment fast, given that selection averages about three weeks for workers and often exceeds two months for managers | Let candidates wait, because delays cause some to take other offers |
| Offer growth first, since Czech Gen Z rates growth opportunities 4.44 out of 5 | Fill the role with routine and call it stability |
| Seek out younger or well-travelled colleagues for early conversation in English | Skip basic Czech on the grounds that everyone is helpful |

## Italy: A Gerontocracy with Excellent Coffee

Italy's generational gap is arithmetic before it is attitude. Il Sole 24 Ore, reporting on the Confprofessioni Observatory in July 2026, notes that the country's median age has risen from 33 in 1976 to 49 in 2026, and that the peak-earning age bracket has drifted from 45 to 54 up to 55 to 64. Among self-employed professionals the median age is 50 for men and 46 for women. In the late 1980s young self-employed Italians earned about 20 percent more than their elders. By 2022 they earned about 16 percent less, and the 25 to 34 group's share of average income had fallen from 97 percent to 78 percent.

The employed fare little better. The same publication, in August 2026, reports that 31.3 percent of employees aged 15 to 29 held fixed-term contracts in 2025, against 10.8 percent of all employees, and that 61.4 percent of young part-time workers said they could not find a full-time job, compared with an EU average of 18.2 percent. Fortune reported in December 2024 that more than a million Italians had left over a decade, about a third of them aged 25 to 34, and that youth unemployment stood at 17.7 percent in October that year against 15.2 percent across the EU. The average age of leaving the family home was 30, a statistic that explains a great deal about the workplace and, frankly, the dinner table.

Attitudes have followed the economics. In the Gen Z Intelligence Report from Il Sole 24 Ore, published in September 2026, work-life balance ranks first among young respondents at 60.4 percent, ahead of stability and pay. Some 52 percent say they would leave over an unsustainable workload and 54 percent over uncompetitive pay. Deloitte's 2023 survey found burnout rising among Italian Gen Z, and about eight in ten Gen Z and millennial respondents would consider a new job if required on-site full time. A Generali bulletin from December 2025, citing IPE Business School Foundation research, adds that 86 percent of young Italians would turn down a job offer that does not support their balance. This is not a generation that dislikes work. It is a generation that has priced it.

## Czechia: Agreement Is Not the Same as Consent

Czechia's divide runs through the meeting room rather than the payroll. Expats.cz, reporting on the 2026 Employee Barometer from Up Benefity, says nearly half of Czech employees do not give bosses critical feedback directly, and only about one in ten trust management almost completely. The same article describes a traditionally hierarchical management culture in which a Czech colleague who appears to agree may simply be declining an open challenge. Younger staff under international managers, meanwhile, are increasingly expected to speak plainly, which is a skill that the older workplace never asked of anyone.

The young are asking for something else too. Deloitte's 2024 Czech country report found that flexible working hours were the most-cited route to better work-life balance, named by 39 percent of Gen Z and 37 percent of millennials. Yet 62 percent of Gen Z and 54 percent of millennials said their company had recently introduced a return-to-office policy, and 59 percent of Gen Z and 63 percent of millennials work fully on-site. Some 43 percent of Gen Z and 46 percent of millennials report feeling anxious or stressed all or most of the time, lower than the global figures of 51 and 56 percent. Grafton Recruitment, reported by Expats.cz in 2025, found flexibility was the second most important factor in job decisions for millennials and Gen Z, behind salary, but only third for Gen X and fourth for boomers.

Research from the University of Economics in Prague published in April 2025 adds a more earnest picture. Some 67.6 percent of Czech Gen Z respondents call work very important, growth opportunities scored 4.44 out of 5, and 73 percent say meaningful work is crucial. Employers, the study notes, value their digital skills and adaptability but struggle with their reluctance toward routine. The old are not spared either. In an earlier Grafton survey reported by Expats.cz in November 2023, age discrimination ranked first among the concerns of baby boomer jobseekers, while Gen Z candidates cited the gap between advertised and actual duties. Czech Gen Z, in other words, wants to grow, be flexible and be heard. It simply has to do so in an office whose senior generation treats silence as a form of respect.

## The Reckoning

The two countries diverge on what the gap is made of. In Italy it is structural: older workers hold the permanent contracts and the higher pay, while the young absorb the fixed-term roles, involuntary part-time work and a family home they leave at about 30. In Czechia the young are employed, and unemployment sits at 5 percent according to Expats.cz, so the friction is cultural: flexibility versus presence, candour versus deference, growth versus routine. The Italian young are priced out. The Czech young are, by comparison, merely misunderstood.

Hofstede Insights scores both countries almost identically on uncertainty avoidance, 75 for Italy and 74 for Czechia, so neither workplace enjoys surprises, and generational change is received with the same wary courtesy. They part on individualism, with Czechia at 70 and Italy at 53 on the same tool. That fits the evidence: a young Italian leaves the country to solve the problem, while a young Czech may stay and wait for the meeting to end. For a newcomer, Italy tests your tolerance for pay and contract insecurity. Czechia tests your ability to hear what has not been said.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Italy — A teacher based in Abruzzo says hourly pay has barely moved since she arrived in 2010, and that online freelance work now offers more flexibility than the schools do.*

> *Fortune — A young graduate who studied abroad and came back to Rome found that Italian job offers often omit the salary or pay several hundred euros a month less than comparable roles elsewhere in Europe, but stayed to be near family.*

> *Expat.com forum — In a Czech culture-shock thread, a reply points out that younger people and older, well-travelled people often speak English, making them the easiest colleagues to talk with, and that basic Czech helps.*

## Conclusion

If you are young or hiring the young, expect Italy to be demanding about money and Czechia to be demanding about communication. In Italy, check the contract type, the salary range and the language requirement before you accept, and treat a personal contact as a credential. In Czechia, ask about flexible hours and return-to-office rules early, and create ways for colleagues to disagree with you that do not require them to do it in front of the boss.

Older managers in both countries should note that the young have read the numbers, and the numbers are not subtle. Italy offers a good lifestyle to those who can afford it, and Czechia offers a good job to those who can read the room. Pick whichever shortage you are better equipped to survive.
