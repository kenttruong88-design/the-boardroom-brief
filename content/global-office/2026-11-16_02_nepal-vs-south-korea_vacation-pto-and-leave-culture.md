# Nepal Grants the Holidays and Dashain Takes the Rest; South Korea Grants the Leave and Hopes You Forget

🇳🇵 Nepal · 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nepal's civil servants have been described in print as the proud owners of 36 public holidays a year, the highest count in a ranking of countries compiled by a Kathmandu newspaper. South Korea has 11 public holidays and, on paper, 15 days of annual leave after a year of service, which Korean employees use roughly as one uses an emergency exit: reluctantly, and only after checking who will cover. The question for the would-be migrant is not which country offers more time off. It is which one lets you take it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get the official holiday calendar early and plan deadlines around Dashain and Tihar | Promise a client a delivery date that falls inside Dashain |
| Expect and budget for the festival bonus and roughly 15 days off around Dashain and Tihar that staff commonly receive | Be surprised when half the office leaves before the holiday officially begins |
| Check your contract for home, sick and casual leave; the Labour Act sets minimums, and encashment of unused days is possible | Assume the leave you read about in a forum applies to your employer |
| Allow for long mourning leave when a close relative dies; staff are expected to take it | Schedule a launch the week after a family bereavement and ask for a status update |
| Build slack into any plan that depends on a government office, which may declare or reshuffle a holiday on short notice | Treat "tomorrow" as a binding date |
| Take your leave around festivals, when everyone else does | Plan a lone vacation in October and expect colleagues to cover for you |

### In South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract: 15 days after a year of 80% attendance, rising by one day every two years to a cap of 25 | Assume your hagwon or small employer will volunteer your full entitlement |
| Ask your manager early and name a cover colleague; staffing is the commonest obstacle | Announce a two-week trip the day before |
| Keep the dates in writing, particularly if you are a foreign teacher on a fixed-vacation contract | Rely on verbal assurances about your winter break |
| Notice whether unused leave is paid out in cash; many workers take the money | Treat the cash as free money if it means you never rest |
| Use leave in the staggered gaps outside the July and August peak | Expect the summer break to be longer than a few days |
| Learn the vocabulary of the "workers' representative" if your employer invokes one to set leave dates | Sign a leave schedule you have not read |

## Nepal

Nepal's formal leave regime is, on paper, generous and layered. The Labour Act of 2017, as summarised by Onlinekhabar, sets a maximum of eight hours a day and 48 hours a week, one weekly day off, 13 public holidays (14 for women), home leave accruing at one day per 20 days worked, up to 12 days of fully paid sick leave, 14 weeks of maternity leave, 15 days of paternity leave and 13 days of mourning leave. Unused home leave of up to 90 days and sick leave of up to 45 days can be accumulated and are paid out on departure.

The public sector is a separate world. Republica reported that the official gazette lists 28 public holidays for 2082 BS excluding Saturdays, and that once Saturdays, festival days and provincial and local holidays are counted, employees may not need to report to work for at least 140 days a year. Nepali Times' Manish Jha, writing in 2016, calculated that an average office worker had 36 holidays, 15 days of annual leave, 12 casual leave days and six sick days, for 69 days off and about 244 working days. The two newspapers use different years and categories, so treat the numbers as the shape of the thing and not a spreadsheet to plan a budget around. Jha also noted that Nepal has a one-day weekend, and that the Cabinet's readiness to declare new holidays makes reform unlikely.

The calendar is dominated by Dashain and Tihar. A guide for expats published by a Kathmandu trekking company reports that staff are expected to receive an extra month's salary and roughly 15 days off around the two festivals, and that vacations outside them are not customary. The Himalayan Times, in an older district-level report, described office chiefs leaving two weeks before Dashain and residents walking two days to a headquarters only to find the land revenue officer gone. The system gives time off and the discipline to take it, in a pattern that is predictable enough to plan around and unpredictable enough to ruin a Thursday.

## South Korea

Korea's leave law is generous on paper and more coy in practice. Employees earn 15 days after a full year with at least 80% attendance, gain one day every two years from the third year, and reach a cap of 25, according to Vacation Tracker's summary of the law. Carry-over is not set by statute; unused days are generally lost unless the company allows otherwise, though employers must notify workers in writing and are expected to encourage use. Unused days can be paid out in cash. Koreans also work long hours: the OECD series as tabulated on Wikipedia has South Korea at 1,833 hours per worker in 2025, down from 1,908 in 2020.

The usage figures have improved but still tell a story of leave as a privilege. A 2019 government survey reported by Asia Business Daily found regular workers used 10.9 days, a 72.4% utilization rate, with the main reasons for leaving days unused being a preference for the allowance (21.8%), no substitute staff (15.9%) and excessive workload (14.4%). A 2022 work-life balance survey found workers had 17.03 days and used 11.63. The most recent figure, from a Deel analysis of roughly 4,700 contracts across 17 Asia-Pacific countries reported in June 2026, put Korea's share of allotted leave used at 53.3%, second only to Singapore, with a median of 15 days taken. Those numbers rank Korea well within the region while still leaving nearly half of entitlement on the table.

The picture is bleaker for small firms and non-regular staff. A January 2025 survey by the civic group Workplace Bullying 119 found that only half of office workers could use paid annual leave freely, that 23.6% had no paid leave at all, and that 12.8% had a request refused, with 42.2% of those being told it would seriously disrupt the company's operations. Collective breaks are short as well: a Korea Enterprises Federation survey of 720 companies, reported by The Korea Times in 2021, found workers averaged 3.7 days of summer vacation. The foreign English-teacher industry has its own variant: The Korea Herald reported that hagwon teachers frequently find leave assigned by the employer, cut to fixed weeks, or unpaid.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's scores, as reproduced in a business communication text, put South Korea at power distance 60, individualism 18 and uncertainty avoidance 85. A Nepalese journal article citing Hofstede's 2001 edition gives Nepal 65, 30 and 40. The difference that matters here is uncertainty avoidance: Korea's high score suggests a workplace that dislikes the disruption of an absent colleague, which fits the commonest reason workers give for not taking leave, namely that nobody can cover. Nepal's holiday calendar is communal and fixed, so absence is scheduled, shared and therefore tolerated.

Put differently, Nepal offers collective time off that individuals are not asked to justify, while Korea offers individual entitlement that individuals are asked to justify. The worker who moves from Kathmandu to Seoul will find more law and less leave; the one who moves the other way will find the opposite and some difficulty explaining why the office is closed again. Neither is a mistake, and neither is quite what the contract summary suggests once you meet the actual office. They are two answers to the question of who owns your time.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — A Dutch resident of Nepal answered a complaint about the number of holidays by estimating that the Netherlands has a comparable total of days off, and argued the real problem is unpredictability: businesses never know when people will be unavailable. His advice was to obtain a Nepali calendar and plan around it.*

> *Expat.com forum — A woman who exports goods from Kathmandu described Dashain shutting offices and customs, so shipments sat while overseas buyers asked where their orders were. She concluded that the festival calendar makes international business harder.*

> *The Korea Herald via Asia News Network — A teacher who arrived in Korea at 22 said he worked about nine hours straight most days and was not allowed any annual leave; at his next school, leave was limited to five set days in summer and five in winter. Another described leave handed down as a random Wednesday off, which made visiting family abroad nearly impossible.*

> *ESL Cafe forum — In a 2014 thread, teachers advised a colleague whose school had revoked planned vacation days to insist on the contract dates. One poster said holding firm on five contracted days had worked; another said Korean employers often give less vacation than expected, and that a teacher whose winter break was revoked later heard her former school had spoken ill of her.*

> *Gord Sellar blog — A Canadian teacher who spent about ten years in Korea wrote that employers expected considerable deference while showing little respect, that overtime was often unpaid, and that he was once dropped from the schedule with no notice.*

## Conclusion

If you value being left alone with your leave, Nepal gives you a calendar you can study and a legal framework with real minimums, provided you accept that a holiday may be added overnight and that Dashain is not negotiable. Bring a plan for deadlines that falls on neither side of it. If you value individual control, Korea grants a statutory ladder up to 25 days, but you will need to ask early, name your cover and, if you teach in a hagwon, get the dates in writing.

The practical test is simple: before you sign, ask how many days the last person in your role actually took. In Nepal the answer will be a number you can predict. In Korea the answer will be a pause, and the pause will tell you the rest.
