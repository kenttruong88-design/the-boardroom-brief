# Slovenia Pays You to Stay Home With the Baby; Italy Pays You Back for the Nursery, Eventually

🇸🇮 Slovenia · 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Slovenia's government says maternity leave runs 105 days at full pay. Italy's pays 80 percent for five months. In Slovenia, according to a European Commission report using 2024 data, 57.8 percent of children under three are in formal childcare, against an EU average of 39.2 percent. In Italy, Istat counts 31.6 nursery places per 100 children under three, and nearly one in five Italian women under 50 left their job after their first child, according to a government report cited by Reuters. Two neighbouring countries have chosen different answers to the same question, and the answer you will live with depends mostly on where the nursery is.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the long, fully paid leave; the government page lists 105 days of maternity leave at 100 percent | Quote day counts for parental leave without checking; sources disagree |
| Book kindergarten hours early; half of surveyed parents want longer opening hours | Assume the hours match a modern commute |
| Check the language of the programme; most run in Slovene | Assume childcare will be in English |
| Ask about shorter working hours if you want them | Expect your employer to offer them; only 4 percent of parents used them until a child turned three |
| Plan for fathers' leave to be real, and take it | Assume the average take-up of 14 days is a target |
| Be ready for family plans to come up in interviews or promotions | Assume the workplace is neutral |

### In Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply early for the bonus nido, and budget to pay nursery fees upfront and wait for repayment | Assume the subsidy arrives with the invoice |
| Check your city and region; places range from 19 per 100 children in the South to 48 in Umbria | Assume nursery availability is national |
| Expect paternity leave of ten days | Plan around fathers' leave being equal; an equal-leave bill failed in 2026 |
| Know that state nurseries cost about 300 to 500 euros a month and private ones in northern cities 800 or more | Budget for a single price |
| Ask for flexibility in writing | Accept informal pressure to resign or go part-time |
| Keep records of any change to your role after maternity leave | Assume you cannot challenge a dismissal |

## Slovenia

Slovenia's family policy is generous on paper and honest about its weaknesses. The government's page on family rights lists maternity leave of 105 days at 100 percent of pay with no ceiling, and Eurofound's Slovenian profile agrees on the 105 days, 28 of them before the birth. The two sources disagree on the rest: the government page lists 15 days of paternity leave and parental leave of 160 days per parent, while Eurofound lists up to 30 days of paternity leave and 130 days of parental leave per parent. We cannot resolve the discrepancy, and an expectant parent should check the primary statute before planning anything around it.

The childcare picture is clearer. According to the European Education and Training Monitor for 2024, 57.8 percent of children under three in Slovenia are in formal childcare, well above the EU average and above the country's own target of 46 percent. Radio Slovenia International reported a survey of Slovenian mothers in which half of respondents wanted kindergartens open longer, 35 percent of men took paternity leave but only for 14 days on average, and only 4 percent of parents used shorter working hours until the child was three. A working parent in the same piece said kindergarten hours are too short for modern commutes and should stretch into the evening, and another planned to work part-time until the son turned three.

The less comfortable finding concerns the workplace. In that survey, almost one in four respondents, mostly women, said family plans came up in job interviews or before a promotion. A country with excellent childcare coverage and long paid leave is still, apparently, a country in which an interviewer wonders what your plans are. We could not retrieve Hofstede's scores for Slovenia, and therefore make no comparison on that front, which seems a restrained response to a topic that has enough confident opinions already.

## Italy

Italy's challenge is supply and money. Istat figures reported by The Local Italy in May 2026 show 31.6 nursery places per 100 children under three, with 19 in the South and islands and 48.4 in Umbria. State nurseries cost around 300 to 500 euros a month and private ones in northern cities 800 or more. The 2024 bonus nido went to about 521,000 children, a third of those aged 0 to 3, and reimbursed an average of 205 euros a month, about 54 percent of fees, with families paying upfront and waiting for repayment. According to the OECD figures cited by Reuters, Italy spends about 0.1 percent of GDP on childcare for ages 0 to 2, against 0.6 percent in France and 0.8 percent in Denmark.

The careers consequence is documented. Reuters reported in 2023 that nearly one in five Italian women under 50 left work after their first child, according to a government report on 2021, and that 29 percent of those said they were fired or not renewed. Female employment stood at 52.6 percent in the second quarter of 2023, the lowest in the EU, and 37,662 mothers claimed unemployment benefits for resigning before their baby turned one in 2021. Il Sole 24 Ore, citing the World Bank's Women, Business and the Law 2026 report, put mothers' earnings about 40 percent below other women's 15 years after a birth, and Istat found that women do 4 hours 44 minutes of domestic work a day, against 2 hours 6 minutes for men.

Leave rules do not rebalance this. The Local Italy's guide, from 2022 and possibly outdated, lists five months of maternity leave at 80 percent of pay and ten days of paternity leave at 100 percent, and a 2026 parliamentary vote rejected equal non-transferable parental leave, 137 votes to 117. Hofstede's dataset, via a university copy, gives Italy a masculinity score of 70, which captures the arrangement more precisely than we could, though there is no Slovenian number to set beside it.

The practical lesson for an expat parent is that the headline benefit is rarely the one that decides your year. In Slovenia, it is likely to be the kindergarten day, since half the surveyed parents want longer hours and most programmes run in Slovene, which matters if your child's first language is not. In Italy, it is likely to be the cash-flow: the bonus nido reimburses about 54 percent of fees on average, after you have paid them, so a family with a tight month should treat the reimbursement as a delayed refund and not as a subsidy. In both countries, find out early what your employer's actual practice is, because the statute and the office are not always on speaking terms.

## The Reckoning

Slovenia is the easier country in which to combine a career and an infant, and the evidence is the childcare rate: 57.8 percent of the under-threes in formal care against Italy's 31.6 places per 100. It has full-pay leave, a nursery system with real coverage and a government that publishes its own survey of mothers' complaints. Its flaws are the length of the kindergarten day, the low take-up of shorter hours and an employer culture that still asks about your plans.

Italy offers a more fragmented system whose quality depends on the region and the size of the employer. A mother in Umbria with a state nursery place and a supportive firm will fare very differently from one in the South with neither. For an expat family, Italy rewards research and punishes assumption: check the city, check the fees, check the rules for your type of contract and check what happens to the job when you come back. Slovenia is simpler. Italy is bigger, and, at the right address, warmer.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Radio Slovenia International — a working parent says kindergarten hours are too short for modern commutes and should stretch into the evening; another plans to work part-time until their son turns three; a young man says he would share childcare with his future wife and stay home as long as finances allow.*

> *The Local Italy (2014 interview) — an American mother of two in Florence says work is the biggest obstacle for expat mothers, that you should arrive with a job lined up or save heavily, that employees are expected at work when a child is ill and staying home means using your own sick days, and that mothers who avoid speaking Italian struggle most. The interview is dated.*

> *Reuters via Business Day — a woman identified only as Elena, with a seven-month-old, had no nursery place and could not afford a full-time babysitter, and her small employer refused flexible hours. Another woman, 50, was dismissed after her second maternity leave in 2011, sued and won, so her daughters would not be pushed out of work.*

> *Al Jazeera — a 30-year-old writer earning about 800 euros a month on a part-time contract says she and her partner want children but are too financially unstable, and a 26-year-old social media strategist in Padua on about 1,200 euros a month expects little salary growth and finds a child unaffordable.*

> *Gulf Today — an Italian father who is the primary caregiver while his wife works full-time says he was the only dad at kindergarten six years ago and there are now three or four. About 85 percent of his online followers are women.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two for a family, Slovenia is the more predictable option, with fully paid leave, a high share of children in formal care and a reasonably open conversation about what remains to be fixed. Check the leave day counts with the authority that pays them, and ask about kindergarten hours before you pick a neighbourhood. Italy requires more work: choose the city with the nursery places, apply for the subsidy early and treat any employer's flexibility as something to put in writing.

In both countries, the system will provide the minimum. The career effect is a matter of the company you work for, and the interview is the cheapest time to find out what it thinks of children.
