# Thailand Bows to the Founder, Vietnam Salutes the Org Chart

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇻🇳 Vietnam

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In May 2026, StartupBlink ranked Thailand 49th in the world for startup ecosystems, its first appearance in the top 50 in six years. In January 2026, Vietnamese outlets reported that the country's venture market had fallen for a fifth consecutive year. A reasonable observer might conclude that Thailand is the place for the entrepreneurial mindset and Vietnam is the place for the sensible job with a pension. A better observer will notice that both countries score highly for hierarchy and low for individualism, which means the startup in either one is simply a corporation with fewer chairs.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Repeat instructions back and invite questions, since staff may signal understanding when they are unsure | Take a nod as confirmation that anyone has understood anything |
| Give criticism privately and constructively | Correct a colleague in front of the team, where it is frowned upon |
| Check a startup's work permit and visa arrangements in writing before you resign from anything | Accept a promise of a quick visa without checking the firm is registered to employ foreigners |
| Weigh a multinational's branding and package against its approvals, which often wait for headquarters | Expect a regional office to decide anything on a Tuesday |
| Learn the concept of kreng jai, the reluctance to impose on others | Read silence from a junior as agreement or as dissent without asking again |
| Ask about overtime and leave in the offer, because long hours are common | Assume the statutory minimum of six leave days after a year is the norm you will enjoy |

### Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go through your line manager, even when the director is two desks away | Email the director directly to speed things up |
| Soften direct feedback, which can be heard as blunt | Deliver the full unvarnished opinion in the meeting |
| Expect a working day of about 7 am to 5 pm with a long lunch and often a nap | Schedule a decisive call for the hour after lunch |
| Offer private, one-to-one feedback where face is at stake | Criticise anyone in public, where the damage is lasting |
| Ask whether a startup's salary has room to grow with profits | Assume a startup offer is as large as the market rate for your skills |
| Budget six to twelve months to adapt, as one relocation firm advises | Judge the company in the first two weeks |

## Thailand: Respect Is the Operating System

Thailand's startup scene is genuinely better than it was. According to The Nation, citing StartupBlink's 2026 index, the ecosystem grew by 62.6 percent, Bangkok ranked 76th globally, and Thailand led ASEAN in medical-technology startups. The same report ranks the country second in Asia-Pacific for startup community activity. Regional context tempers the story: a Thailand Business News report on the full-year 2025 funding data put Southeast Asian venture funding at 5.4 billion US dollars across 461 deals, with Singapore taking 91 percent of the capital.

The workplace underneath the startup is the traditional Thai one. Hofstede-derived figures reproduced by Rework give Thailand a power distance score of 64 and an individualism score of 20, which is the arithmetic form of what The Thaiger describes in its guide to the Thai workplace: decisions generally flow from the top, staff may hesitate to question superiors and may signal understanding when unsure, and public criticism is avoided. Michael Page Thailand adds a corporate wrinkle. Local companies, it says, are associated with a hierarchical office culture and more politics, while multinationals offer better branding and packages but slow approvals, because major decisions need headquarters, and a low chance of reaching the top because employees are seen as replaceable.

Time off is where the mindset becomes measurable. According to The Nation, the ILO ranks Thailand third in the world for long working hours, with 46.7 percent of workers exceeding 48 hours a week. A survey cited in the same piece, which the newspaper does not attribute, found that 80 percent of employees feel they deserve more leave but hesitate to take it, and 74 percent have cancelled a holiday because of workload. That is the corporate and startup mindset together: presence is a form of loyalty.

Small firms have their own particular hazards. A 2017 Techsauce guide to foreign startups in Thailand, which is dated and should be read as such, noted that a foreigner generally needs Thai partners, in a 51 to 49 ownership split, and that six-day working weeks were then standard. Michael Page adds one modern inducement: some Thai companies now offer shares as an incentive. A candidate comparing a local startup with a multinational should therefore ask about equity, the working week and the registration of the employer in that order, which is the reverse of how most job advertisements present them.

## Vietnam: The Reset Reaches the Office

The Vietnamese startup market is in an earnest period of correction. VinVentures figures reported by VnEconomy put 2025 at about 41 deals worth roughly 215 million dollars, the fifth consecutive annual decline. DealStreetAsia data, relayed by Vietstock, had 2024 at 494 million dollars across 68 deals, and the investor Vinnie Lauria of Golden Gate Ventures described the slowdown as a structural reset rather than a cyclical one. The two trackers do not use the same method, but the direction is consistent. The regional full-year funding report, as relayed by Thailand Business News, likewise says Vietnam's deal volumes weakened in the second half of 2025, though it gives no country figure. For an employee, this is the practical meaning of a reset: fewer funded startups, more caution about burn rates, and a stronger case for the employer that already owns a building.

The workplace is hierarchical in a way that startups do not escape. Rework's reproduction of the Hofstede scores gives Vietnam a power distance of 70 and individualism of 20, and VnExpress International's reporting reads like the footnotes. An American teacher was told to go through his line manager rather than talk to the director, colleagues stayed until 9 pm despite official hours of 7 am to 5 pm, and a Navigos survey found that 60 percent of foreign respondents experienced culture shock at work. A 2025 VnExpress piece on returning overseas Vietnamese adds that time at the desk is sometimes used to judge productivity, and that a manager at a financial firm preferred temporary data-entry staff to a digital system that would have cost far more.

The corporate and startup distinction does exist, though it is more a matter of structure than of attitude. A career-advice article from the Vietnamese platform Aniday says big corporations have well-established management structures and comprehensive rule systems, whereas in startups the boundaries between roles are negligible, and interns there took on significant tasks. Expat.com's guide to Ho Chi Minh City warns that startup salaries may be lower than expected, and that some companies must plan to train Vietnamese staff to replace foreign ones. The same guide calls Vietnamese hierarchy less strict than in other Confucian-influenced cultures, notes that English is widely spoken in multinationals, and observes that locals often come first in the job market. VnExpress adds a detail that no organisational chart records: a company-funded team-building trip for 100 employees was valued by one returnee as the best route to colleagues in other departments, and the post-lunch nap, initially baffling, eventually improved her afternoons.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Thailand has the healthier startup narrative and Vietnam has the more sobering one, yet the day-to-day experience of an employee is similar. Both are hierarchical, both prefer private feedback to public debate, and both reward visible effort. Thailand's longer working hours are documented by the ILO, whereas Vietnam's are documented by anecdote, which says more about the data than the workplaces.

The differences that matter to an individual are practical. One anecdote from a Bangkok startup suggests visa and registration risk is real for small firms, and a multinational there offers branding with slow approvals. Vietnam's startups are operating in a funding drought that favours capital efficiency, which may be cheerful for survivors and unpleasant for everyone else. Hours are the final differentiator, and the sources disagree on tone. Expat.com's Ho Chi Minh City guide describes work-life balance as high compared with some home countries, with Korean and Chinese expats noticing more free time, yet VnExpress records colleagues staying until 9 pm. Perhaps both are accurate: the formal day is short and the real day is long, and nobody objects to either. Choose Thailand if you want the better ecosystem trend, and Vietnam if you want a market in which the sensible employers are still the ones with a balance sheet.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, paraphrased experience: A European engineer who worked on a high-tech plant north of Bangkok found Thai colleagues friendly but reluctant to make decisions, saw budget wasted by corruption, and quit after a year. Another poster described an office where nobody arrives on time. The thread is small and mostly negative, so treat it as individual testimony.*

> *HackerNoon, paraphrased experience: An American engineer in Bangkok joined a five-person startup for the salary and a promised quick visa, then found four months later that neither he nor three colleagues had a permit. Hours stretched to six days a week with no sick leave, and he left when he discovered the firm was not registered to employ foreigners.*

> *VnExpress International, paraphrased experience: An English video editor quit a Ho Chi Minh City media company after two months, finding the office so quiet and the colleagues so unresponsive to after-work socialising that he called it dull and cold. An American teacher in the same city thought the formal hierarchy was surprising but admired how little his colleagues complained.*

> *Reddit, paraphrased experience: A young Indian engineer weighing a Ho Chi Minh City offer, with a thirteen-month pay structure and a flat tax, was told by commenters to think of it as a rare chance, a bigger ocean in which he would be a smaller fish. As reported, the thread never says whether the employer is a startup or a corporation.*

> *Blind, paraphrased experience: A Vietnamese-born commenter who quit when a manager refused remote work now lives in Vietnam at a remote startup, and advises targeting Western-registered companies with a Vietnam presence and negotiating hard. One reply warned that Vietnam is a restricted country for some corporate tools.*

## Conclusion

If the choice is between a startup and a corporation in either country, make it on structure, not on slogans. A multinational gives you rules, branding, and the slow approvals that come with them. A startup gives you breadth and a smaller title, and in Thailand in particular it should give you a registered employer and a work permit before it gives you a ping-pong table. In both countries, learn the local form of disagreement, which is usually private and indirect, before you announce an open-door policy.

Expect the adjustment to take most of a year, and do a trial stay if you can. The honest summary is that the startup mindset travels poorly in both places, while the hierarchy travels extremely well.
