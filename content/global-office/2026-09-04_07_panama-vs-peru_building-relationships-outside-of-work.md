# Panama Has the Second-Widest Hierarchy Gap on Earth. Peru Just Wants You Over for Dinner First.

**🇵🇦 Panama · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Panama scores 95 on Hofstede's power distance index and just 11 on individualism — a gap of 84 points, the second-largest divergence recorded anywhere in the world. Peru, by contrast, sits close to the Latin American average on nearly every dimension: unremarkable, in the statistical sense, in almost every way except one. Both countries will tell you the same thing if you ask how to get ahead: it isn't your resume, it's who invited you to dinner.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇦 Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect small talk to open every meeting — it's relationship infrastructure, not filler | Rush past it to "save time"; it reads as disregard for the person, not efficiency |
| Invest real time before expecting new contacts to trust you with anything substantial | Expect immediate acceptance after one good meeting |
| Attend community events and local traditions when invited, even outside your comfort zone | Decline repeatedly and expect the same invitations to keep coming |
| Respect rank and role even with colleagues who are personally warm and informal | Mistake social warmth for permission to skip formal deference to seniority |
| Use InterNations and expat meetups — Panama City runs an active chapter with biweekly events | Assume you'll build a network passively without showing up somewhere regularly |
| Show consistent respect for family values in conversation and social settings | Treat family topics as small talk to move past quickly |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to shared meals, drinks, and group outings with colleagues — they're where trust ("confianza") is actually built | Treat after-work socializing as optional networking rather than core relationship work |
| Expect timelines of 2–6 months to build a solid social network, faster with real Spanish effort | Get discouraged early or assume slow social integration reflects unfriendliness |
| Learn and use "personalismo" — loyalty to specific people, not just institutions, drives outcomes | Assume institutional credentials substitute for personal relationships |
| Join structured groups — running clubs, volunteer work, InterNations — to accelerate real connections | Wait for organic encounters alone; structured entry points work faster |
| Calibrate social behavior to hierarchical position — age, authority, and status all matter | Treat every relationship as equally informal regardless of seniority |
| Recognize younger professionals' expectations are shifting even as family-centered norms hold | Assume Peru's workplace culture is monolithic across generations |

### Panama: Warmth Layered Over Steep Hierarchy

Panama presents a genuine puzzle for newcomers: its business culture is described as warm, sociable, and full of small talk, yet it sits on top of one of the steepest hierarchies measured anywhere. According to [ResearchGate's Hofstede analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Hofstedes-Scores-for-Individualism-Comparing-Latin-America-and-East-Asia-with-the-USA_fig1_370136888), Panama's 84-point gap between power distance (95) and individualism (11) is the second-largest divergence of any country surveyed globally — meaning deference to rank and deep group loyalty coexist in a way that can feel contradictory if you're used to either trait showing up alone.

[Commisceo Global's business guide](https://commisceo-global.com/country-guides/panama-guide/) describes the practical result: a Panamanian colleague may be genuinely warm and sociable in conversation while still expecting clear, unspoken respect for rank and role underneath it. New contacts need real time to build confidence before business moves smoothly — [Pallas's relocation guides](https://pallaslife.com/knowledge/building-a-social-network-in-panama/) recommend leaning on co-working spaces, community events, and consistent participation in local traditions rather than expecting networking to happen passively. Panama City's InterNations chapter, with meetups roughly every two weeks, is frequently cited by expats as the fastest structured entry point into this otherwise slow-building social world.

### Peru: Confianza as Currency

Peru's relationship-building culture runs on a specific, named concept: confianza, or trust, built through sustained personal relationship rather than credentials or institutional standing alone. [Cultural Atlas](https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/peruvian-culture/peruvian-culture-business-culture) and [Commisceo Global's Peru guide](https://commisceo-global.com/country-guides/peru-guide/) both emphasize "personalismo" — loyalty to specific people, including family and friends — as the actual linchpin of successful business relationships, more so than formal process.

Team outings, shared meals, and group activities aren't treated as extracurricular perks; they're described as the mechanism through which trust and warmth are built in ways that directly support collaboration back at the office. Social behavior calibrates to hierarchy — age, authority, and economic status all shape expected conduct — while family remains, per [Rivermate's cultural guide](https://www.rivermate.com/guides/peru/cultural-considerations), the central influence on ideas about loyalty and responsibility, even as younger professionals begin to shift expectations somewhat.

### The Reckoning

Both countries agree that relationship capital outweighs credential capital — but they diverge sharply on how visible the hierarchy underneath that warmth actually is. Panama's extreme PDI-IDV gap means the warmth can feel almost performative to an outsider: genuinely friendly, but layered directly over rank that never actually softens. Peru's more "average" Hofstede profile masks a system that's arguably more consistent — the trust-building process (confianza) and the hierarchy calibration happen together, continuously, rather than existing as two separate registers.

The practical trap for newcomers is assuming Panama's friendliness means the hierarchy is negotiable, or assuming Peru's family-centered warmth means personal relationships alone can substitute for demonstrated respect toward seniority. Neither assumption survives contact with either office for long.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *InterNations Panama — A long-time member described the biweekly meet-and-greet events as the single most reliable way expats actually break into Panama City's social scene, noting that people who skipped the organized events in favor of trying to network organically consistently took far longer to build any real local connections.*

> *Quora — An expat who had lived in Panama for eight years with family was asked what finally made the country feel like home, and pointed not to language or paperwork but to the moment a Panamanian neighbor started including them, unprompted, in extended-family gatherings — something they said no amount of professional networking had achieved on its own.*

> *Expat.com, Lima forum — A contributor asking how to make friends in Peru was advised that joining a structured group — specifically the Lima Hash House Harriers running club — did more for their social integration in two months than a year of casual encounters had managed, crediting the recurring, built-in reason to show up.*

> *Quora — Someone considering retiring to Panama was warned by a longtime expat that the initial warmth of new Panamanian acquaintances shouldn't be mistaken for closeness — genuine friendship, they said, arrived only after repeated, patient presence over months, not after a single good conversation.*

> *Expat.com, Lima forum — A separate poster cautioned that building a real network in Lima typically took two to six months, and that proactivity and Spanish fluency were the two biggest levers — those who waited passively for invitations reported feeling isolated far longer than those who joined volunteer work or clubs early.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving for work, treat the office org chart as secondary information in both countries — the real map is who you've shared a meal with, who's invited you back, and how patiently you've shown up before expecting anything in return. According to expat communities in both, the timeline is measured in months, not weeks, and shortcuts tend to backfire.

Priya's honest take, over a drink: in Panama, mind the hierarchy under the smile. In Peru, mind the dinner invitation — accepting it is half the job.
