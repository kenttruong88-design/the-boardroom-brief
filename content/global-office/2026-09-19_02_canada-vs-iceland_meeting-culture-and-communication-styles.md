**"Have You Thought About Approaching It From This Angle?" Means Something Very Different in Reykjavík**

🇨🇦 Canada · 🇮🇸 Iceland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Canadian manager will spend four sentences hedging a piece of feedback you're expected to correctly decode as urgent; an Icelandic manager will spend four words telling you exactly what's wrong, over coffee, and consider the matter closed. Both offices will call themselves collaborative. Only one of them means it will happen in a room where the CEO might personally weigh in before lunch.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for the "sandwich" — a compliment, then a concern, then another compliment — the concern is the real message | Take "you might want to consider..." as an optional suggestion; treat it as a required correction |
| Soften your own disagreement in meetings ("I wonder if there's another way to look at this") | Contradict a colleague bluntly in a group setting, even if you're certain you're right |
| Match the room's diplomacy — restraint reads as competence, not weakness | Mistake politeness for indecision; Canadians are direct about facts, just gentle about delivery |
| Follow up one-on-one if you're unsure whether feedback was a suggestion or an instruction | Assume silence in a meeting means agreement — it may mean unspoken discomfort |
| Use first names and casual tone once invited to, but let seniority set the pace | Skip the small talk at the start of a meeting — it's doing real relationship work |

#### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a straight answer when you ask a direct question, and give one back | Read bluntness as rudeness or hostility — it's meant as respect and efficiency |
| Speak up regardless of your title — flat hierarchy means interns' input gets genuinely weighed | Wait to be invited to contribute; silence reads as disengagement, not deference |
| Treat informal, coffee-table meetings as fully legitimate decision-making venues | Assume a casual setting means the outcome isn't binding |
| Go directly to whoever's relevant to your issue, skipping formal chains of command | Over-explain or pad requests with lengthy preamble — get to the point quickly |
| Show up on time — punctuality is treated as a form of respect, not a formality | Take blunt feedback personally or expect a "compliment sandwich" softening it |

---

Canada's communication style is best understood as directness wrapped in diplomacy rather than indirectness itself — Canadians generally do say what they mean, but the packaging is doing a lot of work. Berlitz's guide to Canadian workplace communication describes phrases like "you might want to consider..." or "I wonder if there's another way to approach this..." as corrections delivered diplomatically, where the hedge functions as a courtesy rather than genuine uncertainty; missing that distinction is one of the most common ways newcomers misread the seriousness of feedback. The compliment sandwich — positive observation, concern, positive close — is so embedded in Canadian workplace feedback that professionals from more direct cultures sometimes miss the middle layer, the actual concern, entirely.

Iceland runs on the opposite operating assumption. With one of the flattest organizational structures in the developed world, Icelandic CEOs are frequently in direct contact with managers well below them in the org chart, and decisions move fast precisely because there are fewer layers to route through. BusinessCulture.org and multiple expat guides describe Icelanders as valuing honesty over politeness for its own sake: ask a direct question, expect a direct answer, and don't read bluntness as coldness — Icelanders simply don't perform friendliness the way some cultures do, though the warmth, once trust is built, is described as genuine and durable. Meetings are frequently informal, held over coffee, and that informality doesn't diminish their authority — a decision made casually is still a decision.

**The Reckoning**

The instructive contrast here isn't "polite versus rude" — it's where each culture locates its formality. Canada keeps its hierarchy loosely implicit but wraps communication in visible ceremony; Iceland strips away nearly all hierarchical ceremony but expects communication to be unadorned and immediate. A newcomer who over-explains and hedges in Reykjavík will be read as evasive; a newcomer who states blunt disagreement in Toronto will be read as combative — the exact same instinct, direct problem-naming, lands as a virtue in one office and a liability in the other.

There's also a speed paradox worth noting: Iceland's flat structure and blunt style should, in theory, make decisions faster, and largely do — but Canada's more hedged, consensus-oriented style, despite looking slower on the surface, is often optimized to avoid the exact kind of rework that comes from a decision made too fast without buy-in. Neither approach is more "efficient" in isolation; they're solving for different failure modes.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One respondent on why Canadians avoid direct confrontation explained that the instinct isn't conflict-avoidance so much as risk-management — an indirect phrasing leaves both parties room to back away from an idea without anyone losing face, which he said took him years working outside Canada to fully appreciate as a deliberate skill rather than mere politeness.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about Canadian workplace soft skills wrote that the real test isn't whether you can be polite, but whether you can hear the actual ask buried inside three sentences of hedging — new hires who take feedback at face value, he noted, tend to repeat the same mistake for months.*

> *Expat guide (Iceland, Talkpal cultural do's and don'ts) — A contributor described asking an Icelandic colleague for feedback on a first draft and receiving, without preamble, a list of exactly what didn't work — jarring at first, until she realized no one in the office seemed to think anything of it, and that the directness meant she never had to guess where she stood.*

> *Guide to Iceland relocation forum — One new arrival wrote that the biggest adjustment wasn't the bluntness itself but the pace it enabled — a decision that would have taken three meetings and a follow-up email in their old job got settled over one coffee, which felt almost too casual to be binding until it clearly was.*

> *Northeastern University culture-shock blog (Iceland) — A study-abroad participant described initially reading Icelandic reserve as unfriendliness, then realizing weeks later that colleagues who seemed distant at first had quietly become genuine, low-maintenance friends — the warmth was real, it just never announced itself.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding between them, ask yourself honestly whether you're someone who needs to be told the actual problem, or someone who needs the problem framed gently enough that you can act on it without feeling attacked — both are legitimate ways to run an office, and each will frustrate a newcomer wired for the other. My honest read, over a drink: Iceland is easier to adjust to fast, because the rules are stated out loud; Canada takes longer, because the rules are the thing left politely unsaid.
