# Singapore Ranks You on a Curve and Hong Kong Disagrees With the Result

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both cities run annual performance reviews as a matter of routine, and both employ staff who would sooner not discuss them. In a Randstad survey, 47 percent of Singaporean respondents said they are reviewed annually, while in Hong Kong 52 percent reported the same, the highest share of any market in that Workmonitor round. The difference lies not in the calendar but in what happens afterwards. Singapore tends to produce a ranking, often on a curve, and a manager with limited time to explain it. Hong Kong tends to produce a score that employees, by their own account, mostly dispute. The reader considering either should plan for the meeting, and also for the silence that precedes it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Fill in your self-assessment carefully, since managers compare it against their own ratings before ranking | Treat the self-grading form as a formality |
| Ask your manager early in the year how ratings are calibrated and whether a forced distribution applies | Assume a good year guarantees a good grade when a curve means someone is placed at the bottom |
| Keep a dated record of your own results to use in the review conversation | Wait for the annual meeting to learn how you are doing |
| Ask for feedback in writing if your manager is rushed, as many meetings are brief | Take a brief, generic meeting as proof that nothing is wrong |
| If you dispute a rating, prepare evidence and a plan B at the same time | Escalate before checking who in the chain has already approved the score |

### In Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Agree measurable goals in writing at the start of the cycle, because unclear goals are a leading source of disagreement | Accept vague objectives and hope the review is fair |
| Ask for a mid-year check-in, since many employees say daily feedback is thin | Rely on the annual review as your only information |
| Expect a score and prepare to discuss it calmly, as most staff disagree with at least part of it | Argue the number in the meeting without supporting figures |
| Ask what the rating means for bonus and promotion before the meeting | Assume a high rating guarantees a high bonus |
| Treat office conversations as potentially reaching your boss | Confide in a colleague what you would not say to your supervisor |

## Singapore

According to The Peak, most Singapore workplaces still use an annual or semi-annual appraisal. Employees fill in a self-grading form, managers compare it with their own assessment, and managers then rank staff, often on a bell curve, before HR finalises the result. The process can take weeks or months, and the feedback meeting at the end is frequently brief or generic because managers are rushed. EngageRocket's chief executive told the magazine that managers can spend up to 210 hours a year on performance management, about a tenth of their working time. Critics quoted in the piece describe the curve as rewarding relative rather than absolute performance, so that in a team of strong performers someone still lands at the bottom.

The survey data suggests the culture is less brittle than the machinery, though the Randstad figures are undated on the page I read and should be treated as indicative. In Randstad's Singapore survey, 72 percent of respondents said they were comfortable giving and receiving feedback from their managers, and 49 percent said their employers use real-time feedback to track productivity. Yet 26 percent still felt uncomfortable during reviews, 88 percent regarded the review as chiefly a session where the manager gives them feedback, and 15 percent said they had never been asked for feedback during an appraisal. Only 45 percent said their employer offered training on giving and receiving feedback. Some firms have moved on: The Peak reports that Deloitte Singapore dropped the bell curve for regular check-ins and quarterly snapshots, and Adobe Asia Pacific abolished rankings, with voluntary attrition falling afterwards.

For the individual employee, the consequence is that the rating is the product of a ranking, and the ranking is the product of a calibration meeting at which you are not present. A bad grade can follow you. A civil-service employee on Reddit's r/askSingapore, as reported by The Independent SG, asked whether to dispute a C- rating issued by a boss who had joined only months earlier. Commenters were sympathetic but mostly advised looking for another job, warning that an appeal could invite retaliation. That is anecdote, not data, but it fits the structural picture: disputes are possible, and the advice for using them is cautious.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's problem is less the existence of the review than its reception. A Morgan McKinley white paper, reported by Human Resources Online in July 2018 from a September 2017 survey of 353 respondents, found that 78 percent of employees felt they were not reviewed accurately, 66 percent disagreed with their manager's feedback, and 65 percent found the annual review demotivating. On the other side of the desk, 72 percent of managers reported disagreement over feedback and scores. The same research found 55 percent of employees dissatisfied with the amount of daily feedback they receive. The research also found that employees consider annual reviews too infrequent to solve problems in time and prone to weighing recent performance over the whole year, and that unclear goals and shifting direction were among the biggest complaints. These figures are several years old and the sample is modest, and I found no newer survey of comparable scope to say whether Hong Kong workers have since come round to the annual review.

Randstad's Workmonitor for Hong Kong, from the first quarter of 2019, gave a more upbeat picture. It found 65 percent of respondents comfortable giving and receiving feedback from managers, 74 percent working in an open environment where feedback can be shared at any time, and 54 percent offered training on giving and receiving it. Even so, 86 percent said the review is mainly a session where the manager gives feedback, and 14 percent lacked a chance to raise career aspirations or concerns. The South China Morning Post, drawing on a broader survey of 2,100 leaders and employees across regions, noted that a standardised approach is common at Hong Kong banks and insurers, and that 60 percent of respondents said rankings and ratings create a negative experience for everyone except those at the top. Yet 89 percent of employees still thought ratings should play some role in determining rewards.

The practical difficulty is that candour is not always on offer. A thread on chinese-forums.com, about mainland China and so an adjacent source, describes a foreigner receiving praise shortly before being taken off a project and struggling to get direct criticism even when asking for it. Replies suggested cultivating one trusted colleague, reading subtle hints, and asking the supervisor directly for a progress review. They also warned that anything told to a friend at work should be assumed to reach the wider office. Hong Kong is not mainland China, and the thread should not be read as a verdict on it, but the advice to ask formally and listen carefully transfers neatly.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights scores Singapore at 74 for power distance and 8 for uncertainty avoidance, against 68 and 29 for Hong Kong. Both cities are hierarchical and low on individualism, at 20 and 25, which explains why few employees volunteer a disagreement with a superior. The notable difference is that Singapore's very low uncertainty avoidance sits oddly beside its fondness for process, while Hong Kong's slightly higher figure sits comfortably with a culture that tolerates ambiguity and then complains about it.

Head to head, Singapore offers a more structured appraisal with a self-assessment and a calibration step, at the cost of the curve. Hong Kong offers a looser one, with a score that the surveys suggest many employees reject, and a bonus conversation that the SCMP notes workers want ratings to inform. In Singapore, the risk is being ranked by people who never discussed you with you. In Hong Kong, the risk is being scored by a manager with whom you disagree and having little to say about it afterwards. Neither city has a monopoly on candour, and both reward the employee who has documented their work well before anyone asks.

## Conclusion

If you want a predictable process, a written self-assessment and a manager obliged to place you somewhere on a scale, Singapore offers it, so find out early whether a curve applies. If you can tolerate a score you may disagree with, and are prepared to chase feedback rather than wait for it, Hong Kong will suit you. In either city, ask for written goals, a mid-year conversation and a clear statement of how the rating feeds pay, and do it before the annual meeting.

You will be told how you are doing eventually, usually on the day it is too late to change.
