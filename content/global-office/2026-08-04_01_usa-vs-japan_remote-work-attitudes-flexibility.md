# The Laptop That Stays Home: Remote Work in America vs. Japan

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An American engineer who negotiates a fully remote offer can expect to never see head office again except at the holiday party. A Japanese engineer who negotiates the same clause can expect to see head office again the following Monday, because roughly seven in ten Japanese companies have already stopped offering work-from-home entirely, and the ones that still do treat it as a courtesy rather than a right. The contract may read the same in both countries. The consequences of using it do not.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate remote or hybrid terms explicitly in writing before signing — verbal promises evaporate under new management | Assume "flexible" means the same thing at every company; 34% of US firms now mandate full-time office attendance |
| Keep your camera on and calendar visible during core hours — visibility substitutes for the desk-presence Japan gets from actual desks | Go dark for stretches during the day even if your output is fine; US managers still equate silence with slacking |
| Use a dedicated home office space if you can — many employers ask about it during onboarding for tax and equipment reasons | Expect a company-subsidized commute or transit stipend if you're remote; that benefit mostly vanishes with the desk |
| Read the return-to-office memo closely — RTO mandates in the US often arrive with 60–90 days' notice, not a grace period | Treat a hybrid policy as permanent; two-thirds of US firms still offer location flexibility, but the trend line is tightening |
| Build your professional network deliberately since you won't get hallway collisions | Assume remote work exempts you from being "on" for cross-timezone meetings just because you're not commuting |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly, before accepting an offer, how many days per week telework is actually practiced versus technically permitted — the gap is large | Assume a remote-friendly job posting means remote-friendly management; policy and practice diverge sharply here |
| Expect to be evaluated partly on presence, especially in your first year — trust is built by being seen, not just by deliverables | Be the first to leave, remote or not; the habit of not-leaving-before-the-boss persists even in hybrid teams |
| Treat any remote day as a scheduling privilege to be used judiciously, not a default | Skip the seat-warming culture entirely and expect no social cost; Tokyo Shoko Research found company remote adoption fell from 37.0% to 29.1% between late 2021 and 2025 |
| Use paid telework days around family obligations — this is the socially safest justification | Push back openly on an RTO mandate; the more effective route is quiet negotiation with your direct manager |
| Learn your company's chat-tool etiquette (Slack, Chatwork, LINE Works) since digital communication norms are still catching up to Western defaults | Expect IT and HR systems to be as digitized as a comparable US employer; lagging digitization is a widely cited barrier to telework here |

### 🇺🇸 United States

The American default has quietly shifted from "remote work as perk" to "remote work as baseline expectation," even as employers claw some of it back. In 2025, 35% of employed Americans did some or all of their work from home on a given workday, and among workers in remote-capable roles, 52% work hybrid and 26% are fully remote — only 22% are back at a desk five days a week, according to workforce tracking cited across multiple 2026 labor surveys. Two-thirds of US firms still offer some form of location flexibility. The other third has drawn a line, and 55% of job seekers now rank hybrid arrangements as their top preference when comparing offers — meaning flexibility has become a recruiting lever as much as an operational choice.

What this produces on the ground is a patchwork. Two engineers at the same company, on the same team, can have different working arrangements depending on when they were hired and how loudly their manager complained about empty desks. The American approach treats flexibility as a negotiated individual entitlement rather than a national norm, which means the burden of securing it falls on the employee — in the interview, in the offer letter, and again every time a new VP decides open floor plans build culture.

### 🇯🇵 Japan

Japan's telework story runs in the opposite direction. Pre-pandemic, only about 9% of the Japanese workforce had ever teleworked, compared with roughly a third of American workers, and the pandemic briefly erased decades of stigma around it. That window is closing. Tokyo Shoko Research's tracking shows the share of companies offering work-from-home fell from 37.0% in October 2021 to 29.1% more recently, and separate reporting from Nippon.com found over 70% of Japanese companies are no longer implementing remote work in any form. Only 68% of Japanese employers say remote work is important to retaining staff — a notably lower figure than global benchmarks, suggesting many companies see it as optional goodwill rather than competitive necessity.

Yet employee appetite hasn't followed the retreat. Surveys find 61.2% of working professionals still prefer a role that includes some remote option, even though only about a quarter are currently exercising it. The result is a familiar mismatch: demand from the workforce, retrenchment from management, and a rigid business culture — one Hofstede Insights scores at 92 on uncertainty avoidance versus the US score of 46 — that treats deviation from established office ritual as a risk rather than an efficiency gain.

### The Reckoning

The two countries are not just at different points on the same curve; they're solving different problems. American remote work retrenchment is about control and culture-building — executives worried that dispersed teams erode cohesion and are willing to spend political capital forcing people back. Japanese remote work retrenchment is about something closer to trust architecture — an entire management style built on visible diligence, where seniority and reliability are still partly assessed by whether you were at your desk when the boss walked by. The US problem is philosophical. The Japan problem is structural.

The irony is that Japan's workforce is, by the numbers, more receptive to remote work than its employers are willing to admit, while America's workforce increasingly has to fight for what was briefly standard. A newcomer moving from the US to Japan should expect to lose flexibility they've come to take for granted. A newcomer moving from Japan to the US should expect that flexibility exists — but only until the next return-to-office memo lands in their inbox.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/japanlife — One American transplant working for a Japanese firm described being technically approved for two remote days a week but never actually using them after realizing his manager quietly tracked who showed up versus who didn't ahead of promotion cycles.*

> *r/expats — A contributor who moved from a fully remote US role to a Tokyo office job said the hardest adjustment wasn't the commute itself but relearning how to structure a day around being watched rather than being measured by output.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing offers in Tokyo and San Francisco noted that the US role paid more but came with an unwritten expectation of being reachable evenings and weekends, while the Japanese office role paid less but had genuinely enforced boundaries once you physically left the building.*

> *teamblind.com — An engineer who transferred internally from Google's Mountain View campus to its Tokyo office wrote that the remote-work language in the offer letter was identical to the US version, but in practice nobody on the Tokyo team used it, and being the one person working from home made him conspicuous in a way that felt professionally costly.*

> *Quora — A respondent addressing whether Japan or the US is more overworked argued that Japanese employees often work longer logged hours but report lower daily stress than Americans, who work fewer hours on average but face more unpredictable, always-on expectations tied to email and Slack.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility is the deciding factor in your move, look past the job posting and ask a blunt question in the interview: on an average week, how many people on this specific team actually work from home, not how many are allowed to. In the US, the answer will tell you about the manager. In Japan, it will tell you about the company's actual culture versus its stated one — and the gap between those two things is currently the widest labor-market variable either country has to offer. My honest advice, over drinks: negotiate the remote clause everywhere, but only believe it in the country where people actually use it without flinching.
