# Serbia Assigns the New Hire a Mentor, Croatia Assigns Them a Form

🇷🇸 Serbia · 🇭🇷 Croatia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two neighbouring countries, one shared language family, and a nearly identical legal ceiling on probation: six months in both. Yet the first week of a new job in Belgrade and the first week in Zagreb are different experiences, and the difference is one of philosophy. Serbian employers, when they do anything deliberate at all, tend to attach a person to the newcomer. Croatian employers tend to attach a process. Anyone relocating for work should decide, before signing, which of these they would rather be rescued by.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Serbia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask in the first week who your mentor is, by name, because mentoring is common but rarely written down | Assume a formal induction programme exists; some employers openly run none for new hires |
| Ask for the probation assessment criteria in writing, since the law prescribes no method | Treat a quiet end to probation as a pass; the employer must issue a written, reasoned decision |
| Know that probation is capped at six months and either side owes at least five working days' notice | Expect your line manager to escalate everything; many HR decisions are made by the line itself |
| Use the structured junior programmes at larger IT firms, which pair you with a mentor and a project | Wait to be taught; mentors expect you to arrive with questions and to work out answers |
| Ask foreign-hire support questions up front: language classes, housing help, paperwork | Assume your employer will volunteer any of it unprompted |
| Treat the mentor as a colleague with a day job, and book their time | Interpret a slow reply as indifference |

### Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist on a signed contract in Croatian before your first working day, and get it translated | Start work on a handshake and a promise to send the paperwork later |
| Confirm that pension and health insurance registration is done before day one | Assume late registration is your problem; it is a common employer violation |
| Complete the mandatory safety training before you touch any actual duties | Skip the safety briefing because it looks like a formality |
| Ask for a named mentor and a development plan, because neither arrives by default | Assume your direct superior is a formal mentor, even if informally they are the one you will learn from |
| Give seven days' written notice if you leave during probation, as the employer must do | Quit mid-probation by message and consider the matter closed |
| Ask what training budget exists; availability varies sharply by employer size | Assume the training that large firms offer is also offered by small ones |

## Serbia: Mentors Everywhere, Manuals Nowhere

The most useful number on Serbian workplaces is not about onboarding at all. According to the Cranet 2022 survey, conducted by the University of Novi Sad in 2021 and 2022 across 106 Serbian organisations employing about 103,000 people, mentoring or on-the-job training is used to a significant degree in nearly half of them. Employees spend about ten days a year in training, a little over eleven for managers and professionals, and nearly all of the organisations have a dedicated HR department. The same report finds that in roughly four in five organisations, line managers make the key HR decisions, alone or in consultation. In practice, that means your onboarding is whatever your direct manager and their goodwill can manage on a Tuesday.

The report offers no findings on formal induction, which is itself informative. Serbia's own business press is consistent on the point. The CEO of a Serbian outsourcing firm, interviewed in 2020, said plainly that recruitment ends when the contract is signed and that his company has no separate programmes for new hires, though it rewards long-term loyalty. Juniors, he added, are easier to guide because there is a path to show them. That remark is accurate and quietly describes the whole system: a path, a guide, and no document.

The legal frame is firm enough. According to a July 2025 note from Zunic Law, probation in Serbia may last up to six months, either party owes at least five working days' notice, and an employer ending probation must give reasons and evidence. The law does not prescribe how performance is assessed. The Serbian government's own Welcome to Serbia portal, aimed at employers hiring from abroad, recommends the humane version: a mentor or colleague for housing and paperwork questions, language lessons, and regular feedback. It is a recommendation, which in an onboarding context is the weakest form of rule.

Where mentoring does happen, the better practitioners describe it as an exercise in restraint. A Belgrade technical lead at the software firm Adacta, interviewed in January 2022, said he avoids dictating each step and prefers open dialogue until the team reaches a solution together, while urging newcomers to ask for help the moment they are stuck. This is admirable and also unscheduled. A mentor who works by conversation is available exactly as often as their calendar lets them be, and nothing in the Cranet data suggests anyone audits that calendar.

## Croatia: A Process, a Superior and a Suggestion Box

Croatia leads with paperwork, and says so without embarrassment. According to a Qureos hiring guide updated in July 2026, the contract must be written in Croatian and signed before work begins, the employee must be registered for pension and health insurance before the first day, and safety training is mandatory before any duties are performed. Legal onboarding takes one to three days if prepared; full integration usually takes thirty to ninety. Probation is capped at six months and, according to RemotePeople, ending it requires seven days' written notice from either side. The guide discusses the hiring manager's role in great detail and formal mentoring not at all.

What follows the paperwork is a training culture that depends heavily on the size of the employer. The Croatian Bureau of Statistics reports that in 2020, 48.2 percent of enterprises provided some form of vocational training, rising to 84.6 percent of large firms and 74.7 percent in information, communication and finance, but only 43.2 percent of small firms. A 2025 survey by Alma Career and Paylab found 48 percent of Croatian respondents had taken employer-funded training in the previous six months, and only 36 percent said their daily tasks helped their professional development. Meanwhile SELECTIO Group's survey of 50,000 employees, reported by AmCham Croatia in November 2024, found staff most satisfied with relationships with colleagues and supervisors and least satisfied with development opportunities, communication and pay. The affection is real. The pathway is thin.

The cost of that thinness shows up in retention. Croatia Week, citing a 2025 MojPosao survey reported by RTL Danas, said a large majority of workers were open to changing jobs within six months, naming low pay, a lack of professional development and poor relationships with management as the main grievances, while the colleagues who kept people in place were good ones. Alma Career's September 2025 figures put the share of Croatians seriously considering a move at 37 percent. An employer that cannot explain how a new hire grows should not be astonished when the new hire explores the market.

A 2022 academic case study of two Croatian firms is instructive. One gave trainees a mentor and an administrative buddy, with feedback twice a week. The other mentioned that there are no formally assigned mentors, because, in the words of one manager, a person's superior is their mentor. That is efficient, and it is also how a single manager comes to be your boss, trainer, evaluator and only friend in the building.

## The Reckoning

On power distance and uncertainty avoidance, the two countries score as neighbours, with Serbia more so. Hofstede Insights' original figures, as reproduced in a 2014 paper by Podrug, Filipovic and Stancic because the Hofstede site itself would not load, put power distance at 86 for Serbia and 73 for Croatia, and uncertainty avoidance at 92 and 80. Those are high numbers, and they predict the observed behaviour: hierarchy decides, and rules are either followed strictly or avoided quietly. Croatia has converted its caution into a checklist. Serbia has converted it into a mentor, partly because the checklist is optional.

The head-to-head is therefore not warm versus cold. Croatia gives the better floor: the legal onboarding is clearer, the safety and registration steps are documented, and the notice period is simple. Serbia gives the higher ceiling at employers that care, notably the larger IT firms whose junior programmes pair participants with named mentors for a few months of paid, realistic project work. Croatia's weak point is development; Serbia's is consistency. Pick your risk.

One caution on the Hofstede figures: the same 2014 paper ran its own 2012 survey, on a small sample of students and young employees, and found both countries drifting towards individualism, with power distance falling but the ranking unchanged. Treat the scores as a direction of travel and not as a verdict. The newcomer's actual manager will outweigh any national index by a comfortable margin.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Academic case study, Croatian firm, paraphrased experience: A manager at a small advisory firm said new trainees get both a mentor and a buddy, with feedback twice a week after each task, which interviewees called the fastest way to learn. Outside the trainee programme, though, mentoring is informal and unwritten.*

> *Academic case study, second Croatian firm, paraphrased experience: A social enterprise folds its introduction into recruitment itself, observing candidates on real processes and in pair work before hiring, and then supports staff with individual change plans. There was no separate induction programme to point at.*

> *HTEC company blog, paraphrased experience: Participants in a three-month junior programme in Serbia said mentors were patient, reviewed their code and pushed them to find answers themselves. They noted mentors have other duties, but help arrived quickly whenever they asked.*

> *Engineering Serbia interview, paraphrased experience: A developer who joined as an intern with no programming background credits strong teams and mentors for his first years, was later promoted and now mentors others, which he finds the most rewarding part of the job.*

> *FishingBooker content specialist in Belgrade, paraphrased experience: An employee of a Belgrade company described an international, close-knit team that works in English and promotes from within, but did not describe any formal onboarding steps.*

## Conclusion

If you are a junior engineer, Serbia's best employers are an excellent bet: you will likely get a named mentor, paid project work and tolerance for questions. If you are a mid-career hire who values clear process, Croatia's legal scaffolding is more predictable, although you will need to ask for the development plan in writing, because the survey data suggest nobody will volunteer it.

In either country, the practical advice is the same. Ask who your mentor is before you accept, ask for the probation criteria in writing, and treat the answer as data about the company. A manager who hesitates on the first question has already handed you your onboarding.
