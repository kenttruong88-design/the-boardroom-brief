**Shake Every Hand or Fail Silently: Onboarding in France vs. Canada**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In France, you will be judged, gently but permanently, by whether you did the morning round of handshakes on day one. In Canada, you'll likely be handed a "buddy," a Slack channel, and a vague sense that someone, somewhere, is responsible for your integration — though Gallup finds that 88% of employees globally don't think their employer's onboarding is actually any good. One country over-formalizes the ritual and under-delivers the substance; the other does the reverse.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shake hands (or *la bise*, once you know people) with colleagues each morning | Walk straight to your desk without greeting the room |
| Dress deliberately — appearance reads as competence | Assume "casual Friday" is a universal office norm |
| Read your contract's trial period (*période d'essai*) and notice terms closely | Expect a two-week U.S.-style exit if you resign later |
| Let relationships deepen slowly through lunch and shared debate | Push for fast-tracked promotion in your first year |
| Use *vous* and formal openings in written communication until invited otherwise | Default to first-name, casual email tone with senior colleagues |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Actively use your onboarding buddy — ask the "silly" questions | Assume the buddy program means structured, guaranteed mentorship |
| Build your own network beyond your immediate team | Wait for the org chart to hand you relationships |
| Ask directly for feedback in the first 90 days | Assume silence means things are going well |
| Treat the first quarter as a trial for both sides | Expect job security comparable to a French CDI |
| Say so if onboarding feels ad hoc — it often is, even at big employers | Suffer through a sink-or-swim ramp-up without flagging it |

---

France structures the first weeks around form. A new hire typically works through a 90-day arc — often checkpointed at 30, 60, and 90 days — inside a *période d'essai* that functions as a mutual trial run; showing up for the end of it is enough of an event that, as one American who worked in Paris recounted, colleagues congratulated her simply for making it through, the closest the French system gets to American at-will employment. The formality extends past paperwork: French firms lean on mentorship and structured check-ins for cultural integration, and TotalEnergies has reported that follow-up-heavy onboarding programs cut early turnover by roughly 25%. Underneath the ritual is a genuinely low-turnover system — the French Labour Code guarantees five weeks of paid leave and, thanks to the "right to disconnect" law, actual protection from after-hours email, which is part of why the pace, once you're in, tends to be steady rather than frantic.

Canada structures the first weeks around goodwill and hopes it's enough. Employers increasingly pair new hires with a peer buddy — someone distinct from the manager or HR, focused on the human, unwritten-rules side of the job rather than performance — and the data backs the instinct: employees with a buddy system report being 12% more productive, and buddy programs are linked to a 52% bump in retention, according to workplace research compiled by HR platforms drawing on the model. But formal mentorship remains patchy. Statistics Canada counts more than 4.4 million workers aged 55 or older, a looming knowledge-transfer cliff that most organizations aren't structured to catch, and Mentor Canada finds that only about 16% of Canadian adults had access to formal mentoring in their youth — suggesting the instinct to mentor is cultural, but the infrastructure to do it consistently at work is not.

The Reckoning: France front-loads structure and legal weight — the trial period, the paperwork, the five-week vacation guarantee — then relaxes once you're proven in, at which point relationships (not process) carry you. Canada front-loads warmth — the buddy, the Slack welcome, the casual "reach out anytime" — but the substance is discretionary, team-dependent, and only as good as whoever happens to be assigned to you. Hofstede Insights scores France around 68 on power distance versus Canada's 39, meaning the French system's formality is coming from a genuine hierarchy that expects you to earn your way in, while Canada's informality assumes a flatter structure that, on paper, needs less earning — except someone still has to do the mentoring, and nobody's contractually on the hook for it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *thelocal.fr — A guide aimed at foreign workers described the ritual of shaking every colleague's hand each morning as non-negotiable office etiquette, and noted that skipping it, even inadvertently, on day one, reads as cold rather than simply unfamiliar with the custom.*

> *Quora — Someone describing French office life recalled that literally everyone began the day by shaking hands around the room, and framed the broader culture as one where advancement is collective rather than individually staged — a contrast to the self-promotion new arrivals sometimes bring with them.*

> *Quora — A Canadian who relocated to France for work described daily life there as noticeably less stressful once settled, but warned that the immigration paperwork was a long, bureaucratic slog and that going in without solid French would make the first months considerably harder.*

> *teamblind.com — A commenter discussing onboarding at large Canadian tech employers noted that despite formal buddy and mentorship programs being advertised, actual training was often ad hoc and entirely dependent on which team a new hire landed on — with some describing new hires left to fail quietly without anyone actively checking in.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway: in France, invest in the ritual — the handshakes, the dress, the patience through the trial period — because the system rewards people who visibly respect its structure, and once you're in, you're genuinely in. In Canada, invest in yourself — don't assume the buddy program is doing the work of a real mentor, and ask directly and often for the feedback and access that the informal culture implies but doesn't guarantee. If you want a friend's honest read: France makes you prove you belong before it relaxes, Canada tells you that you already belong and then quietly leaves you to figure out what that means.
