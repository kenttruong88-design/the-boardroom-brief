# The Suit and the Sweater: Dress Codes in Brazil and Sweden

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In São Paulo, a person of higher status is culturally expected to visibly dress the part — appearance functions as a quiet but real signal of seriousness and standing. In Stockholm, the opposite instinct governs: Jante's Law, the informal Nordic social code, actively discourages anyone from dressing or acting as though they're better than anyone else. Both countries care enormously about what your clothes communicate. They've just agreed on opposite messages.

| Ask directly or simply observe colleagues in your first week if the dress code isn't obvious from the job posting | Show up underdressed to a client-facing meeting in traditional sectors like banking or law — appearance is read as a signal of seriousness there |
| Expect engineering and tech firms to sit in a more relaxed middle ground — jeans and polo shirts are common | Assume Rio and São Paulo share identical norms with smaller or more informal Brazilian cities; major-city business hubs skew notably more formal |
| Maintain grooming and polish even in casual-dress industries — the cultural emphasis on appearance persists even where the dress code itself is relaxed | Confuse "casual" with "careless" — Brazilian workplace casual still tends to be more put-together than the Scandinavian equivalent |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to "power casual" — neutral, understated, well-made basics in black, navy, grey, or beige rather than flashy or logo-heavy pieces | Wear visibly luxury-branded or ostentatious clothing to signal seniority; Jante's Law actively discourages standing out through display of status |
| Save formal suits and ties for genuinely high-stakes meetings or events — daily office wear leans toward dress pants, blouses, and closed-toe shoes rather than full formalwear | Assume "casual" Swedish dress code means sloppy; polish and quality still matter, just expressed through understatement rather than formality |

### 🇧🇷 Brazil

Brazilian workplace dress code is sharply sector-dependent, but the underlying cultural logic is consistent: appearance functions as a genuine, non-trivial professional signal. Traditional industries — banking, law, and finance especially — expect formal suits and ties for men and formal business attire for women, particularly in major hubs like São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, according to guidance from Safeguard Global and The Brazil Business. Creative sectors run in the opposite direction: someone in a full suit at an advertising agency or publishing house would, per multiple business etiquette guides, genuinely feel and look out of place.

What ties the whole system together, more than any specific dress code, is a broader cultural expectation that people of higher status visibly dress the part — a norm cited across several Brazilian business-etiquette resources. This isn't unique to the office; it reflects a wider social pattern in which appearance signals seriousness and standing well beyond any single meeting. Hofstede Insights data situates Brazil at meaningfully higher power distance than most Nordic or Northern European comparators, and the dress culture reflects that hierarchy directly — clothing is one of the more visible ways status gets communicated day to day.

### 🇸🇪 Sweden

Swedish workplace dress code runs on almost the opposite cultural engine. Business etiquette guides describe the everyday norm as "power casual" — polished but deliberately understated, with neutral colors dominating over flashy branding or luxury signaling, per guidance from CE Sweden and Commisceo Global. The driving cultural force isn't indifference to appearance, it's Jante's Law, the broader Scandinavian social code holding that no one should present themselves as better than anyone else — a norm that shows up as directly in wardrobe choices as it does in Swedish workplace communication style more generally.

Sweden scores 5 on Hofstede's masculinity dimension — the lowest of any country measured, reflecting a culture oriented toward consensus, modesty, and work-life balance rather than competitive display. Formal attire hasn't disappeared entirely — high-stakes external meetings and formal events still call for suits and tailored dresses — but the daily default sits well below what a Brazilian professional in an equivalent industry would consider baseline. The Swedish concept of "lagom" — balance, moderation, just enough — extends directly into the wardrobe: showing up in something too formal for the room can read as more socially awkward than showing up underdressed.

### The Reckoning

Brazil's dress code communicates hierarchy; Sweden's dress code actively suppresses it. In Brazil, dressing up is how you signal that you take a role, a meeting, or your own career seriously. In Sweden, dressing up unprompted can signal the opposite — an unfamiliarity with the local instinct to downplay status rather than display it. Neither approach is really about clothes; both are downstream of much deeper cultural assumptions about what hierarchy is for and how visible it should be allowed to become.

The trap runs predictably in both directions. A Brazilian professional transplanted to Stockholm who shows up in a sharp suit to an internal meeting risks reading as trying too hard, or worse, as subtly claiming status the culture actively discourages. A Swedish professional transplanted to São Paulo who shows up in jeans to a client meeting in a traditional sector risks reading as unserious about the relationship, regardless of the quality of the actual work. Neither read is really about fashion sense — both are about whether you understood the room.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — A finance professional comparing banking dress codes across markets described their Brazilian office as still requiring a full suit daily, contrasting it with peers at Nordic banks who mentioned dress shirts without ties being standard even for client-facing roles.*

> *Quora — Someone addressing whether skipping a suit reads as unserious in a business meeting explained that in Brazil the answer depends heavily on sector, but in traditional industries the honest answer is yes — appearance genuinely factors into how seriously colleagues and clients take you.*

> *r/expats — A Scandinavian professional who relocated to São Paulo for work described the early culture shock of being visibly the most casually dressed person in every client meeting, and how quickly upgrading her wardrobe changed how quickly colleagues took her proposals seriously.*

> *Quora — A contributor discussing Brazilian business etiquette noted that dressing well in Brazil isn't really about the meeting itself, it's a broader social signal that persists outside the office — showing up underdressed to a business dinner can land as poorly as showing up underdressed to the office itself.*

> *teamblind.com — A worker who moved from a Brazilian office to a Swedish one described the adjustment to "power casual" as harder than expected, noting it took months to stop over-dressing for ordinary internal meetings before realizing his sharper suits were quietly read as slightly try-hard by Swedish colleagues.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Brazil, research your specific sector before you pack — a banking job and an advertising job in the same city call for nearly opposite wardrobes, and appearance carries more real professional weight here than in most Northern European markets. If you're heading to Sweden, resist the instinct to dress up for safety's sake; understated, well-made basics will read as more competent than a sharp suit in most everyday settings. My honest advice: in Brazil, dress for the job you want; in Sweden, dress like you already have it and don't need to prove it.
