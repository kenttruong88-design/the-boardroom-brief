# Norway Will Make You Build a Deck With Strangers. Vietnam Will Make You Family By Lunch.

**🇳🇴 Norway · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Vietnam, a new colleague is folded into the office "gia đình" — family — often within the first week, invited to Tet celebrations, fed constantly, and asked personal questions that would raise eyebrows in most Western offices. In Norway, a new colleague may be handed a pair of work gloves and invited to a dugnad — unpaid, communal labor, typically involving painting a fence or building a deck on a Saturday — and this, not a dinner invitation, is the actual bonding ritual. Both cultures believe deeply in group cohesion. Neither believes cohesion is built by talking about it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the dugnad (communal work day) even if you don't fully understand what it's for | Skip it — non-participation is remembered longer than any single missed meeting |
| Volunteer for unglamorous, unpaid group tasks without being asked | Talk up your accomplishments or expertise unprompted — janteloven punishes visible self-promotion |
| Treat the hyttetur (cabin weekend) invitation, if it comes, as a genuine bonding milestone | Expect a coffee invite to lead quickly to real friendship — cultural adjustment here is a multi-year project |
| Be patient and consistent; trust accrues through repeated small interactions, not charm | Assume friendliness at work equals being invited into someone's private life |
| Build your primary social circle partly through hobbies or sports clubs, not just the office | Rely solely on the workplace for social integration — InterNations data says this rarely works alone |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept food when offered, repeatedly — sharing meals is the primary bonding currency | Refuse a colleague's food offer outright; a polite partial acceptance is better than a flat no |
| Participate fully in after-work dinners and Tet-season office events | Treat these outings as optional extras — they are, functionally, part of the job |
| Learn and use colleagues' family details — asking about someone's parents or children is warm, not intrusive | Bring an overly individualistic frame to team wins; collective credit is the norm |
| Expect quick emotional warmth and closeness with the team | Mistake fast warmth for equal footing with management — hierarchy still governs formal interactions |
| Give small gifts or lucky money during Tet if you're in a position to reciprocate | Skip contributing to group gifts or celebrations for a boss's or colleague's milestone |

---

Norway's social contract runs on janteloven, the informal "Law of Jante" that discourages standing out and rewards quiet, collective competence — a colleague who talks up their own achievements or positions themselves as the smartest person in the room typically loses trust rather than gains it. Bonding, accordingly, happens through shared, humble labor: the dugnad, a tradition of unpaid communal work, remains one of the most reliable ways for a newcomer to actually integrate, according to relocation guides that track expat adjustment in Norway. The results of this reserve are stark. InterNations' 2026 Expat Insider survey ranked Norway dead last out of 31 countries overall — its worst result yet — with just a quarter of expats satisfied with their social life, against 54 percent globally, and more than half reporting that most of their friends and acquaintances were fellow expats rather than Norwegians. The Local Norway has reported this as a persistent, multi-year pattern, not a one-off. None of this means Norwegians are unfriendly — it means bonding is slow, understated, and built through repetition rather than performance.

Vietnam inverts nearly every one of those defaults. The OECD's Social Cohesion Policy Review of Viet Nam notes the country's rapid economic growth has been accompanied by deliberate efforts to preserve communal social structures even as urbanization accelerates. In practice, this shows up at the office level as what HR researchers describe as a "family-like workplace," where colleagues form close, almost kin-like attachments quickly, reinforced by frequent after-work dinners, shared meals, and company-wide celebrations of Tet, Vietnam's most significant holiday. Collective rewards — celebrating team wins over individual ones — are standard practice, consistent with the group-oriented end of Hofstede's individualism scale, where Vietnam scores notably lower than Norway. The bonding is fast, physical, and food-centered, and it starts almost immediately rather than after years of quiet trust-building.

The reckoning: both countries privilege the group over the individual, but they've arrived at opposite bonding mechanics. Norway treats emotional restraint as a form of respect and slow-built trust as the only kind worth having; showing enthusiasm too early can actually read as suspicious. Vietnam treats immediate warmth and shared meals as the foundation trust is built on top of, not a substitute for it. A Norwegian transplant in Hanoi may find the constant food-sharing and personal questions overwhelming at first; a Vietnamese transplant in Oslo may spend a year wondering why nobody has invited them anywhere, only to realize the invitation was a work glove and a paint roller all along.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/Norway — A foreign resident of 17 years said the vast majority of their close friends were still other foreigners, and described being quietly frozen out by a Norwegian acquaintance who seemed to feel the relationship had moved faster than was socially appropriate — a correction they only understood as such well after the fact.*

> *Quora — Someone posting about integrating into Norwegian workplace culture noted that celebrating a personal achievement out loud at work got a noticeably cooler reception than expected, and that reframing the same win as a team effort changed how colleagues responded almost overnight.*

> *The Local Norway — A recurring complaint among foreign residents surveyed was loneliness specifically, with only a quarter reporting satisfaction with their social life — far below the global expat average — despite otherwise positive views of pay and quality of life.*

> *Internations — An American professional relocated to Hanoi described being invited to a Tet celebration with a colleague's extended family in her first month, and said the hardest adjustment wasn't the invitations themselves but learning to accept food a second and third time, since a single polite refusal was read as genuine disinterest.*

> *r/expats — A Vietnamese engineer who transferred to a Norwegian tech office said the biggest shock wasn't the cold weather but the silence: no one asked about his family for months, and he eventually learned that the real invitation to belong came disguised as a request to help build a colleague's garden shed.*

### Conclusion

If you need to feel embraced quickly, Vietnam will deliver that within weeks, food included, though the depth behind that warmth still takes time to earn on top of it. If you can tolerate feeling like an outsider for a year or more in exchange for something durable and unperformed, Norway eventually offers real cohesion — it just arrives disguised as manual labor. The practical advice is almost identical despite the opposite tempo: show up, don't self-promote, and let repetition do the work invitations alone won't. If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: go to Vietnam if you want to feel like family by Friday; go to Norway if you're willing to spend a weekend painting someone's fence to eventually earn the right to be quietly, permanently trusted.
