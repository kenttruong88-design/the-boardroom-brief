# Vietnam Bonds Over the Dinner Table. Indonesia Bonds Over the WhatsApp Group Called "WorkFam."

**🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇮🇩 Indonesia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Englishman who spent time in a Vietnamese office described feeling "a bit odd and somewhat lonely and isolated," even as an American colleague nearby marveled that Vietnamese staff "rarely complain about their workload" and treat sleep, not leisure, as the reward for finishing it. Six hundred miles south, an Indonesian office manager was quietly instructed by HR to stop publicly praising a star developer in stand-up meetings — the room, he wrote, "went silent," because individual recognition can embarrass rather than motivate. Both countries build genuinely strong office bonds. They just consider almost opposite behaviors the bonding ritual.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to *nhậu* (group drinking/eating sessions) once they start coming — they're a real relationship signal | Don't decline repeatedly without explanation; it reads as disengagement from the team, not just from alcohol |
| Respect hierarchy even in informal settings — elders and seniors are still deferred to at the dinner table | Don't assume a private-room restaurant booking is just a nice touch; it signals cultural respect deliberately |
| Expect gift-giving rituals around Lunar New Year and Mid-Autumn Festival — high-end gifts for clients, mooncakes for internal teams | Don't skip Vietnamese Women's Day recognition (a rose, a team lunch) — it's an established, expected ritual, not optional flourish |
| Let the bill-splitting norm play out; group contribution after one person fronts payment is standard | Don't insist on paying your own way rigidly — the back-and-forth over the bill is itself a small relationship ritual |
| Treat long hours and modest complaint culture as the norm, not a personal failing if you're struggling to match it | Don't publicly vent about workload the way you might elsewhere — it can read as poor attitude rather than honesty |

#### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the "WorkFam" WhatsApp group and birthday potlucks — these aren't fringe extras, they're core belonging signals | Don't praise individual performance loudly in a group setting; it can embarrass rather than motivate |
| Let someone else grab the lunch bill sometimes — the boss paying, then a colleague "fighting" to pay next round, is a genuine display of care | Don't insist on splitting every bill evenly; the competitive generosity is part of the ritual, not inefficiency |
| Mobilize with colleagues under pressure — spontaneous mutual help (gotong royong) during deadlines is a celebrated norm | Don't route serious feedback through formal channels only; a lot of real communication happens over lunch invites and private messages |
| Recognize achievement privately when possible — a quiet word or DM lands better than a public callout | Don't assume Western-style public kudos translates directly; check the room's reaction before repeating it |
| Expect a genuine "family atmosphere" framing (*kekeluargaan*) at many companies | Don't treat the family framing as purely rhetorical — it shapes real obligations around attendance and participation |

### Vietnam: The Dinner Table Is Where Trust Gets Made

Vietnamese office bonding runs through food, drink, and hierarchy simultaneously, and *nhậu* — the informal shared eating-and-drinking gathering — sits at the center of it. Per VLStudies' breakdown, these sessions include "relatives, neighbors, friends or colleagues" and revolve around "eating, drinking, talking, laughing, and sometimes playing games together," with "respect for hierarchy and elders" holding even in the loosest social settings. It isn't purely social lubrication; per Vietcetera's business-etiquette guide, once a professional relationship is established, "one can expect to be invited to regular after-work drinking sessions" — participation functions as an informal test of investment in the relationship, alcohol moderation and all.

The ritual calendar reinforces this: Lunar New Year brings "high-end gifts such as wine, imported snacks, and fruits" for clients, the Mid-Autumn Festival brings mooncakes for internal teams, and Vietnamese Women's Day brings roses and office lunches for female staff — recurring, expected touchpoints that keep the relationship warm between the bigger dinners. One Englishman's account of failing to adapt is instructive precisely because it's a failure account: he found the office "too dull and cold" and felt isolated, which — read against everything above — suggests he wasn't declining the wrong things so much as never getting pulled into the dinner-table rhythm that actually carries Vietnamese office relationships. An American colleague's read on the same environment was nearly the opposite: he noted Vietnamese staff "rarely complain about their workload," a data point less about masochism and more about a culture where showing effort, not voicing friction, is the socially rewarded behavior.

### Indonesia: Harmony Is the Ritual, Not a Side Effect

Indonesian office bonding runs on a different operating principle entirely: *gotong royong*, the deep-rooted cultural norm of mutual assistance and collective effort, transplanted directly into the modern workplace. Per Saibumi's cultural analysis, this shows up as spontaneous, unprompted mobilization when a colleague is under deadline pressure — not a formal escalation process, just people showing up to help because that's what's done. Kaiwa's business-etiquette guide frames the broader posture as harmony-and-respect-first, a value system that shapes not just how help is offered but how feedback and praise are delivered too.

That last point produced the most vivid data point in this research: one Indonesian office manager, recounting his own learning curve in a first-person Medium essay, described publicly praising a developer during a stand-up — and watching the room go silent. HR's explanation afterward was direct: individual public recognition can embarrass rather than motivate in a culture built around group harmony. The same account documents the rituals that do work: team lunches where "the boss grabs the bill" and the next round becomes a genuine, affectionate contest over who pays; birthday potlucks; group selfies; WhatsApp groups literally named "WorkFam" — all expressions of *kekeluargaan*, the family-atmosphere framing that Indonesian companies lean into deliberately, not as HR branding but as an operating description of how the office is supposed to feel.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures build strong bonds through ritual and both explicitly subordinate the individual to the group — but they diverge sharply on where emotion is allowed to show. Vietnam channels bonding through hierarchy-respecting shared indulgence: eat, drink, defer to elders, repeat, and the relationship deepens through repetition and generosity at the table. Indonesia channels it through protected harmony: mutual aid, collective celebration, and a deliberate suppression of anything — including praise — that could single someone out and disrupt group cohesion. Hofstede's collectivism scores put both countries on the same broad side of the individualism/collectivism divide relative to Western Europe or North America, but the on-the-ground texture couldn't be more different: one country's warmest gesture is a toast in your honor; the other's is very deliberately not naming you in front of the room at all.

> *VnExpress International, same feature, second voice* — Zach, an American in the same feature, offered the contrasting read: "People here always work and rarely complain about their workload," observing that where Americans "consider work merely a part of their lives," his Vietnamese colleagues often "sleep in their free time because they are tired" — exhaustion worn less as complaint than as evidence of commitment.

> *Medium, Daffa Ghiffary Kusuma, "Indonesian work culture and management styles"* — The author's own stand-up-meeting misstep became the essay's central lesson: after publicly praising a developer, "the room went silent," and HR later explained that Western-style individual kudos can embarrass rather than motivate — a specific, concrete moment that no business-etiquette guide would have flagged in advance.

> *Same Medium essay, on team-lunch ritual* — The author's shorthand for the office lunch-bill ritual doubles as the clearest one-line summary of Indonesian workplace warmth in this research: "Boss grabs the bill. Next round, someone else will fight to pay. Let them, it's love" — a small, specific detail that captures more about *kekeluargaan* than any formal definition could.

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Vietnam, treat the dinner invitations as the real onboarding process — decline them and you risk the isolation James Brown described; accept them, defer appropriately to seniority at the table, and the hierarchy that looks rigid on an org chart will start to feel more like a family structure than a chain of command. If you're heading to Indonesia, hold your praise back a notch from instinct — save the individual callouts for private messages, lean into the group rituals (the lunch-bill contest, the WorkFam group chat, the spontaneous deadline rescue squad), and you'll find the harmony isn't stiffness, it's the actual bonding mechanism. The honest version I'd give a friend over a drink: in Hanoi, say yes to the next nhậu invite even if you're tired; in Jakarta, keep the compliment for later and buy the next round instead.
