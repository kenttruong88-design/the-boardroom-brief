**Iran Will Tell a Woman Which Jobs She Can't Have. Canada Will Just Pay Her Less For the One She's Got.**

🇮🇷 Iran · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Iran, women earn roughly half of Iran's university graduates yet make up only 14.9 percent of the workforce, held back by a labor code that doesn't ban gender preferences in hiring ads and a civil code that still lets a husband legally bar his wife from a job he dislikes. In Canada, women make 88 cents for every dollar a man earns — a gap that has narrowed by six cents since 1997, which is either real progress or a pace that suggests parity sometime in the 2080s, depending on your patience. Both countries will tell you they're working on it. Only one of them means working on the law.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect job ads to state a gender preference outright — one review found 60% of reviewed public-sector vacancy ads explicitly preferred men | Assume a woman's degree guarantees a matching job; one in three female bachelor's graduates is unemployed |
| Research maternity-leave practice before accepting a role; thousands of women on legally protected leave have reportedly still lost their jobs on return | Expect pay transparency to help — salary surveys found women earning roughly a quarter less overall and over a third less in management |
| Understand that a wife legally needs her husband's involvement for some employment and travel decisions under the civil code | Expect informal workplace advocacy to substitute for unions — assembly and union rights are themselves restricted |
| Note that women dominate certain sectors by design — nearly all reviewed secretarial job ads explicitly asked for women | Expect harassment policy to exist on paper or in practice; the law doesn't address it and many employers report no awareness of any policy |
| Expect dress-code enforcement to extend into the office itself, not just the street | Mistake high female university enrollment for workplace parity — the gap between the two is the whole story here |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about bonus and equity structures specifically, not just base salary — women in tech leadership get smaller bonuses and less equity even at equal levels | Assume a 22-cent gap is a tech problem alone; it narrows at the very top (VP) and widens again in the C-suite |
| Expect racialized and Indigenous women to face a materially larger gap than the national average — budget your advocacy accordingly if you manage a diverse team | Treat "the gap is narrowing" as the same thing as "the gap is closing soon" — it's moved six cents in roughly three decades |
| Push for transparency on leadership-level pay bands specifically; the gap is worst at the "leads and managers" level, not entry level | Assume formal legal protection means informal bias has vanished; well-being scores for women in tech still trail men's |
| Expect women to hold about a quarter of tech roles — ask directly about team composition if representation matters to you | Treat "equal pay" legislation as equal pay in practice; the gap "never hits zero" across any level, per industry's own survey data |
| Use the law as leverage, not reassurance — human rights and pay-equity statutes exist here and are enforceable, unlike in many peer markets | Expect every province or sector to move at the same pace; national averages mask real variation |

Iran's numbers describe a system built, in places quite explicitly, to keep women out of paid work rather than merely underpaid within it. Human Rights Watch's "It's a Men's Club" documented that women make up about half of university graduates but only 14.9 percent of the workforce, with unemployment at 20.7 percent — double the male rate — and a full third of women with bachelor's degrees unemployed. The Civil Code's Article 1117 allows a husband to bar his wife from a job he considers damaging to family values or his reputation; the Passport Law requires his permission for her to get a passport at all. A salary survey found women earning 23 percent less overall and 36 percent less in managerial roles. Hofstede Insights scores Iran's masculinity dimension at a moderate 40 and individualism at a low 38 — numbers that, read alone, suggest a fairly collectivist, not especially male-coded culture, which only underscores how much of the gender gap here is constructed by specific statute rather than by broad cultural values alone.

Canada's gap is smaller in magnitude and entirely legal in the sense that nothing in it is explicitly written into law — which makes it, in its way, a harder problem to point at. Statistics Canada found women earning 88 cents per dollar in 2025, up from 82 cents in 1997, with racialized women earning just 78 cents and Indigenous women 79 cents against the same benchmark. In tech leadership specifically, an Artemis Canada survey found a 22 percent gap before bonuses or equity, with the gap widest among leads and managers (27 percent) and narrowing, oddly, at the vice-president level (12 percent) before widening again in the C-suite (26 percent). Canada's Hofstede individualism score of 80 — among the highest in the world — theoretically predicts a culture where merit, not category, should determine pay. The data suggests merit is doing a lot of that work, but category is still quietly doing some of it too.

### The Reckoning

Here's the uncomfortable symmetry: Iran's barriers are explicit enough to document with a spreadsheet — a specific article number, a specific percentage of job ads — which makes them legible and, in theory, repealable by a single legislative act. Canada's gap survives because it isn't written down anywhere; it lives in bonus structures, equity grants, and “culture fit,” durable precisely because no single piece of it is illegal. An Iranian reformer knows exactly which words of the Civil Code to strike. A Canadian advocate is fighting a gap that “never hits zero” at any level of a company, with no clause to point to and no villain to subpoena.

That doesn't make the two problems equivalent — being legally barred from a job is categorically worse than being underpaid in one you can keep — but it should temper any assumption Canada has “solved” what Iran hasn't. It has solved the part a law could fix.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *IranWire — A nurse who relocated to Montreal described being actively misled before the move: immigration consultants "kept reassuring us that it was easy" to enter the Canadian job market, when in practice the credentialing process, the English-language bar, and the local job market were all far harder than advertised.*

> *IranWire — A radiologic technologist preparing to immigrate said his single biggest obstacle wasn't Canadian bureaucracy at all, but simply "getting my degree released from Iran" — a reminder that for many Iranian professionals, gender dynamics compound with institutional friction on both ends of the move.*

> *York University thesis (academic interview research) — A woman identified as Layla, a former radiologist in Iran, described being barred from clinical practice over her "ideology," pivoting into a university teaching post instead — only to eventually be barred from teaching too, a demotion-by-attrition that no formal termination notice ever quite captured.*

> *York University thesis (same research) — A woman identified as Shirin described the day-to-day texture of restricted autonomy in Iran plainly: "I was being told what to wear, and what to do" — a detail that lands differently once you clock that Iran's own Hofstede individualism score is lower than Canada's, but not dramatically so, suggesting the lived gap is wider than the cultural-dimension numbers alone would predict.*

### Conclusion

If you're an Iranian woman weighing a move to Canada, you're trading a system that may legally stop you from taking a job for one that will almost certainly let you take it and then quietly underpay you for it — worse in theory for some, far better in practice for most, which is why so many make the move anyway. If you're a Canadian employer hiring from this pool, understand you're often getting someone who left behind a credential, not just a country, and crediting it is the real act of equity on offer. The honest question for anyone moving between these systems isn't whether women can work here, but whether the system notices when they're paid less for it — both countries have an answer, and only one is proud of it.
