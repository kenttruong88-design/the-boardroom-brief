# Bulgaria Hands You a Laptop and a Colleague Who Started Last Year; Britain Hands You a Welcome Pack and Says Someone Will Be in Touch

🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A 2024 YouGov poll of 1,016 UK HR professionals, commissioned by the CIPD and Omni RMS, found that 27 percent of employers had a new starter fail to turn up on day one, and 41 percent had new hires resign within their first 12 weeks. A person who has accepted a job in Britain, in other words, has about even odds of vanishing before the induction. Bulgaria offers an alternative model, which is that you turn up, nobody has a plan, and the person who started a year before you quietly becomes your mentor. Neither system is designed. Both work, to a degree.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find your own buddy, ideally a colleague who started a year earlier | Wait for a formal mentorship programme unless you work for a multinational |
| Expect to learn by doing | Assume a missing induction means hostility |
| Assume firm oversight and clear rules from management | Expect freewheeling autonomy in the first month |
| Learn some Bulgarian; it changes how people treat you | Assume English will carry every office conversation |
| Ask a local colleague to guide you through bureaucracy | Try to handle health insurance and offices alone |
| Stay through the first year; staff who do often become loyal | Judge the whole workplace on the first week |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the start details to arrive late, possibly the Friday before a Monday start | Panic when HR says "soon" |
| Chase politely and confirm your start time in writing | Chase every day |
| Prepare to be at your desk at 9 on Monday regardless | Assume silence means the job has gone |
| Ask directly who your point of contact is | Assume an induction includes a mentor |
| Request training and system access explicitly | Wait to be shown the targets |
| Read the paperwork, since much of the training may be reading | Expect a person to walk you through it |

## Bulgaria

Bulgaria has little tradition of structured workplace learning, and the data say so. The European Commission's Education and Training Monitor 2022 reported that adult learning participation among 25-to-64-year-olds in Bulgaria was 1.8 percent in 2021, against an EU-27 average of 10.8 percent, the lowest in Europe. Only 5 percent of Bulgarian adults took an online course in the previous three months, against 18 percent across the EU. A culture with little formal adult education tends to produce onboarding by apprenticeship, and Bulgarian workplaces generally do.

The formal exceptions are in the multinational and software sector. The Bulgarian News Agency reports that the software sector has more than 60,000 workers and about 6,100 companies, with revenue up 11.5 percent in 2024 and exports at 87 percent of revenue, with the Sofia-versus-regions pay gap at around 5 to 7 percent. SAP Labs Bulgaria, in a 2016 corporate blog, describes a formal buddy programme in which an assigned colleague gives culture guidance and supports a newcomer for their first six months, with a session before the start date. That is an employer's description of itself and should be read as such, but it illustrates the point that the structured buddy is imported with the multinational.

On Expat.com, a hospitality owner with around 50 Bulgarian staff said those who survive the first year turn loyal, while another poster argued that mixed foreign and Bulgarian management works worst, because foreign managers give in too easily and local senior managers do not see why some staff need firm oversight. Several replies argued that blaming nationality is lazy and that pay, hiring practices and management style explain more. The voices are employer-side, so they say more about what managers expect than what newcomers experience.

## United Kingdom

British onboarding is formal on paper and informal in practice. The CIPD poll indicates the problem is at the start: more than a quarter of employers have been ghosted on day one and four in ten lose a hire within 12 weeks. A 2017 survey of 4,000 office workers worldwide, commissioned by a vendor and reported by HR Review, found that 56 percent did not receive a full induction or training plan and 36 percent lacked basic equipment on day one, with UK workers taking roughly a month longer to settle in. These are global and vendor-commissioned figures and fairly old, so they should be taken as indicative.

The forums are consistent with the pattern. On Mumsnet, one poster who had resigned and signed a contract had no start time or schedule days before beginning, and HR would say only that details were coming soon; the replies calmly said this was normal and that the information would arrive on Friday. Another poster, starting at a dental clinic after being assured at interview that non-dental staff were welcome, spent three weeks teaching themselves from videos and paperwork, with nobody showing them the systems or the targets.

The British approach to this is a polite reluctance to over-explain. The assumption is that a competent adult will ask, and that an incompetent one will be identified by the absence of questions. This has the effect of making mentorship dependent on personality: the new starter who asks gets help, and the one who waits gets the paperwork.

The structural difference shows in the evidence on who bears the cost. In Britain the cost of a thin induction is visible in the resignation figures, since 41 percent of employers in the CIPD poll had new hires leave within 12 weeks, which suggests that people react to a poor start with their feet. In Bulgaria the cost is less visible, because the labour market for software staff is tight and the multinationals that dominate it have strong reasons to provide structure. The Expat.com thread on first impressions adds a further point about settling in: one couple needed about a year to find expat meetups and hired a translator to deal with health insurance, which indicates that the practical onboarding for a foreigner includes the country as well as the employer. The sensible newcomer to either country treats the first three months as a project to be managed and not an experience to be waited for.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's scores, from tables of mixed vintage, put Bulgaria at a power distance of about 70 and uncertainty avoidance of 85, and the United Kingdom at about 35 and 35. That is the profile of a Bulgarian workplace that expects clear rules and supervision and a British workplace that expects individuals to find their own way within loose rules.

Hence the difference in what a new starter should do. In Bulgaria the mentor is found by proximity and time: you identify the colleague who knows the place and attach yourself to them. In Britain the mentor is found by asking: you request the training, the access and the introduction, because nobody has been assigned to give them. Bulgaria punishes the person who expects a programme. Britain punishes the person who expects a person.

## Conclusion

If you thrive on being left alone with a task and a colleague who will answer your questions, Bulgaria will suit you, especially if you work for a multinational that has imported a buddy scheme. If you can work from a welcome pack and a polite email chain, Britain will suit you, provided you are the sort of person who asks questions on day three.

Whichever you pick, ask the employer three questions: who will show me the systems, who will I go to when I am stuck, and what does the first month look like. The answers will tell you whether the induction is a programme or a person. In both countries the second is usually better than the first, and in neither is it in the contract.
