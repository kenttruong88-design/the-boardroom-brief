**A Government-Built Startup Machine vs. Corporates That Already Act Like Startups: Chile vs. the Netherlands**

**🇨🇱 Chile · 🇳🇱 Netherlands**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Chile built its startup culture on purpose, through a government program that flew in founders from around the world and told them to think regionally instead of just locally — and it worked, turning entrepreneurship from a fringe pursuit into something socially normal within a generation. The Netherlands never needed a program like that, because its biggest corporates already run on flat networks and blunt, fast decision-making that startups elsewhere have to fight to build. One country manufactured its startup energy. The other exports it from inside firms like ING and Philips as a matter of course.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build real personal relationships before expecting fast decisions — trust still precedes speed here | Expect Silicon Valley–style rapid iteration from traditional corporate employers |
| Take advantage of the genuinely international, English-friendly startup scene in Santiago if that's your path | Assume the startup energy has fully transferred to legacy corporate culture — it largely hasn't yet |
| Show patience with scheduling — arriving on time to informal plans is not always mutual | Take a loosely confirmed meeting or event time as fixed; confirm again close to the date |
| Recognize entrepreneurship as newly prestigious — it's a real career path now, not a fallback | Assume startup roles carry the same pay or stability as in more mature ecosystems |
| Engage with risk-averse corporate colleagues respectfully — caution is cultural, not personal | Push aggressively for disruption inside a traditional Chilean company without first earning credibility |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, unsoftened feedback even in large, established corporates | Read Dutch bluntness as rudeness — it's typically meant as respect and trust, not hostility |
| Build broad, flat professional networks — a two-minute conversation can become a lasting connection | Expect deep hierarchy to shield you from direct challenges to your ideas, even at a big company |
| Move quickly through the straightforward company registration process if founding your own venture | Assume starting a company is bureaucratically difficult; it's designed to be simple |
| Treat corporate and startup culture as more similar than different — informality runs through both | Assume a role at a legacy firm like KLM or Heineken means slower, less entrepreneurial work |
| Say directly what you think in meetings — silence is more likely to be read as disengagement than tact | Sandwich criticism in praise, hoping it reads as diplomatic — it often just obscures the point |

Chile's startup identity is a deliberate, state-engineered achievement. Start-Up Chile, launched by the government's development agency CORFO in 2010, was explicitly conceived to shift national culture and mindset around entrepreneurship, not just fund individual companies (Forbes; WEF Competitiveness Repository). By operating entirely in English and pulling in founders from around the world, the program built an international innovation culture in Santiago that didn't exist a generation earlier — entrepreneurship has gone from a fringe choice to something universities now actively support and students actively pursue (Seedstars). But the transformation has been uneven: traditional Chilean corporate culture remains comparatively risk-averse, and startups still cite limited late-stage capital and difficulty retaining talent as the ecosystem's central constraints, with real questions about how quickly large incumbents will adopt more dynamic practices (LinkedIn/Stefan Pribnow).

The Netherlands didn't need to manufacture a startup mindset because its corporate culture already runs close to one. Amsterdam offices, including at legacy giants like ING, Heineken, Philips, and KLM, tend to operate with notably informal hierarchies even by broader European standards, while maintaining professional rigor (Mindspace). Founding a company is deliberately frictionless — straightforward document submission and signing, no heavy bureaucratic gauntlet — and the startup scene itself is defined less by aggressive competition than by dense, collaborative networks: entrepreneurs describe building connections "like stamps" through brief conversations that turn into lasting professional relationships (iamsterdam.com). Growth in the Dutch startup scene has been comparatively lean rather than explosively fast, consistent with a culture that prizes pragmatism over hype.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony is that a Chilean startup can feel more internationally dynamic than a Chilean corporate job, while a Dutch corporate job can feel nearly as informal and direct as a Dutch startup. Chile's mindset gap runs primarily between sectors — join the startup world and you're in a fast, English-speaking, globally minded environment; join a traditional firm and you're back in a more cautious, relationship-first, hierarchy-respecting culture. The Netherlands' mindset gap barely exists at all — bluntness, flat networks, and quick decision-making show up whether you're at a five-person startup or a multinational with a century of history.

Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance scores partly explain this: Chile scores considerably higher on uncertainty avoidance, consistent with corporate caution and a preference for established process, while the Netherlands scores lower, consistent with comfort navigating ambiguity and moving fast regardless of company size. The Dutch didn't need a government program to build startup instincts — the national culture already had them built in.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone advising a Norwegian intern heading to Chile noted that scheduled business meetings tend to start punctually, but informal social plans — a barbecue, a casual meetup — often slip or simply don't happen as arranged, a distinction worth understanding before assuming any commitment is fixed.*

> *dougsblog.substack.com — An American who relocated to the Netherlands for work described Dutch feedback culture as unlike anything they'd experienced professionally: colleagues told them plainly when work wasn't good enough, without any softening, and it took weeks to realize this wasn't hostility but what one local later described as "trust and care" — not wasting someone's time with hollow praise.*

> *Internations Santiago — A foreign founder who joined the Start-Up Chile program noted the contrast between the accelerator's genuinely international, fast-moving culture and the traditional Chilean firms he later worked with as a consultant, which moved at what felt like a completely different institutional speed.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech professional working at a large Dutch corporate wrote that colleagues calling an idea "not good" directly in a meeting, with no cushioning, was initially jarring coming from a more indirect corporate culture elsewhere, but ultimately sped up decision-making because no one wasted time decoding what people actually meant.*

> *Quora — A respondent asked what surprises people most about working in Chile answered candidly that salaries were lower than expected relative to cost of living in Santiago, a detail rarely mentioned in enthusiastic coverage of the country's growing startup reputation.*

### Conclusion

The decision here comes down to which mindset gap you're more willing to navigate: a real divide between two different Chiles — one entrepreneurial and international, one cautious and traditional — or a country where the entrepreneurial mindset is simply the water everyone swims in, startup or otherwise. If you're moving to Chile, choose your sector deliberately; the experience of "startup Chile" and "corporate Chile" can be almost unrecognizable from each other. If you're moving to the Netherlands, brace for directness everywhere you land, and stop waiting for someone to soften the feedback — they're not going to, and eventually you'll be grateful for it.
