# Nairobi Wants You to Survive the Startup, London Wants You to Survive the Interview

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇬🇧 UK

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Kenya raised roughly $638 million in startup funding in 2024, about 29 percent of the African total, according to The Big Deal as cited by Pulse Kenya. A country that attracts that much venture capital might be expected to treat a startup job as an ordinary career move. It does, and also keeps a polite reserve of suspicion, because a bank with a pension scheme remains the more reassuring answer at family gatherings. Britain, which has a rather longer relationship with both pensions and suspicion, runs the same debate in a quieter register.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a startup in writing about medical cover, pension and notice before signing | Assume a startup offers the benefits a corporate would |
| Address senior colleagues by title and full name until told otherwise | Treat startup informality as a licence to skip courtesy |
| Invest in small talk and shared lunches, since networking is valued | Rush straight to the agenda in a first meeting |
| Have a Kenyan advocate review any equity promise | Count equity as part of your salary |
| Budget for several interview rounds at a large corporate | Expect a bank to hire as quickly as a startup |
| Check how long the startup's funding is expected to last | Ignore the funding round when judging job security |

### UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get terms in writing, since British business leans on written agreements | Rely on a friendly verbal assurance |
| Ask a startup what proof of traction it has shown investors | Join on enthusiasm alone |
| Offer opinions directly at a small company, where rank counts for less | Wait to be asked in a corporate meeting only to be ignored anyway |
| Ask what a corporate hybrid policy actually requires | Assume hybrid working is available to everyone |
| Allow time for relationships to mature, sometimes close to a year | Expect a warm first meeting to become a contract |
| Compare startup pay with a corporate offer including benefits | Compare base salaries only |

## Kenya: The Startup Is Cool, The Bank Is Family-Approved

The pay ranges in circulation are informal, so treat them as indicative. McTaba Labs, a Kenyan training outfit, estimates junior startup salaries at KES 50,000 to 100,000, against KES 70,000 to 150,000 at large corporates, with total corporate compensation often 20 to 40 percent above base once pension, medical cover and transport are counted. Its author cites no external data, which is worth remembering before negotiating from his figures. Tech in Africa puts the average Nairobi developer salary at about $14,000, against a global average it gives as $47,000, and says startup roles often demand 50 to 60 hours a week, again without a firm source.

The cultural setting matters as much as the pay. Expat.com describes hierarchy in Kenyan workplaces as very important, with junior staff deferring to senior managers and elders. According to Hofstede Insights, African regions score very high on power distance, though the Kenya-specific figure was not retrievable, so East Africa serves as a proxy here. A startup is the one place where that deference loosens, and employees in small teams report the breadth that comes with it. One startup employee writing for Startup Grind lists multitasking and living cheaply during cash shortages among the things every Kenyan startup employee will experience, and a growth lead at a Kenyan e-commerce company wrote of leadership urging staff to be thoughtful but impatient.

The risk is legible. The Standard reported in 2023 that Twiga Foods cut about a third of its staff, to 543 from over 1,000, months after raising more than Sh20 billion. In June 2026 Kenya's Employment and Labour Relations Court ruled that citing restructuring is not enough to justify redundancy, awarding KES 9.8 million against Nokia Solutions and Networks Kenya, according to TechCabal. That ruling applies to startups too, which is mildly reassuring for employees and mildly alarming for founders.

Hiring follows the same split. McTaba Labs says a startup may conduct one or two interviews and care little about degrees, while a corporate runs four to six rounds with HR screens and technical assessments, and that some bank developers spend 30 to 40 percent of their time in meetings. Pulse Kenya adds that a fast-growing startup can onboard dozens of staff in a single quarter, whereas a bank might take only a few graduates a year. The statutory baseline, according to Expat.com, is at least 21 days of annual leave and about 12 public holidays, which applies whichever logo is on the lanyard.

## UK: Flat Hierarchies, Firm Paperwork

Michael Page, a recruiter, describes UK startups as fast-moving, with less emphasis on rank and more direct input into decisions, while corporates offer clear reporting lines, defined tasks and more stability. The same firm notes that corporate decisions run through longer, more layered processes. None of this is a revelation, but it is a useful reminder that the British startup-versus-corporate argument is mostly about autonomy, whereas the Kenyan one is also about whether the employer will exist next spring.

Pay is more contested than the folklore suggests. A study of Danish data summarised by Yale School of Management found that, among firms of similar size, startups paid higher wages than older companies, possibly as compensation for the risk of unemployment. The same summary warns that Denmark provides retirement and health insurance through the state, so in the UK benefits could narrow the gap. Hybrid working is another corporate sweetener with limits: the Office for National Statistics found that 28 percent of working adults in Great Britain hybrid worked in early 2025, but only 8 percent of those earning under 20,000 pounds did, against 45 percent of those earning 50,000 or more.

Hofstede Insights scores the UK at 89 for individualism, as summarised in secondary sources, which fits the British habit of treating a career as a personal project rather than a family enterprise. A non-British founder writing on HackerNoon found UK business culture methodical, with an emphasis on written agreements, but also found trust slow to build, sometimes taking about a year. That patience extends to funding: investors, the founder wrote, generally want proof of traction before backing a pre-seed company.

The British startup scene is also not short of company. Michael Page cites research by Hacker Young showing the number of new UK businesses rose by 14 percent despite the early impact of the pandemic, against a 6 percent global average. More founders means more small employers offering broad roles and fast progression, and also more small employers that may still be finding their market, which is the recruiter's polite phrase for uncertain pay.

## The Reckoning

On paper the two countries sell the same trade. A startup offers breadth, speed and the possibility of equity; a corporate offers structure, benefits and a promotion ladder. In Kenya the corporate wins on predictability by a wide margin, since benefits and redundancy packages are part of the deal, and the startup wins on informality in an otherwise deferential culture. The startup employee is buying relief from hierarchy and paying for it in salary certainty.

In the UK the safety net is thicker and the hierarchy thinner to start with, so the startup premium is partly cultural and partly financial. The British employee is more likely to be asking whether a small company will teach faster than a large one, whereas the Kenyan employee is asking whether it will pay in March. Neither question is unreasonable, and the person moving between the two countries will need to ask both.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Startup Grind — A Kenyan startup employee says relatives struggle to understand the job, and keep asking when the proper job, meaning one that pays in cash rather than equity, will begin.*

> *EA Forum — A growth lead at a Kenyan e-commerce startup likes the speed, the field visits and the mentorship, but admits that tightening funding after 2021 makes the future less certain.*

> *HackerNoon — A non-British founder says UK investors judged traction and team rather than nationality, but that immigration paperwork, banking and slow trust-building were the hardest parts.*

> *Expat.com — A poster who relocated to Kenya advised that a job offer comes before a work permit and that results depend more on how experience is presented than on the experience itself, though the thread is many years old.*

## Conclusion

Anyone choosing between the two should decide first what they are optimising for. If it is predictability, the Kenyan corporate and the British corporate both deliver, with the Kenyan version leaning on benefits and the British one on a thicker legal and social cushion. If it is learning speed, a startup in either country will provide it, at a price paid in salary, hours or both.

The funding climate deserves a final look before any decision. TechCabal notes that headcount reductions have been widespread across Africa's tech sector since venture investment fell from its 2021 peak, and The Standard observed that private startups publish no annual financials, so their claims are rarely tested. In Britain the equivalent due diligence is simpler, because a company's filings are public, but the principle survives the crossing: ask to see the numbers before you hand over the notice period.

Practical steps are unglamorous: get benefits, notice and equity in writing, ask how long the funding lasts, and have a local lawyer read the contract. The startup mindset is, after all, the willingness to take a risk, and the corporate mindset is the willingness to have it reviewed by three departments first.
