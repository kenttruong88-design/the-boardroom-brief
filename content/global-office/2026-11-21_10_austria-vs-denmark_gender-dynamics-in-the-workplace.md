# Austria Offers Women the Part-Time Job, Denmark the 4 p.m. Pickup, and Neither Has Finished the Argument

🇦🇹 Austria · 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Austria, 51.1 percent of employed women work part-time, against 13.7 percent of employed men. In Denmark, a male colleague declining a four o'clock client call because of "kiddy pick-up" is considered a complete explanation. Both countries are wealthy, orderly and fond of paperwork. They differ in what the paperwork assumes about who collects the children.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask in the interview about childcare opening hours; in most urban centres facilities have closed at 16:00 | Assume an after-school place will cover a full working day |
| Check what the employer offers beyond salary; perks are negotiable and tend not to be volunteered | Wait for benefits to be offered unprompted |
| Learn the parental leave rules, which allow 1,063 days in total if both parents take leave | Assume the second parent's share is optional; 91 to 212 of those days must be used by the partner to claim the full period |
| Ask about the equal-pay protections: the Equal Treatment Act has barred gender discrimination at work since 2008 | Take pay silence as pay equality |
| Look at the employer's supervisory board; a 30 percent quota has applied to large and listed firms since 2018 | Presume the senior ranks reflect the junior ones |
| Expect a mother's part-time request to be treated as ordinary | Expect a father's six-month leave to be treated the same way |

### In Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the early finish; leaving to collect children is routine and does not need an apology | Schedule a four o'clock meeting and expect parents to attend |
| Book daycare early; the subsidised places start at 26 months, at roughly 3,800 kroner a month in Copenhagen | Assume a place appears at the moment you need one |
| Claim your 11 weeks of earmarked leave if you are the second parent | Leave it unused because your employer's culture seems to expect you to |
| Ask whether the company is large enough to report salaries by gender; firms above 35 employees must | Assume small employers publish anything |
| Expect a consultative, flat office in which decisions are made after discussion | Expect the boss to hand down decisions without consulting |
| Check the sector: women dominate the public sector, men the private | Treat the headline equality ranking as a description of every office |

## Austria

Statistik Austria reports that the unadjusted pay gap in its private sector stood at 17.6 percent in 2024, an improvement from 22.2 percent in 2014, against an EU average of 11.1 percent. Women's employment rate, at 70.7 percent, exceeds the EU average of 66.2, but 51.1 percent of employed women work part-time, up from 46.9 percent a decade earlier. The gender pension gap is 40.3 percent. The arithmetic is not mysterious. Women work shorter hours for longer, and are paid accordingly in old age.

Expatica reports, using data from 2017 to 2019, that childcare facilities in most Austrian urban centres closed at 16:00, that women do about 25 hours of unpaid work a week against 16 for men, and that a 30 percent quota for each gender on the supervisory boards of listed and large companies came into force in 2018, when women held 18.5 percent of such seats. Those figures are old and may have improved, which the quota at least intended.

The policy that was meant to help has produced mixed results. A 2026 working paper from Johannes Kepler University in Linz examined Austria's 2004 right to parental part-time work and found large, persistent increases in part-time employment but only modest, short-lived gains in labour-market attachment. Earnings fell, and the "child penalty" rose by about five percentage points. Fathers remain the missing variable: 97 percent of recipients of Austria's childcare allowance are mothers, according to the University of Graz, and men accounted for 4.1 percent of allowance days claimed in 2022, according to the Austrian Science Fund. Only about 1 percent of couples claimed the partnership bonus, which requires a split of at least 40 to 60.

The attitudes are more encouraging than the behaviour. The Graz study notes that 41 percent of Austrians favour an equal split of leave when both parents' jobs are comparable. A University of Vienna project that interviewed 42 couples in 2021 and followed up with 41 in 2023 found fathers who take long leave fall into three types: the highly committed, the joint-project fathers who treat leave as family time, and the career-focused ones whose arrangements can leave women with unexpected burdens. Its lead researcher proposes a "use it or lose it" model of the Nordic kind and notes that it is not under consideration in Austria. Prospective residents should therefore not wait for it.

## Denmark

Statistics Denmark puts the 2025 pay gap at 12.3 percent, which compares with the 13.9 percent the Local cited a year or so earlier and an EU average of 12.7 percent at that time. Employment is 79.2 percent for men and 75.6 percent for women between 16 and 64. Among parents of children under six, about twice as many mothers as fathers work part-time, and fathers work on average 2.8 hours more per week. Denmark is not a country in which the old division has vanished. It is one in which the division is shallower.

Leave is where the system is most visible. Mothers took an average of 240.1 days of paid leave in 2024, down from 278.2 in 2021, while fathers took 76.7, up from 39.1. Fathers now take about a quarter of the total, according to Statistics Denmark. Each parent has 11 weeks of earmarked leave since 2022, and the Copenhagen Post reports that non-birth parents took an average of 2.5 more weeks the following year. The same report cites a 2024 EQUALIS barometer finding little gender progress except in leave, and notes that women are 78 percent of public-sector employees while men are 74 percent of private-sector ones.

Danish women are, on the face of it, well placed. The Local reports that Denmark ranked third in the European Institute for Gender Equality's 2023 index, behind Sweden and the Netherlands, and that women have outnumbered men in long university courses since 2017. Statistics Denmark records that 48 percent of those elected to Parliament in 2026 were women. Daycare is subsidised, leave is shared by statute, and firms with more than 35 employees must report salaries by gender internally. The Copenhagen Post cites a 2019 study linking that rule to faster wage growth for women, with no harm to company profits. The rule does not reach firms under that threshold, which account for about 16 percent of private-sector employees.

The summit is the weak point. Statistics Denmark records women holding 21 percent of board seats and 16 percent of executive and management positions. A Copenhagen Business School study cited by the Local found women earning about 7 percent less than men for the same job. The director of EQUALIS told Women's Agenda that many Danes believe the lack of women at the top reflects personal choice rather than systemic injustice, a view that tends to survive any amount of subsidised daycare.

## The Reckoning

On the narrow question of whether a woman can combine a career with children, Denmark is ahead: a smaller pay gap, fewer lost hours, a fathers' share of leave that is large by Austrian standards, and an office day that ends when the childcare does. Austria's mothers retain employment but not equal earnings, and its fathers, with a leave take-up measured in single digits, have a long way to go. Denmark's weakness is the same as Austria's, only quieter: both countries have more women in the workforce than in the boardroom.

The Hofstede framework, as summarised in an open textbook, groups Austria and Denmark together at the low end of power distance while classing Scandinavian cultures as feminine, which suggests the two offices share a distaste for visible hierarchy and differ in their assumptions about who does the caring. The reader should check this against the arithmetic above. In both countries, a flat hierarchy has proved compatible with an uneven distribution of the school run.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a Ukrainian mother in a small Austrian town says she could not find work in her field, has looked at part-time options and describes the arrangement as far from ideal. She knows parents who put babies into daycare at four months and was not comfortable doing the same, and one income does not cover living costs.*

> *Expat.com forum — an Austria-based respondent in a work-culture thread says the country is a good place to work, partly because of 14 monthly salaries a year, notes that employers often offer a tax-free lunch allowance or a transport pass, and warns that perks are negotiable and rarely offered unless you ask.*

> *First-person column — a British woman who moved to Copenhagen for a think-tank job reports that the office is a ghost town at five, that colleagues of both sexes cite pick-up duty without embarrassment, and that a female director told her women understand they have equal rights to a career but the idea has not formed in their minds.*

> *Women's Agenda — a museum director working in Aarhus says Danes trust that family comes first, that people leave around three to collect children without objection, and that decisions are taken only after consultation, a habit she has since taken home.*

> *University of Vienna interview study — couples describe fathers on long leave as praised by friends, while their partners can face disapproval at work, and say that traditional roles tend to return once the leave ends, with mothers keeping the mental load.*

## Conclusion

The practical comparison is not between a good country and a bad one. It is between two sets of defaults. In Austria the default is that the mother reduces her hours, and the system supports her in doing so, which is why the system keeps producing the result it supports. In Denmark the default is that both parents are expected to be at the school gate by mid-afternoon, which is why the offices empty early and the arithmetic improves. Neither country has solved the executive suite.

If you are a woman considering a move, Denmark offers the more forgiving arithmetic: earmarked leave, a shorter workday and a cultural shrug at pick-up duty. It does not offer a boardroom full of women, and it does not guarantee equal pay for equal work. If you are considering Austria, plan around the 16:00 childcare closure, negotiate benefits in writing and assume the part-time default will apply to you before it applies to anyone else in your household.

If you are a man, the practical advice is simpler. In Denmark, you will be expected to collect the child. In Austria, you will be admired for considering it. In both, the real equality test is a calendar entry, and the calendar tends to win.
