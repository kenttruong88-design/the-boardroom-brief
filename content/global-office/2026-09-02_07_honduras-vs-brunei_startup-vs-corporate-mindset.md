# One Country Wants You to Found a Company. The Other Wants You to Wait Your Turn in Line.

🇭🇳 Honduras · 🇧🇳 Brunei

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Honduras, roughly 250,000 small and micro businesses generate about six in ten jobs in the country, largely because almost nobody else is going to hire you. In Brunei, a wealthy young person's most rational career move is often to wait, sometimes literally in a queue, for a government post that comes with better pay, shorter hours, and more prestige than anything the private sector can offer. Both countries have, in their own ways, built economies where "get a real job" and "start a business" mean almost exactly opposite things.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇳 Honduras

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your business informally first and formalize once revenue proves the concept | Assume a bank loan will be available, or affordable, before you have collateral and a track record |
| Build a personal network of suppliers, fixers, and a trustworthy accountant before you need them | Expect government paperwork to move on a predictable timeline |
| Budget for security and, in some regions, informal "protection" costs as a real line item | Discuss extortion payments ("war taxes") openly outside a small circle of trusted local partners |
| Hire for adaptability over credentials; most competent people learned on the job | Wait for a perfect regulatory environment before launching — it will not arrive |
| Keep pricing flexible and cash flow tight; access to credit is scarce and expensive | Over-invest in fixed infrastructure before demand is proven |
| Learn Spanish business etiquette and relationship-first negotiating norms | Try to close deals purely over email without in-person rapport-building |

#### 🇧🇳 Brunei

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address the most senior person in the room first, using appropriate titles like "Dato" or "Datin" | Jump straight into your pitch before an opening round of pleasantries |
| Expect a genuine 40-hour week and respect it — colleagues will notice if you don't | Assume long hours signal commitment; here they often signal poor planning |
| Frame new ventures as complementary to, not competitive with, the civil service career track many colleagues' families expect | Openly criticize the appeal of government jobs to younger staff |
| Build slowly through consensus; decisions ultimately rest with senior leadership | Push for a fast, unilateral decision in your first few meetings |
| Take advantage of government-backed youth entrepreneurship schemes if you're building locally | Expect the private sector to match public-sector wages or job security |
| Learn the basics of Islamic business etiquette and modest workplace norms | Schedule important meetings without checking around prayer times and Friday observances |

Honduras runs on improvisation because it has to. According to the World Bank and development groups such as Partners Worldwide, only about three percent of the country's small and micro enterprises have meaningful access to bank credit, and where loans exist, interest rates near 36 percent are not unusual. Formal firms that do survive two decades tend to stay smaller than their regional peers, growing employment at under three percent a year on average, according to World Bank research on Latin American entrepreneurs. Meanwhile, researchers at World Economics estimate Honduras's informal economy at roughly 46 percent of GDP, and the World Bank puts informal employment at close to 80 percent of the rural workforce. The upshot is a business culture that treats bureaucracy less as a system to navigate than as weather to plan around.

None of this has stopped a wave of nearshoring-driven optimism. Honduras's young, English-capable workforce and its participation in the DR-CAFTA trade agreement have made it an attractive base for business process outsourcing and manufacturing, and homegrown accelerator programs have sprung up to teach coding and entrepreneurship to a generation that has never known institutional support to be reliable. The entrepreneurs who succeed tend to describe the same skill set: relationship-building, tolerance for ambiguity, and an ability to solve problems that, in a country with functioning institutions, would not need solving in the first place.

Brunei has the inverse problem: institutions that work extremely well, for a shrinking set of jobs. Brunei ranks around 66th globally on the World Bank's ease-of-doing-business measures and fourth within ASEAN, with a "Starting a Business" subscore near 95 out of 100 — on paper, launching a company is simple. But researchers studying Brunei's labour market describe a public-sector "social contract" so deeply institutionalized that government jobs pay more, demand less, and confer more prestige than private employment, which continues to act as a serious disincentive to diversification. Surveys of young Bruneians find that high income, job security, and prestige are the most commonly cited job priorities, with most aspiring toward clerical, white-collar government roles rather than private enterprise. His Majesty Sultan Haji Hassanal Bolkiah has himself publicly urged jobseekers to shed their preference for the civil service.

The government has noticed the mismatch and is trying to engineer its way out of it: youth entrepreneurship schemes have expanded to roughly 50 initiatives covering financing, infrastructure, and market access, and localization programs aim to shift Bruneians into private roles historically filled by migrant labor. But changing incentive structures is slower than changing curricula, and the wage gap between a government desk and a private one remains the more persuasive argument.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive part isn't that Honduras is scrappier — that's expected of a country with a 46-percent informal economy — it's that Brunei, despite topping Honduras on every conventional ease-of-business metric, produces measurably less entrepreneurial ambition. Both cultures also score as strongly hierarchical: Honduras registers a Hofstede power-distance score of 80 against an individualism score of just 20, while independent applications of the Hofstede model put Brunei's power distance in a similar range near 78 — two very different economies converging on the same instinct to defer to the person with the most seniority in the room.

Where they diverge is what happens after the deference. In Honduras, respecting hierarchy doesn't stop anyone from moonlighting, selling something on the side, or quietly building a business the tax office doesn't know about — necessity flattens the org chart. In Brunei, respecting hierarchy is the whole point, and the safest, most socially sanctioned path is to climb inside an institution rather than build one next to it. Honduras has entrepreneurs because it lacks safety nets. Brunei has safety nets so effective they have quietly crowded out entrepreneurs.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter describing early-stage business attempts in Honduras recalled spending months on permits and bank paperwork, only to be told financing required collateral they didn't have; they eventually funded the venture through family savings and informal lenders instead.*

> *UpSpring (entrepreneur interview) — A Honduran founder said the pleasant surprise of doing business locally was how quickly trust-based deals moved once a personal relationship was established, often faster than any formal contract process would have allowed.*

> *impact.bn Job & Work Experiences — A foreign hire in Brunei described opening a meeting by outlining their proposal first and addressing the room generally, only to realize afterward that colleagues expected the most senior official to be greeted and consulted before anyone else spoke.*

> *Quora — A young professional in Brunei described feeling social and family pressure to hold out for a civil service posting even after a promising private-sector job offer, describing the wait as "expected" rather than optional.*

> *Nearshore Americas — An entrepreneur working in Honduras advised newcomers to secure a trusted local partner and accountant before formalizing any venture, arguing that navigating bureaucracy and informal costs alone was the single biggest avoidable mistake he'd seen other founders make.*

### Conclusion

If you are building something in Honduras, your job is to accept that the institutions won't help you and to build a personal network sturdy enough to substitute for them. If you are building something in Brunei, your job is to accept that the institutions work fine, and your real competition isn't a rival startup but the appeal of a secure government desk with your name on it. Neither mindset is irrational once you look at the incentives each country has actually built. The honest advice for an outsider in either place is the same: figure out who benefits from things staying exactly as slow, or exactly as secure, as they currently are — and don't expect them to hand you the org chart that explains it.
