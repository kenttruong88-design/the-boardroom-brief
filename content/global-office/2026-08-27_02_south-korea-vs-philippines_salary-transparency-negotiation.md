# South Korea and the Philippines Agree: Never Say the Number First

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇵🇭 Philippines

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Earlier this year, South Korea's president had to stand at a public policy forum and ask employers, essentially, to please just write the salary in the job ad ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20260324/koreas-salary-blind-culture-meets-transparency-push)). In the Philippines, there is no law stopping a company from telling you exactly what your desk-mate earns — and yet colleagues will discuss almost anything else first, from rent to relationship status, before they'll discuss that. Two countries, two very different reasons for the same silence, and one universal truth: whoever names a number first in these labor markets has already lost some leverage.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for the company to name a figure before you name yours | Bring up pay in the first interview round |
| Negotiate housing stipends and jeonse-style benefits, not just base pay | Assume a "per company policy" job ad means the number is fixed |
| Learn basic hoesik etiquette before your first team dinner | Skip the after-work dinner circuit and assume it's optional |
| Cite tenure and title when arguing for a raise, not just output | Expect a Silicon Valley–style multi-offer bidding war |
| Watch for the new disclosure rules once they land, but verify them yourself | Treat a disclosed range as the ceiling — bonuses stay opaque |
| Bring a Korean-fluent colleague or recruiter into the final negotiation | Negotiate the same way by email as you would face to face |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Frame the ask as a shared solution, not a demand | Push hard for numbers in the very first meeting |
| Ask about the full package — HMO, 13th-month pay, allowances | Assume base salary alone reflects total compensation |
| Benchmark against BPO and multinational pay bands separately | Cite the national PSA average as your leverage — it undercounts skilled pay |
| Negotiate directly and openly if you're a foreign or remote hire | Assume local colleagues can, or will, discuss their own pay |
| Build rapport with HR before raising the topic of money | Treat an internal "confidentiality policy" as legally binding — it usually isn't |
| Check the regional minimum wage where you'll actually work | Treat Metro Manila's wage floor as the national baseline |

---

South Korea's pay system still runs largely on hobongje — the seniority-based structure that rewards how long you've stayed over what you've produced, a holdover from the growth-era 1980s that survived the 1997 IMF crisis mostly intact ([Dongguk University, Asia Pacific Business Review](https://pure.dongguk.edu/en/publications/examining-the-seniority-based-pay-system-in-korean-companies-curr/)). That makes negotiation less a conversation and more an archaeology project: your number is largely a function of your start date, your title, and how many hoesik dinners you've survived. It also produces some ugly arithmetic — South Korea has the widest gender wage gap in the OECD, 29.3 percent as of the latest count, more than double the OECD average ([Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/3400350); [Asia Business Daily](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2025061710052076130)), which is why President Lee Jae Myung has been publicly pushing mandatory pay disclosure in job postings, over the objections of a labor ministry worried it will simply shrink hiring ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20260324/koreas-salary-blind-culture-meets-transparency-push)).

None of that changes the choreography for an individual hire: offers in Korea are typically extended first, negotiated second, a sequencing that catches Western candidates off guard when they expect back-and-forth during interview rounds and instead get silence until a contract lands ([TeamBlind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/salary-negotiation-with-south-korea-5qs4ccmo)). Hofstede's data gives the cultural logic: Korea scores 60 on power distance and a striking 18 on individualism, among the most collectivist readings in Hofstede's global dataset — which is a formal way of saying that asking your future boss for more money before you've even started feels, to a Korean HR manager, faintly insubordinate.

The Philippines has no statutory rule on salary secrecy at all — employers manufacture confidentiality out of internal policy and NDAs, not law ([Manila Recruitment](https://manilarecruitment.com/manila-recruitment-articles-advice/understanding-salary-confidentiality-laws-and-how-it-is-handled-in-the-philippines/)). The Philippine Statistics Authority puts the average monthly wage for formal-sector workers at roughly ₱21,544, though real professional bands stretch far above that, and the wage floor itself splits by region — ₱755 a day in Metro Manila under the newest NCR wage order, ₱411 in BARMM, set independently by seventeen separate regional wage boards rather than a single national number ([DOLE / Regional Tripartite Wages and Productivity Boards](https://sweldoph.com/calculators/minimum-wage)). That's before you get to the BPO sector, where local rates and remote/expat rates diverge so sharply that comparing them on the same chart is close to meaningless.

Hofstede's numbers produce a genuine surprise here: the Philippines scores 64 on masculinity — a proxy for competitiveness and achievement orientation — versus Korea's 39, and 94 on power distance versus Korea's 60. On paper, Filipino workplace culture is both more hierarchical and more achievement-driven than Korea's. In practice, that hierarchy expresses itself as deference rather than direct competition: pushing hard for a number in a first meeting reads as aggressive, not ambitious, and the safer move is to frame a counteroffer as collaborative problem-solving rather than a demand.

**The Reckoning.** The instructive part is who's doing the intervening. In Korea, the state is trying to force transparency onto a culture that still treats salary as a private, hierarchy-coded fact — a president publicly lobbying employers to just write the number down. In the Philippines, no one in government is trying to force anything; the opacity is entirely self-administered, sustained by social norms and the same "crab mentality" that punishes anyone who stands out by asking for — or getting — more than their peers.

Flip the countries and the mismatch gets sharper still. Korea's Hofstede numbers predict a quiet, deferential negotiating style, and that's roughly what shows up. The Philippines' numbers predict something closer to competitive individualism, and yet the lived culture is arguably more conflict-averse about money than Korea's. Cultural dimension scores, in other words, are a starting hypothesis, not a script — useful for packing the right expectations, useless as a substitute for actually watching how your specific office behaves before you open your mouth about a number.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind poster — was told by a colleague hiring for a Korean firm to ask specifically for a jeonse-style housing arrangement rather than a bigger cash number, since companies like Samsung and Coupang bundle it for foreign hires and it's worth more than the raw salary difference.*

> *TeamBlind poster — went into interviews expecting to negotiate round by round the American way, and instead got no salary discussion at all until an actual offer letter showed up, which felt less like negotiation and more like being handed a verdict.*

> *Quora respondent on Philippine workplaces — assumed asking directly and firmly for a specific figure would be read as confidence, and instead found colleagues and even the hiring manager visibly uncomfortable, since a collaborative, softer framing gets further than a hard number stated upfront.*

> *Quora respondent on foreign hiring in Korea — was surprised to learn Korean labor law requires foreign workers be paid on the same scale as Korean colleagues in the same role, which undercut the assumption that expatriates automatically get quietly lowballed.*

> *Expat.com forum contributor — described Philippine office culture as built around visible deference to management and a strong service veneer, which made it harder to gauge whether a stated deadline, or a stated pay ceiling, was actually fixed or just the opening position.*

### Conclusion

If you're negotiating in South Korea, do your homework before the offer arrives, because that's the only real negotiating window you'll get — and put housing and tenure-linked progression on the table, not just a cash figure. If you're negotiating in the Philippines, do your homework on total package rather than base pay, and expect the conversation to reward patience and rapport over directness, regardless of what any cultural-dimensions chart predicts about Filipino competitiveness. Both countries will tell you, in their own idiom, that transparency is coming — Korea by legislative decree, the Philippines by market pressure from remote and BPO benchmarking sites. Neither has arrived yet, so bring your own number, and don't say it first.
