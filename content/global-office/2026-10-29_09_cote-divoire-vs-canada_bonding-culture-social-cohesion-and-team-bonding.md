# In Côte d'Ivoire, Your Coworkers Call You Family by Week Two. In Canada, You're Still an "Acquaintance" After Six Months.

**🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

An expatriate employee interviewed about his posting in Ivory Coast didn't hedge when asked about his team: "we work together like a family, with very short lines of communication," he said. "On a human level, it has created strong bonds between us all." Compare that to the experience one newcomer described after moving to British Columbia: Canadians are "generally very nice, polite and friendly," but also "a little bit reserved, distant and cliquish" — and friendships, once formed, tend to stay exactly where they started, rarely extending past the office or classroom that produced them. Both countries bond. They just disagree, almost completely, on the timeline and the price of admission.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect colleagues to describe the team in explicitly familial terms — "like a family" is not a figure of speech here, it's the operating model. | Be surprised when a colleague's financial obligations to extended family or community come up as a normal, unremarkable topic. |
| Accept informal requests for small loans or contributions from coworkers as part of a wider, two-way system of mutual support. | Assume that system is one-directional — the same colleague who asks for help today is expected to give it tomorrow. |
| Lean into workplace camaraderie as a genuine source of day-to-day support, not just professional courtesy. | Treat redistribution pressure as simply "generosity" — for many workers it's also a real constraint on saving and getting ahead. |
| Recognize that short, warm communication lines often mean less formal hierarchy signaling in daily interactions than the org chart implies. | Expect privacy around personal finances the way you might elsewhere — sharing resource pressures with colleagues is normalized. |
| Join in when unexpected guests show up to a shared meal — hosting people who weren't explicitly invited is a normal, almost constant occurrence. | Assume every colleague experiences this pressure equally — researchers found it concentrated most heavily on those with the widest networks of obligation. |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect politeness immediately and real closeness slowly — the two are not the same thing and shouldn't be confused for each other. | Mistake a friendly "we should get coffee sometime" for an actual, scheduled plan — in Canadian register, it often isn't one. |
| Put in the first move yourself — invite a coworker to lunch, join the office sports team, show up to the optional social event. | Wait for a Canadian colleague to chase the friendship along; by most accounts, that effort has to come from you. |
| Give it real time — a few months of regular, low-stakes contact before being treated as a genuine friend rather than a familiar face. | Take a "maybe" at face value — it quite often functions as a polite way of declining without saying so directly. |
| Treat coworkers as the easiest people to befriend precisely because you see them on a predictable schedule, which does most of the relationship-building work for you. | Expect the friendship to extend naturally beyond the context that created it — many Canadian relationships stay bounded to the setting they started in. |
| Value a strong, nationally-measured sense of belonging as real even when it coexists with everyday social reserve — the two track together more than you'd expect. | Read reserve as rejection — multiple newcomers describe it as a stable cultural default, not a personal judgment on them. |

### Côte d'Ivoire

The strongest evidence for Ivorian workplace bonding isn't a survey, it's a study of what happens to people's wallets. Researchers studying cashew-processing workers in Côte d'Ivoire between 2016 and 2019 found that 83 percent gave money to someone else multiple times a month, and 75 percent agreed that this redistributive pressure made it difficult to accumulate savings, even though 78 percent said they strongly wanted to save more. The same social fabric that produces "we work together like a family" also produces a near-constant, low-level expectation of sharing resources with the people around you — colleagues very much included. The researchers found that workers who'd had uninvited guests show up for a meal in the past week were 56 percentage points more likely to take up a locked commitment-savings account when offered one, and those paying school fees for others were 33 points more likely — a fairly direct measurement of how much daily life runs on obligation to a wider circle. This isn't unique to the cashew sector, either: tontines, the informal rotating savings and credit associations found across West Africa, work on an almost identical logic. A group of colleagues, neighbors, or relatives each contribute a fixed amount on a schedule, and the full pot rotates to a different member each round. It's a communal financial safety net built entirely on trust between people who already bond in every other part of life, which is precisely why it maps so neatly onto office relationships that already run "like a family."

That same circle is what produces the camaraderie the expatriate interview described: short communication lines, a genuinely supportive team culture, families treated as the office's concern rather than a private matter kept outside it. One expat in Abidjan, by contrast, named the harder edge of adjusting to this system from the outside: "difficulties in connecting and building relationships, feeling isolated and disconnected," paired with the ordinary challenge of "balancing work and family life" once inside it. The bonding is real and fast. So is the bill that eventually comes with belonging to it.

### Canada

Canada's official data tells a strange, two-layered story: a large majority of residents report a strong sense of national belonging, while person-to-person warmth is famously, almost proverbially, slow to arrive. Statistics Canada's General Social Survey work on social identity has repeatedly found that roughly three in four Canadians report a strong or somewhat strong sense of belonging to Canada itself — a high number for a country whose individual social style reads, to newcomers, as anything but effusive.

At the office specifically, a Randstad Workmonitor survey found that a majority of employees report close friendships with colleagues and that only 21 percent see those friendships as something that interferes with work, below the global average of 26 percent — evidence that bonding at work is genuinely valued, not merely tolerated. What takes longer is converting that workplace warmth into something that survives outside it. Newcomers describe Canadians as "friendly but passive": chatting easily at the gym or in the break room, but rarely initiating the follow-up that turns an acquaintance into a friend. One widely echoed piece of advice puts a number on it directly — expect three to six months of regular, ordinary contact before you're treated as a friend rather than a familiar face, and six to twelve months before anything deeper forms. Technology appears to be nudging that timeline along rather than replacing it: in the same Randstad data, 44 percent of Canadian respondents said social media had increased their contact with coworkers outside working hours, suggesting the slow in-person build is increasingly supplemented, if not sped up, by a lower-effort digital one.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real difference isn't warmth versus coldness — both cultures clearly value connection — it's where each one puts the cost. Côte d'Ivoire front-loads the bonding: you're folded into the team's social fabric almost immediately, and the price is a standing, lifelong expectation that you'll share resources with that same fabric when it needs you to. Canada back-loads it: the warmth is genuine but metered out slowly, gated behind months of unglamorous repeat contact, and the price is mostly patience rather than obligation.

Hofstede's framework offers a tidy, if imperfect, way to name this. Canada scores firmly individualist, in the 80s on Hofstede's 0-100 individualism index, which tracks with friendships that form through deliberate, bounded choices rather than circumstance alone. Côte d'Ivoire isn't individually indexed in Hofstede's published country list, but the "West Africa" regional cluster Hofstede groups it alongside — scoring low on individualism and high on power distance — lines up closely with a culture where belonging to a group, and the mutual obligations that come with it, is simply assumed rather than opted into. Neither framework fully resolves the paradox each country lives with: Ivorian bonding is fast and warm but comes bundled with financial pressure researchers can actually measure; Canadian bonding is slow and hard-won but, once formed, appears to ask very little of you in return.

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Canada to Côte d'Ivoire, prepare to be absorbed into a team's social and financial life faster than you're used to, and budget accordingly — both your calendar and, eventually, your wallet. If you're moving from Côte d'Ivoire to Canada, lower your expectations for instant warmth without lowering your expectations for eventual depth: the friendliness is real on day one, the friendship takes months, and the only way to speed it up is to be the one who keeps showing up.

Either way, the two countries agree on one thing neither says out loud: belonging is never actually free. Côte d'Ivoire just bills you up front, and Canada bills you in installments.
