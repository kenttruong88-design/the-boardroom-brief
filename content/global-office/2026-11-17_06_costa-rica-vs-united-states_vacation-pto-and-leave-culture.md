# Costa Rica Gives You Two Weeks and Means It; America Gives You Two Weeks and Hopes You Won't Notice

🇨🇷 Costa Rica · 🇺🇸 United States

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The United States is the only country in this comparison where the law does not promise you a single day off. According to the employment law firm Littler, Costa Rica guarantees at least two weeks of paid vacation for every 50 weeks worked for one employer, written into Article 59 of its Constitution and the Labor Code. The US guarantees nothing at the federal level for most private employees, except the right to be told how generous your employer feels. The result is a comparison between a country that mandates rest and a country that offers it as a perk.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the 50-week rule; the first two weeks arrive after 50 weeks with one employer | Expect a full vacation in your first year |
| Count on the aguinaldo, a thirteenth-month bonus equal to a month's salary, paid around December | Forget to budget for the December payment |
| Remember that the employer chooses the dates, within 15 weeks of the 50 | Book flights before your leave is confirmed |
| Plan your vacation to be taken in one block, or at most two | Assume you can split leave freely |
| Check the number of days in writing; sources count the two weeks as 10 or 12 | Assume 14 |
| Accept that businesses open late and close on Sundays and holidays | Plan Sunday errands or early starts |

### In the United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your offer letter; vacation is whatever the employer says | Assume a standard amount exists |
| Check how long you must work before vacation accrues; it is often 90 days to 12 months | Plan a trip in your first month |
| Ask about paid public holidays separately | Assume that "PTO" includes them |
| Use your days; a quarter of workers did not use any in 2024 | Treat unused days as a sign of dedication |
| Look at the fine print of any "unlimited" policy | Assume unlimited means more days; people on such policies average about 16 |
| Plan to be reachable by email | Expect to be fully offline |

## Costa Rica

The Costa Rican system is simple, written down and difficult to argue with. After 50 weeks of continuous work, an employee is entitled to two weeks of paid vacation, which QCostaRica explains is counted in calendar days, so a five-day week gives 10 vacation days and a six-day week gives 12. Remote's benefits guide says 12 paid days, which is a discrepancy worth settling in your contract before it settles itself. Employees with under 50 weeks accrue one paid day per month. The employer sets the timing within 15 weeks of the 50, and the vacation must be continuous, though it can be split in two with agreement. Add nine fully paid public holidays and the aguinaldo, and the picture is of a country that has decided rest is part of the compensation.

Other leave is statutory too. Remote lists four months of maternity leave at full pay, and sick leave in which the employer pays 50 percent and the social security fund 50 percent for the first three days, then 60 percent from the fourth day from the fund. Hofstede's scores, via The Culture Factor, put Costa Rica at 35 for power distance and 15 for individualism, which is among the most collectivist in the world, against the US's 40 and 91, via Rework. That helps to explain the attitude: a collectivist workplace treats leave as a thing the group manages, not a thing an individual negotiates.

A Houston woman profiled by CNBC Make It, who moved to the Nicoya Peninsula, said she works roughly 25 hours a week across four part-time roles and earns less than in the US, but is a lot happier. She described children turning up at parents' workplaces, parents leaving for a sick child without a reprimand and businesses that close on Sundays and holidays, which she believes prevents burnout. A longtime resident writing on Expat.com advised adding about a month to any Western timeline and accepting that locals work at a different tempo. The cost, as a US expat wrote in the Tico Times, is that economic advancement is still easier in the US, where the same job pays more.

## United States

American vacation is a patchwork. The Bureau of Labor Statistics found in 2026 that 91 percent of workers at the largest private establishments, those with 500 or more employees, had paid vacation, against 71 percent at the smallest, those with 1 to 49. After one year of service, 32 percent of private-industry workers received 10 to 14 days, and after ten years, 30 percent received 15 to 19 days. Most employers require 90 days, six months or 12 months of service before leave begins. There are local rules in a few places, including West Hollywood, Maine and Nevada, according to Littler, but there is no federal floor.

The more telling data concerns what people do with the days they have. A late-2025 FlexJobs survey of 3,000 US workers, reported by Expat.com Magazine, found that one in four took no vacation in 2024 and that 42 percent used only one day despite being entitled to 10. A quarter said their manager discouraged a full week off. Unlimited-vacation policies cover about 7 percent of US companies, and the people on them take an average of about 16 days, so the freedom of the policy is somewhat theoretical.

That is where the Hofstede gap in individualism, 91 against 15, becomes practical. In an individualist workplace, the vacation is yours to claim and also yours to justify, and the price of not claiming it is paid privately. The Costa Rican system makes the same decision for everybody by statute, which is less flexible and more reliable. A US expat in Costa Rica might reasonably prefer a worse offer with a guaranteed fortnight to a better offer with an invitation.

It is worth separating what the law guarantees from what the workplace expects. In Costa Rica, the guarantee is a minimum of two weeks after 50 weeks, nine public holidays and an extra month of pay, and the expectation is that the workplace runs at a slower tempo, with Sunday closures that the Houston expat credits with preventing burnout. In the United States, the guarantee is nothing, and the expectation is set by the individual employer, with the Expat.com article noting that US employees are generally expected to check email on vacation. A newcomer should therefore ask two questions in each country: what does the contract say, and what do people actually do. The second is usually the more expensive to find out.

## The Reckoning

If the only question is how much rest the system guarantees, Costa Rica wins without a recount. It provides statutory leave, public holidays, a bonus month and a culture that closes on Sundays. The US provides a market in which the best employers offer more and the worst offer none, and in which an employee's skill at negotiation determines their time off. The comparison is not between 10 days and 15 days. It is between a floor and a ceiling.

What the US offers in return is money. The Tico Times writer's observation that the same job pays more at home is confirmed by a Hacker News thread about US companies hiring Latin American developers, in which a South American developer said local mid-level offers topped out around $1,000 a month, against about $3,200 for a US remote entry-level role. The thread says nothing about Costa Rican vacation specifically, and should be taken as a pay anecdote only. Still, a reader who is calculating the hourly value of a free Sunday should include the possibility that the Costa Rican hour is paid less.

## Conclusion

If your priority is time, Costa Rica is the better bet and the contract is on your side, provided you are willing to wait 50 weeks and to be paid on the local scale. If your priority is income, the US still offers more of it, and you should read the offer letter as if you were reading a lease, because in effect you are.

In either country, ask to see the leave policy before you sign, put the number of days in writing and use them. The Costa Rican version is the law. The American version is a suggestion with a start date.
