# Uruguay Gives You More Vacation Days. Costa Rica Gives You Fewer Reasons to Use Them.

**🇺🇾 Uruguay · 🇨🇷 Costa Rica**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Uruguay legislates its relaxation: 20 paid vacation days a year, climbing to 25 with tenure, plus a full month's extra salary just for taking them. Costa Rica legislates less generously — 14 days minimum — and then quietly makes up the difference by simply not being in a hurry about anything, ever, including whether you show up on time. One country built work-life balance into the labor code. The other built it into the national vocabulary. Both outcomes look similar from a hammock; they are not the same system.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇾 Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect and request your "vacation salary" — a bonus payment on top of normal wages during leave | Assume vacation pay is optional or negotiable; it's a statutory entitlement |
| Plan for 20 days minimum after one year of service, rising with tenure | Undersell your leave in salary negotiations, thinking it's a perk rather than a floor |
| Take the five mandatory paid public holidays seriously — offices genuinely close | Schedule client calls on July 18 or August 25 expecting anyone to answer |
| Use the digital nomad permit route if working remotely for a foreign employer | Assume you can work locally without proper registration — enforcement is real |
| Expect sick leave to shift onto social security after day three | Panic if HR mentions "BPS" — it's the standard social security fund, not a red flag |
| Enjoy the reputation for stability — Uruguay ranks among the safest, most transparent economies in the region | Expect Buenos Aires-style improvisation; Uruguayan process tends to be by-the-book |

#### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bank on your 13th-month "aguinaldo" bonus before Christmas — it's mandatory, not discretionary | Forget to budget for it as an employer, or expect it as optional as an employee |
| Adjust your sense of urgency — "pura vida" genuinely shapes deadlines and start times | Interpret a relaxed schedule as low commitment; it's cultural, not a performance issue |
| Confirm whether your 14 vacation days must be taken consecutively — they often must | Assume you can freely split leave into scattered long weekends without employer sign-off |
| Get proper sponsorship before working locally — informal arrangements are legally risky | Work under the table on a tourist status; enforcement has tightened |
| Expect real, respected boundaries around personal time outside work hours | Message colleagues after hours expecting a same-day reply, even from managers |
| Appreciate the slower meeting culture as a feature, not a delay | Push to "move fast" without first reading the room's actual pace |

### Uruguay: Vacation as a Legislated Floor

Uruguay treats time off as infrastructure. Employees earn 20 working days of paid annual leave after a year of service, rising by a day for every four years up to 25, according to [Remote.com's leave guide](https://remote.com/country-explorer/uruguay/types-of-leave). Crucially, that leave comes with a "vacation salary" — a full additional payment on top of ordinary wages, specifically meant to fund the time off, not just permit it. Add five mandatory paid public holidays among Uruguay's 15 observed dates, three days of employer-paid sick leave before social security (BPS) takes over, and a 2023-era digital nomad permit that formalizes remote work for foreign employers, and the picture is a country that has written relaxation into statute rather than trusting culture to deliver it.

That legal generosity sits inside a broader reputation for institutional steadiness. Uruguay consistently ranks as one of the safest, most transparent, and most democratically stable countries in Latin America — the kind of place where a digital nomad expects the paperwork to be slow but the rules to actually hold once followed, per [Live in Uruguay's expat resources](https://www.liveinuruguay.uy/digitalnomadpermit).

### Costa Rica: Relaxation as a Cultural Operating System

Costa Rica's statutory minimum looks thinner on paper — 14 working days of paid vacation after 50 weeks of service, plus nine public holidays, per [Rivermate's leave guide](https://rivermate.com/guides/costa-rica/leave). But the number understates the lived experience. "Pura vida" is not a tourism slogan; it functions as an actual behavioral norm, and [business-anthropologist.com's interviews with Costa Ricans](https://business-anthropologist.com/costa-rican-work-culture-described-by-costa-ricans/) describe a workplace where late arrivals, early departures, and loosely respected schedules are simply how things run, not signs of disengagement. Costa Rica's Hofstede power distance score of 35 — notably the lowest in Latin America — reinforces this: flatter hierarchies mean less pressure to visibly perform overwork for a boss's benefit.

Costa Rica also mandates the aguinaldo, a 13th-month bonus paid before Christmas equal to the average of the past year's monthly salary — a predictable financial cushion that, unlike Uruguay's vacation salary, isn't tied to time off at all, but shapes the same end-of-year rhythm.

### The Reckoning

The paradox here is that the country with the stingier legal minimum feels, by most accounts, more relaxed day to day. Uruguay's generosity is precise and procedural — you get exactly what the code says, paid exactly as specified, and the rest of the year runs on relatively conventional, Euro-inflected professionalism. Costa Rica's generosity is diffuse and constant — fewer guaranteed days off, but a baseline unhurriedness that doesn't wait for an official holiday to show up.

For a newcomer, this cuts both ways. Uruguay rewards people who like clear entitlements and predictable enforcement; you'll know exactly what you're owed and when. Costa Rica rewards people who can tolerate ambiguity about pace but come to depend on genuine after-hours boundaries. Push either system to behave like the other — demanding Uruguayan-style precision in San José, or expecting Tico-style flexibility in Montevideo — and you'll spend your first year mildly, persistently confused.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who telecommutes for a US company from Costa Rica described the hardest adjustment as recalibrating their own urgency, saying colleagues back home would send a message and expect an answer within the hour, while local friends treated a same-week reply as perfectly normal.*

> *Expat.com, Montevideo forum — A contributor new to the city noted that expat meetups required no membership, dues, or formality at all — you simply showed up — and found the contrast with more bureaucratic expat scenes elsewhere in the region genuinely disarming.*

> *Press Start Evolution blog — A Uruguayan remote worker who spent time working from abroad wrote candidly that returning home meant re-adjusting to how seriously colleagues took the mandatory holiday calendar, after months in a country where "the office never really closes."*

> *Howdy blog — A digital nomad in Uruguay flagged the tax exemption on exported technology services as a genuine, underrated perk that rarely comes up in general expat guides, calling it more valuable long-term than the vacation days themselves.*

> *Quora — An expat weighing a move to Costa Rica was warned by a respondent that legal work requires actual employer sponsorship now, since informal or under-the-table arrangements that used to be tolerated have become a real legal risk as enforcement has tightened.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is a written, enforceable entitlement, Uruguay's labor code will treat you well and rarely surprise you. If your priority is a workplace that genuinely respects the hours outside the office, Costa Rica's culture will get you there faster than its modest statutory minimum suggests. According to the [World Bank's regional indicators](https://data.worldbank.org), both countries outperform most Latin American peers on stability and quality of life — the real choice is whether you want your relaxation guaranteed by law or absorbed into the culture around you.

Priya's honest take, over a drink: ask for the number in Montevideo, and ask for the vibe in San José — in neither city should you ask for both.
