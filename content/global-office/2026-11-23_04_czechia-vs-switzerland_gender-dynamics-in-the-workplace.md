# Czechia and Switzerland Agree Women Earn Less and Disagree Only on the Paperwork

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Switzerland, by official count, pays women 15.7 percent less than men. Czechia, according to Eurostat figures reported by Expats.cz, manages 18.5 percent, the second-widest gap in the European Union. The two countries have spent decades arriving at a similar result by deliberately different routes. One sends mothers away for years and then wonders why they return slowly. The other keeps them in the office three days a week and presents the arrangement as a lifestyle choice. For the prospective migrant, the useful question is not which gap is smaller. It is which set of assumptions about women you will be negotiating with before the first coffee.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the salary range before the interview ends, and benchmark it against the role | Accept the first offer on the theory that employers will correct it later |
| Watch what senior colleagues wear before settling on a version of business casual | Assume a dress code that would pass elsewhere will be read the same way here |
| Document pay discussions and promotion criteria in writing | Rely on a verbal promise made during a long leave of absence |
| Use direct, unemotional language when you object to a remark or decision | Assume that silence from a Czech colleague means agreement |
| Ask whether the employer offers part-time or flexible hours before you need them | Expect part-time work to be common, because it is not |
| Learn enough Czech to catch the jokes you are meant to hear | Treat a sexist joke as a language problem rather than a conduct problem |

### Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research childcare places and costs before accepting a job offer | Assume a subsidised place will appear because your salary is reasonable |
| Negotiate a part-time percentage in writing, including role and responsibilities | Agree to 60 percent hours while keeping 100 percent of the workload |
| Check which canton and commune you will live in, because subsidies vary | Assume the rules are national |
| Ask early about promotion paths for part-time staff | Take a flexible contract on faith at a bank or a traditional firm |
| Track your pension contributions if you reduce hours | Treat reduced hours as a purely short-term decision |
| Ask colleagues how parents are actually treated, not how the brochure says they are | Mistake polite silence for approval of your schedule |

## Czechia: the long leave and the short ladder

Czechia has an unusually generous approach to time off for mothers and an unusually modest approach to what happens afterwards. Expats.cz notes that maternity leave runs up to 28 weeks, and Czech Radio has described the country's parental leave as among the longest anywhere, while also reporting that returning to work is hard. Childcare access has long been among the weakest in the EU, according to the same Expats.cz coverage, and part-time work is rare: only about 10 percent of Czech women work part time, compared with roughly 50 percent in Austria, according to Eurostat figures cited by the site in 2022. The system offers women a long absence, a narrow exit and very little in between.

The pay data reflect the arrangement. Expats.cz reports that the hourly gap in Czechia peaks at 21.6 percent for workers aged 35 to 44, which is precisely when careers are supposed to accelerate and childcare is supposed to end. The same article reports a gap of 35.6 percent in finance and insurance, though it does not name a source for the sector figures, so treat that number as indicative. Women hold 13.8 percent of corporate board seats, against a European average of 25.8 percent, per a 2022 article by the same outlet. These figures are not recent, and some will have moved, but none has moved enough to alter the story.

On the office floor, the reputation is of a culture that tolerates more than it admits. A reader survey run by Expats.cz found that only 36 percent of respondents felt paid fairly compared with male colleagues, and a recurring complaint was that women still come second. Separately, the site's guide to Czech offices reports that sexist jokes and mild harassment are tolerated more than in many Western countries, citing a 2005 study in which nearly half of women in male-dominated fields had experienced or witnessed harassment. A 2005 study is hardly current, but the cheerfully relaxed dress code and the generous attitude to banter suggest the picture has not been entirely rewritten. To be fair, the same sources note that Czech women tend to be direct, and that expats often find the colleagues reserved at first and warmer afterwards. Expats.cz also reports that EU pay-transparency rules are expected to narrow the gap, with an official from the Labour Ministry's equal pay project arguing that everyone should know the pay range for a given role. Whether employers share this enthusiasm is a question the next few years will answer, probably in a footnote.

## Switzerland: the part-time compromise

Switzerland's gender problem is less an absence than an arrangement. According to The Local, just over 18 percent of working men in Switzerland work part time, compared with 58.6 percent of working women. Bloomberg reported in 2023 that Swiss mothers switch to part-time work more than mothers in any other advanced economy, and earn less as a result. The Economist, in its 2025 Glass Ceiling Index, ranked Switzerland fourth-worst among 29 OECD countries, citing childcare that consumes about 49 percent of the average annual salary and a parental leave system it ranked the weakest in the group. Women's participation in the workforce and their presence on boards were rated above average, which is the sort of detail that makes the ranking more interesting rather than more comforting.

The Federal Statistical Office put the overall pay gap at 15.7 percent in 2024, down from 16.2 percent two years earlier, with 6.5 percent unexplained by factors such as education and role. The gap in the private sector was 17.2 percent and in finance and insurance as high as 30.6 percent. In other words, even after accounting for everything that can be accounted for, a slice remains that is simply attributed to being a woman. The unexplained gap is wider at the top, at 12.4 percent for senior managers against 6.1 percent for employees without managerial roles, so the higher a Swiss woman climbs, the more the arithmetic charges her for the view. Swiss progress is real, but it proceeds at the speed of a committee that meets quarterly.

The cultural explanation is traditional roles. Hofstede Insights scores Switzerland at 70 on masculinity against 57 for Czechia, a gap that sounds like a joke given the pay data but at least confirms that neither country is a quiet haven of equality. Swiss part-time work is common enough to feel like a normal choice, and that normality is the trap: it quietly turns a household decision into a career pattern. As The Local's contributor put it, mothers are pushed into a part-time trap that is very hard to climb out of.

## The Reckoning

On the numbers, Czechia is the harder place to be a woman at work: a wider pay gap, scarcer flexible work and a more tolerant attitude to the sort of comments that would prompt an HR memo elsewhere. Switzerland is the harder place to be a mother at work. Its gap is smaller and its workplaces, on paper, more orderly, but the economics of childcare mean that many women trade income for presence and hours for hope. A Czech woman is more likely to confront the ceiling directly. A Swiss woman is more likely to be told that a lower ceiling is a lifestyle. Both arrangements have their defenders, and most of them are not the ones filling in the childcare forms.

For the foreign worker, the practical difference is the decision point. In Czechia, the risk arrives with the long absence and the return. In Switzerland, it arrives with the childcare invoice and the quiet shrinkage of a contract from 100 percent to 60. Neither is a reason to avoid the country, but both are reasons to read the contract before the relocation package. The 2025 Glass Ceiling Index placed both countries near the bottom, and that is the closest thing to a verdict the data provide.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/askswitzerland — A commenter describing life with a young child said childcare costs run to thousands of francs a month, subsidised places are a matter of luck, and mothers are the ones who usually cut hours. Another warned that Switzerland is not an easy choice for a woman who cares about her career.*

> *The Local — Members of an online forum for immigrant mothers with Swiss partners described stress over finding childcare and managing the return to work after maternity leave. They supported one another, though none mentioned the fathers.*

> *Expats.cz reader survey — Expat women in Czechia praised long parental leave and professional growth, but a large share were unsure they were paid fairly. Several felt that women are still treated as second to men at work, and some found dress codes objectifying.*

> *Expats.cz expat account — One foreign woman working in a Czech office said she had never felt treated as lesser, but she was aware of working in a different culture. She concluded that being the only foreigner in an all-Czech office mattered more than being a woman.*

## Conclusion

Czechia suits the woman who wants to be told the truth, negotiates loudly and treats the first offer as an opening remark. Switzerland suits the woman who has the savings and the stamina to absorb childcare costs and the patience to wait for the committee. Women with children should ask hard questions about leave, subsidies and part-time promotion before moving to either country, and should request the answers in writing. A sensible approach is to treat the first year as fieldwork: ask how many women sit in the management meetings, how many mothers work part time in senior roles, and what happened to the last person who asked for a raise. Employers who answer cheerfully and specifically are worth keeping. Those who answer with a mission statement are providing the data you needed.

Neither country is a disaster, and neither is a utopia. Both will pay you less than they pay the man at the next desk, but one will do it more bluntly and the other will do it with better stationery.
