# Japan Automates Everything Except Guilt; Morocco Just Adds a Second Coffee Break

🇯🇵 Japan · 🇲🇦 Morocco

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Tokyo, a government white paper now tracks overwork the way other countries track inflation — and the number keeps going the wrong direction. In Casablanca, the workday pauses for two hours at noon so everyone can eat lunch properly, and nobody has bothered inventing a term for burnout because the concept arrives, culturally, pre-diluted. Both countries clock a similar legal ceiling on the workweek. Only one treats that ceiling as a starting bid.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn "otsukaresama desu" — you'll say it leaving at 6pm and again at 9pm | Don't be first to pack up while your boss is visibly still at his desk |
| Track your own hours; HR's official log and your lived reality are different documents | Don't assume unused vacation days get noticed, let alone rewarded |
| Accept the occasional after-hours nomikai — real decisions get relitigated there | Don't decline every drinking invitation; it reads as disengagement |
| Ask employers directly about "minashi zangyo" (overtime baked into salary) before signing | Don't expect the 45-hour monthly overtime cap to function as a hard ceiling |
| Use "nemawashi" — informal pre-consultation before meetings — to save hours later | Don't raise disagreements cold in a formal meeting; it embarrasses everyone |
| Escalate to a labor bureau if overtime turns unsafe; the legal system now backs you | Don't assume internal complaints alone will move faster than escalation |

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build your day around the noon-to-2pm closure — offices and patience both shut down | Don't schedule anything urgent between noon and 2pm; it will simply wait |
| Expect roughly six-hour Ramadan days and plan deliverables accordingly | Don't push for normal-tempo output during Ramadan; asking twice won't help |
| Get overtime terms in writing — premiums of 25%–100% are real but enforcement is patchy | Don't assume the 44-hour week or overtime caps enforce themselves |
| Invest in personal relationships with colleagues before requesting flexibility | Don't treat the workplace as purely transactional; trust is the currency |
| Match dress code to client exposure — sharper for customer-facing roles | Don't wear casual or overly revealing clothing in client-facing settings |
| Budget extra time for approvals; decisions often wait on one senior signature | Don't expect flat, fast decision cycles from a hierarchical org chart |

## Japan

According to OECD data, Japan's average annual working hours have declined for years, landing around 1,650–1,700 — below the OECD average of roughly 1,736. On paper, that's a success story; it's also beside the point, since the number that matters in Japan was never the average but the tail. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's overwork white paper, per the Japan Times, recorded 6,212 applications for compensation over work-related death and illness in fiscal 2025 — a record, the fourth straight annual rise — with 1,310 claims approved, most for psychiatric conditions rather than the cardiac events "karoshi" originally described.

The gap between average and extreme is the whole story. Government overtime statistics are famously undercounted: surveys have found employees logging closer to 40 hours of monthly overtime while being paid for barely half, the rest absorbed as unpaid "service overtime" — a term Japan needed badly enough to invent. The 2019 Work Style Reform Act capped overtime at 45 hours a month, strict-sounding until you realize it exists because no cap existed before. Gallup's 2025 State of the Global Workplace found just 8% of Japanese employees actively engaged, against a 20% global average, with disengaged workers outnumbering engaged ones roughly three to one — present at the office, absent from the job.

Hofstede Insights offers a tidy explanatory variable: Japan scores 92 on uncertainty avoidance and 95 on masculinity, its term for achievement-orientation — a combination that rewards visible effort and punishes ambiguity, including the ambiguity of leaving before everyone else decides it's acceptable to leave.

## Morocco

Morocco's labor code sets the standard week at 44 hours — comparable to Japan's statutory framework — spread, per Playroll and Rivermate's regulatory guides, across eight-hour days with a ten-hour daily maximum. Overtime pays 125% to 200% of base wage depending on time of day and rest-day status, with annual overtime capped somewhere between 150 and 250 hours depending which guide you trust — itself a small tell about enforcement consistency, which expat-oriented business guides describe diplomatically as "less than ideal" outside the largest multinationals.

Yet lived experience diverges from the legal text. The 2025 Global Work-Life Balance Index, cited by the Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, ranked Morocco 51st of 60 countries — driven by long hours, low wages, and modest paid leave (25 days) rather than intensity per se. The HCP's overhauled EMO2026 labor survey, expanding its sample to 135,000 households under updated ILO methodology, tracks a labor force topping 11.6 million against 10.8% unemployment — context that matters, since where finding steady work is the harder problem, complaining about its pace is a luxury few voice aloud. Hofstede's framework places Morocco, grouped within the Arab World cluster, at a power distance of roughly 70–80 against Japan's 54, with lower uncertainty avoidance — tolerant of both ambiguity and hierarchy, producing a workplace that moves slowly by design rather than urgently by guilt.

## The Reckoning

Here's the counterintuitive part: Morocco's workers have less legal protection actually enforced and lower wages to show for their hours, yet routinely describe their jobs as less consuming of the self. Japan's workers have one of the most heavily regulated overtime regimes in the developed world and a shrinking average, yet still produce record numbers of stress-related compensation claims. The difference isn't the law; it's what each culture treats overtime as evidence of. In Japan, staying late signals loyalty. In Morocco, per a Hofstede masculinity score of 53 against Japan's 95, achievement is a softer metric, and the noon closure isn't a productivity failure but a scheduled admission that humans need lunch.

The stranger twist: both countries are converging from opposite directions. Japan's labor shortage is forcing employers to loosen overtime rules they only recently tightened, per Bloomberg's reporting, because there aren't enough workers left to overwork. Morocco, meanwhile, is professionalizing fast, importing stricter Western output metrics into a culture that historically treated punctuality as a suggestion. Each is drifting toward the other's discomfort.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News (Ask HN) — a former software engineer who spent a decade in Japan described 70-hour weeks as routine, with peaks near 100 hours during crunch periods, and said vacation days existed on paper but were rarely usable in practice.*

> *Quora — a respondent on Japan's overtime culture described 40 hours of "built-in" unpaid overtime as an unofficial baseline at traditional firms, alongside an expectation of after-work drinking sessions with superiors stretching toward midnight.*

> *Quora — another contributor warned that finding a Japanese employer who genuinely follows overtime-pay law is unusually difficult, noting the vocabulary for work-death and work-suicide exists because the phenomena were common enough to need naming.*

> *Quora — a thread on Moroccan employment characteristics observed that job outcomes there depend heavily on personal networks and who you know, with foreign-language fluency functioning as a near-mandatory gatekeeping skill.*

> *Expat.com's Casablanca work-culture guide, written by a long-term resident, described local office culture as notably unhurried by European standards, with pressure to hit results consistently lower — relaxed to newcomers, a lack of commitment to those expecting Northern European tempo.*

### Conclusion

Neither country has solved work-life balance; they've simply chosen different failure modes. Japan has built an elaborate legal and statistical apparatus to measure overwork precisely because the culture keeps generating it faster than policy can absorb — a nation diagnosing itself in real time. Morocco has looser enforcement and lower wages, but a cultural default that treats the workday as one part of life rather than its organizing principle, for better and for worse. If you're choosing a career purely on work-life balance grounds, the honest question is which exhaustion you'd prefer: the kind with a two-hour lunch, or the kind with a name.
