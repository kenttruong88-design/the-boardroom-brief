# In Nigeria the Boss Is Oga; in the United Kingdom He Is Dave, and Both Will Be Disappointed by Your Candour

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A new managing director in Lagos who invites staff to call him by his first name may find, according to one Nigerian commentator, that his staff decline. A new line manager in Manchester who insists on being addressed as Sir may find that he has become a topic of conversation, in the passive-aggressive register for which the country is known. Management hierarchy is a subject on which the two countries agree about almost nothing except that the person with the title should not be contradicted in front of other people.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles such as Sir, Ma, Doctor or Chief until you are invited to stop | Use a senior colleague's first name in a first meeting, however modern your last employer was |
| Raise concerns with the boss privately and carefully | Criticise a superior in front of others |
| Build relationships with the people who actually decide; some decisions are settled before the meeting | Expect votes or formal consensus in a meeting |
| Show respect to elders and anyone senior to you | Treat a polite "yes" as agreement |
| Frame negative points alongside praise | Deliver blunt feedback in public |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Follow the lead of the person you are speaking to on first names; younger colleagues usually offer theirs immediately | Assume an older professional wants first names before they offer; some expect Mr or Mrs until invited |
| Read understatement and humour as a possible signal of disagreement | Take a mild "interesting idea" as endorsement |
| Expect decisions to arrive as suggestions or guidelines, not orders | Wait to be given an instruction that will never be phrased as one |
| Use indirect language yourself, especially with negative information | Deliver criticism with Nigerian-style softening so heavy that it disappears |
| Ask your line manager for feedback regularly | Assume silence means approval |

## Nigeria

Nigerian workplaces are, by most accounts, steeply hierarchical. The hierarchy is also personal. Expat Arrivals describes management as highly hierarchical, with the boss typically an older man to whom subordinates owe respect, and says a boss is never publicly criticised; concerns belong in a private conversation. A guide for expat professionals on the Navigating Nigeria blog adds that age, title and position earn real deference, that decisions are often made at the top and sometimes before the meeting begins, and that a foreign consultant who used a sixty-year-old chief executive's first name in a first meeting watched a deal collapse.

The cultural data is consistent. The Hofstede dataset used here has no separate row for Nigeria, but its West Africa row scores 77 on power distance and 20 on individualism, against 35 and 89 for the United Kingdom. A Guardian Nigeria opinion piece, drawing on Hofstede, describes a new managing director whose request to be called by his first name was ignored, and a Harvard-educated banker rebuked for doing the same at a board meeting. Employees often use an honorific with a first name, or a job title, as the respectful compromise.

The costs are visible in the surveys. Intelpoint's Nigerian Workplace Report, covering more than 2,600 respondents, ranked bad leadership third among reasons for quitting, behind a better offer elsewhere and lack of career advancement. Some 55.01 percent said their pay does not reflect the value they provide, and 9.74 percent said HR is biased toward management. On pay, 34.99 percent of employed respondents earned up to ₦100,000 a month and 63.47 percent earned ₦200,000 or less. Intelpoint also found that 51.43 percent of workers think HR protects the workplace in a balanced way and 38.84 percent think it protects employees, so the complaint is not that nobody is listening, only that the listening has limits. Hierarchy, in this telling, is not a quirk of manners. It is the structure within which a modest salary is negotiated.

## United Kingdom

British hierarchy is better disguised. Nairametrics, advising Nigerians planning to relocate, notes that UK business culture is less hierarchical than much of Europe, though senior leaders still make the key decisions, often presented as suggestions and reached slowly. Older professionals expect formal address until they extend an invitation, while younger colleagues introduce themselves by first name. Expat Arrivals offers a corrective to the stereotype of the all-powerful oga: decisions do not always flow strictly top-down, relationships matter, and compromises are often reachable. Teamwork towards defined goals is valued above individual initiative, which is a useful thing to know before volunteering a bold idea at the first meeting.

Disagreement frequently travels by understatement or humour, which is efficient for those fluent in it and baffling for those who are not.

The quality of the line manager matters more than the structure. According to the CIPD's 2022 Working Lives analysis, 64 percent of UK employees were satisfied with their job overall, but satisfaction ran from 30 percent under the lowest-rated quarter of managers to 88 percent under the highest-rated quarter. About half of employees under the lowest-scoring managers said work worsened their mental health, against 14 percent under the highest. Trust is rarely complete: only 8 percent of employees sat at the full-trust end of the CIPD's index, and 1 percent at the zero end, which suggests that most Britons live in a comfortable middle of mild suspicion.

The CIPD also reported that just over half of managers say they receive the information and training they need to manage people, and that improving the weakest managers appears to matter more than polishing the best. The more recent news is modestly encouraging. The CIPD's Good Work Index 2025 reports that more employees say their line managers support them than in 2023, particularly on learning and openness about mental health, and that positive views of a line manager are associated with feeling more effective and less inclined to leave. In the public sector, the CIPD found that about two-thirds say their manager helps them perform well or gives useful feedback, and 43 percent trust leaders to act with integrity.

## The Reckoning

The difference is not that one country has a hierarchy and the other does not. Both do. Nigeria displays its hierarchy in forms of address, in who may speak in a meeting and in where disagreement may be aired. Britain conceals it behind first names, flat seating plans and suggestions, which makes it harder to see and therefore easier to offend. The Nigerian professional arriving in the United Kingdom is likely to find that the permission to challenge exists but is exercised through tone, not argument. The British professional arriving in Lagos will find the opposite: the rules are plain, and ignoring them is costly.

On the relationship itself, the evidence favours neither country decisively, because the surveys measure different things. Nigeria's data shows frustration with pay and leadership. Britain's shows that a good line manager transforms the experience and a poor one damages it, in a country with otherwise low drama. The Zikoko accounts suggest the Nigerian grievance is not hierarchy as such but hierarchy used to silence, which is a distinction the British, with their own talent for avoiding awkward conversations, may find familiar. Anyone moving between them should plan to relearn when to speak, to whom, and in what grammatical mood.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Zikoko — Nigerians who left toxic employers describe bosses who paid when they felt like it, yelled and turned colleagues against one another, or removed people from email and Slack without warning. At their new jobs the hierarchy remained, but it no longer meant fear, and people could speak, rest and delegate without guilt.*

> *Nairametrics — A Nigerian columnist argues that "oga" has come to mean unquestioned authority, that questioning a superior is read as disrespect, and that staying silent is therefore the safer path even when the boss is wrong. He calls for servant leadership instead.*

> *The Guardian Nigeria — A commentator says first-name address that is routine in Western workplaces can read as poor upbringing in Nigeria, citing a managing director who was ignored when he asked to be called by his first name and a banker corrected at a board meeting.*

> *Nairametrics — A guide for Nigerians heading to Britain warns that the British are indirect, especially with bad news, and that older colleagues may expect Mr or Mrs until they say otherwise.*

> *Navigating Nigeria blog — A guide for expat professionals says criticism in Nigeria is softened or passed through intermediaries, "yes" may not mean agreement, and the sensible approach is to defer in public and raise concerns privately.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Nigeria, learn the titles, defer in public, and find the real decision-maker before the meeting. If you are moving to the United Kingdom, learn to hear the disagreement in a mild phrase and to ask your line manager directly for feedback, since the structure will not volunteer it.

One caution on the numbers: the CIPD trust index measures what employees think of their managers, not how managers behave, and the Intelpoint sample is a workplace survey of mostly degree-holders, with 69.58 percent holding a bachelor's degree. Neither should be read as a verdict on a whole country, and both are better treated as a prompt to ask sharper questions in an interview.

In both countries the safest policy is to watch how the most senior person addresses the most junior one, and then to copy it carefully. The boss, in either case, will be pleased not to have been asked to explain the rule.
