# Portugal Will Tell You Your Salary Before You Sign; Switzerland Would Prefer You Didn't Ask

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Portugal pays its workers fourteen times a year and, in 2026, set its minimum wage at 920 euros a month. Switzerland pays much more, but treats the figure with the discretion usually reserved for medical records. One country is reluctant to publish what it pays because the number is small. The other is reluctant because the number is nobody's business. For the prospective migrant, both arrive at the same place, a job offer with no visible price tag, by entirely different roads.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether the quoted salary is paid over 12 or 14 months before comparing offers | Treat a monthly figure as comparable to a foreign one without checking the payment schedule |
| Negotiate a gross annual figure, and ask about meal allowance and relocation help | Accept an offer framed only as a "net monthly" number |
| Ask for the range in writing before an interview, even though the law will not make anyone give it | Expect a salary range in the job advert |
| Treat a "self-employed" arrangement for an employee-style role as a question to be answered | Sign a contractor agreement for a role that looks like a staff job |
| Raise housing costs politely and tie the request to growth inside the company | Open with a demanding number, because the market is small and relationships carry weight |

**Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the sector's collective labour agreement for the minimum pay before you name a number | Offer to work below the sector floor, because it is not legal to accept it |
| Offer a salary range and a reasoned justification, since most Swiss candidates prefer to | Fire a single precise figure across the table without evidence |
| Negotiate before you sign, when your leverage is at its highest | Wait until after acceptance to raise pay |
| Ask about the pension scheme, insurance and training budgets, not just base pay | Treat the base salary as the only thing on the table |
| Use salary calculators and recruiter reports to prepare | Quiz colleagues about what they earn |

## Portugal

Portugal is where transparency was supposed to arrive and, at the last minute, stayed in the corridor. The EU Pay Transparency Directive required member states to write its rules into law by 7 June 2026, according to The Portugal News, and it bans employers from asking candidates about previous salaries. Yet the government's draft, as reported by WageIndicator in August, picked the least generous reading of the directive. Candidates would be entitled to a starting pay level or range only before the contract is signed, not in the advert and not before an interview, which is what the unions had requested. WageIndicator notes that 87 percent of workers would prefer to see ranges in job ads. They will see them in the way that most people see their pension: eventually.

The draft does contain some genuine teeth. According to WTW, it lowers the reporting threshold to 50 employees, below the directive's 100, with the first reports from the largest employers due in June 2027. A gender pay gap of 5 percent or more that cannot be objectively justified triggers a joint assessment, with 45 days to produce an action plan. The salary-history ban matters in a country where, as the Instituto Nacional de Estatística figures cited by Boundless show, the average gross salary was 1,741 euros a month in the second quarter of 2025, or 1,368 euros once holiday and Christmas subsidies are excluded. A recruiter who knows your last salary has little reason to better it.

Culturally, the gap between the two is wide. Hofstede Insights scores Portugal at 63 for power distance and 99 for uncertainty avoidance, about as close to a love of predictable rules as a country can score, while Switzerland sits at 34 and 58, with a masculinity score of 70 that rewards achievement over consensus. In Portugal, negotiation therefore tends to be polite, relational and modest in scope. Expect to haggle over the structure of the package, such as the fourteen payments, meal allowances and relocation support, rather than to bargain upward on the headline number. Just do not be surprised if the headline number is the part that is least flexible.

## Switzerland

Switzerland has the higher pay, and the lower appetite for discussing it. This is not a country that lacks the data, only the habit of sharing it, and the habit is stronger than the data. A Michael Page study reported by Organisator.ch found that 30 percent of Swiss employees and applicants asked for a raise in the past year, against 40 percent in Germany and France. Only 66 percent of Swiss respondents felt confident negotiating, compared with 80 percent across the border. Just 19 percent of Swiss jobseekers named a specific target, against 41 percent of Germans, and 58 percent preferred to discuss a range. The Swiss do not refuse to negotiate. They simply prefer to do it in a tone that could be mistaken for agreement.

Pay silence has an institutional pedigree. The Local reported that the Swiss employers' federation argued forced transparency "does not fit with the Swiss model", and colleagues can sit side by side for years without learning what the other earns. A Demoscope survey of 1,004 people for Employes Suisse, covered by Swissinfo, found that almost half of affected employees had no opportunity to discuss their salary with their employer at all. The legal floor is the Gender Equality Act, which, according to JobCloud, requires companies with 100 or more employees to run regular, independently verified pay equity analyses and share the results with staff. Switzerland is also not directly subject to the EU directive, though firms with EU operations are expected to drift towards it.

Cracks are appearing. IamExpat reported that Swisscom plans to add salary ranges to some job postings, as it already does in Latvia and the Netherlands, and that Swiss Post will pilot the idea in Graubünden. Roche and Novartis, meanwhile, will show ranges for American jobs only, which says something about where the pressure comes from. In plain terms, you are allowed to ask in Switzerland, provided you can justify it. Collective labour agreements set pay floors in many sectors, so the room for negotiation depends heavily on your industry, your skills and what the employer can afford. A pitch built on evidence, delivered before you sign, is the dialect.

## The Reckoning

Portugal offers a clearer legal direction and a poorer starting position. The rules are moving towards disclosure, but the money in question is modest, and the government has opted to reveal it as late as the directive allows. Switzerland offers the opposite: a high ceiling, a strong floor in many sectors, and a culture in which nobody will tell you what a comparable colleague earns. Portugal has transparency on a delay. Switzerland has opacity with excellent pay.

For the person actually negotiating, the practical difference is the quality of your information. In Portugal, you will need to ask early, in writing, and fight for the gross annual figure, because the formal protections arrive only at the contract stage. In Switzerland, you must do your homework independently, using sector agreements and salary calculators, since neither the advert nor the colleague will help. If you value a rulebook that is on its way, choose Portugal. If you value a salary that is already there, choose Switzerland, and bring a spreadsheet.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — paraphrased experience: A prospective Porto employee weighing a 2,000 euro net monthly offer was told by commenters to find out whether it was paid over 12 or 14 months, to negotiate a gross annual figure and a relocation package, and to treat a "self-employed" arrangement for an employee-style role as a red flag.*

> *Reddit — paraphrased experience: A physicist with a doctorate, approached by a Zurich aerospace recruiter, planned to ask for more than 150,000 francs and wondered whether that was bold. The one reply called it a lot and put the realistic range at roughly 110,000 to 130,000 francs, a reminder that Swiss confidence has a ceiling.*

> *Blind — paraphrased experience: A candidate with a Lisbon engineering manager offer of 70,000 base plus a 10,000 bonus thought it was low. The reply said European pay is generally lower than expected, particularly in the south, and suggested asking for about 100,000 while conceding that was still a great deal in Portugal.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: A long-term Swiss resident said negotiation is simpler than elsewhere because many sectors have collective agreements with minimum salaries, but those vary by canton and you cannot accept anything below the floor. Management roles sometimes start lower for several months, with raises tied to performance.*

> *SwissForum — paraphrased experience: Newcomers debating whether to negotiate agreed that room depends on the trade, that you should do it before signing because leverage disappears afterwards, and that asking rarely does any harm when the offer is otherwise reasonable.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is a legible, rule-bound salary conversation in the future, Portugal is travelling in the right direction, slowly. Insist on the range in writing and on the annual gross figure, and remember that the law will be your friend only at the point when it is nearly too late to matter. If your priority is a large number, Switzerland delivers it, but you must arrive armed with sector agreements, a range and a calm tone, and you should not expect anyone to confirm whether you have been paid fairly.

Whichever you choose, read the contract for the allowances, the payment schedule and the pension terms before the base figure, because that is where the real differences between the two systems tend to sit. Either way, you will learn your market value the same way: by asking politely, repeatedly and in writing. Nobody in Lisbon or Zurich will mind, mostly because nobody will say.
