# In Tel Aviv They Job-Hop for Sport; In Johannesburg, They Job-Hop to Survive

🇮🇱 Israel · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Israel, changing jobs every two years reads as a strength on a résumé. In South Africa, changing jobs every two years reads as a fact of life, whether the résumé likes it or not. Both countries have all but abandoned the idea that staying put is a virtue, and according to LinkedIn's Workforce Insights, 2026 was supposed to be the year of "job hugging," with global switchers finally settling down now that job-hopping no longer guarantees a pay bump. Nobody appears to have circulated the memo in either Tel Aviv or Johannesburg.

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your starting salary hard — everyone else already did | Wait for a formal review cycle to ask for a raise |
| Treat your personal network as the real job board | Rely on a polished cover letter and a cold application |
| Ask blunt, direct questions in interviews | Mistake chutzpah for rudeness — it isn't |
| Expect your manager to argue with you, not just direct you | Assume seniority buys immunity from being challenged |
| Plan around teammates' reserve-duty absences | Read a sudden departure during miluim season as disloyalty |
| Treat two years as a natural, respectable exit point | Apologize for wanting to move on that early |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get promotion and pay-review promises in writing | Rely on verbal assurances from a manager about "next year" |
| Weigh what a counter-offer would really fix | Assume your employer will match a rival offer once you resign |
| Ask about load-shedding and remote-work contingencies upfront | Assume office hours are fixed regardless of the power grid |
| Build a documented portfolio of results early | Wait for tenure alone to justify a promotion |
| Read relocation and study-bond clauses before signing | Sign a bond without knowing the exact payout if you leave early |
| Ask HR directly how raises track inflation | Assume your salary is quietly keeping pace on its own |

### Israel

Israel's low tolerance for hierarchy is not a personality quirk; it is measurable. Hofstede Insights scores Israel at just 13 on Power Distance, one of the lowest readings anywhere, meaning employees are culturally licensed to question a decision the moment they disagree with it — including the decision to stay in their current job. That instinct has powered the country's self-styled "Startup Nation" identity, though the shine has dulled somewhat: according to Calcalist's Ctech desk, high-tech employment growth slowed to under 2% annually from 2023 onward, down from more than 5% for most of the previous decade, even as exits and funding rounds kept making headlines.

What complicates the picture is military reserve duty. Employers cannot legally dismiss a worker during miluim or for thirty days after, but the law says nothing about denying raises, sidelining someone from a project, or simply making the arrangement uncomfortable enough that the employee resigns first. Ctech has documented cases of employees in customer-facing roles quitting after logging over 200 reserve days in a single year because their employer stopped treating five-day availability as optional. In a labour market already primed to reward movement, that kind of friction just adds another reason to update the CV.

None of this reads as instability to an Israeli recruiter, because loyalty was never really pledged to the company logo. It was pledged to the immediate team, the unit, the mission of the moment — the corporate equivalent of reserve duty itself: intense, bounded, and reassigned without much sentiment attached.

### South Africa

South Africa's job-hopping looks similar from a distance and behaves almost entirely differently up close. Recent payroll-derived data reported by BusinessTech puts average job tenure at roughly three years and one month nationally, but the number collapses by generation: Gen Z workers under 27 average just one year and four months in a role, while Baby Boomers average seven years and one month. That is not a culture of restless ambition so much as a market sorted by desperation and scarcity in unequal measure.

Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace put employee engagement in South Africa at 18%, below the already dismal global average of 21%, with intent to leave running highest in Sub-Saharan Africa generally. A 2016 Kelly staffing survey found 44% of respondents had already held two to three jobs since entering the workforce, and a follow-up study found 80% of employed job seekers planned to leave within the year — figures that sit oddly alongside one of the highest unemployment rates among major economies. The paradox resolves once you account for the skills shortage driving the country's well-documented brain drain: scarce, in-demand professionals can name their price and move accordingly, while everyone else simply cannot find the exit.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the difference is about who holds the leverage, not who holds the loyalty gene. Israeli job-hopping is largely offensive — a bet placed from a position of near-zero unemployment in the sectors that matter, backed by a network-driven hiring culture where nobody expects you to justify a two-year stint. South African job-hopping is largely defensive — a hedge against an economy that has made stability a privilege reserved for whoever already has scarce skills, a company car, or both.

The counterintuitive part is that both countries have arrived at the identical conclusion — that loyalty to an employer is a category error — by walking in opposite directions. Israelis job-hop because the market lets them treat every employer as replaceable. South Africans job-hop because the market has already treated every employee as replaceable, and moving is simply how you find out whether that is temporarily untrue somewhere else.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An engineer who relocated to an Israeli-owned startup described being braced for judgment over five job changes in six years, then watching the interview panel treat it as a credential rather than a red flag; nobody there asked her to explain the gaps she'd been rehearsing excuses for.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about Israeli work ethic explained that what actually drives long hours and hard graft isn't devotion to the company brand at all, but devotion to the immediate team — a distinction an expat manager had misread for months, wondering why loyalty didn't transfer when he moved staff between departments.*

> *Quora — A respondent cautioned that despite South Africa's high job-switching rates, recruiters still quietly penalize anyone with more than two stints under eighteen months on a CV, leaving job-seekers stuck negotiating between market pressure to move and the CV optics of having moved too much.*

> *Expat Focus — A newcomer to Israel wrote about the shock of being on a first-name, WhatsApp-message basis with a company's most senior executive within a week, only to later realize the casualness masked serious performance expectations, and that unadvertised roles filled entirely through personal introductions meant cold applications went nowhere.*

> *Robert Walters Africa's career advice desk noted that professionals who accept a counter-offer to stay after resigning frequently leave anyway within a year, because a bigger number rarely fixes the reason they wanted to leave in the first place — advice several commenters wished they'd been given before, not after, they accepted one.*

### Conclusion

If you are moving for work, the practical takeaway is not "which country is more loyal" — neither is, particularly — but which direction the leverage runs. In Israel, treat your tenure as a bargaining chip you are expected to spend periodically, and build the network that makes the next move possible before you need it. In South Africa, treat every verbal promise as unenforceable until it is written down, and assume a documented track record will matter more than time served, because time served is not what anyone there is actually rewarding.

If a friend asked me over a drink which one to bet a career on, I'd say: go to Israel if you want a market that rewards restlessness, and go to South Africa only if you've already secured the scarce skill that lets restlessness pay off — because there, staying still is rarely a choice so much as a symptom.
