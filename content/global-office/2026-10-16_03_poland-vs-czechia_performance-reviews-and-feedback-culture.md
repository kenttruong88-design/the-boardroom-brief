# Poland Will Tell You What's Wrong With Your Work. Czechia Will Let You Find Out From a Colleague.

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Warsaw, a manager might open a quarterly review by noting, without much preamble, that your report was late and the slide deck was ugly. In Prague, the same manager might tell you everything is "under control," smile, and mention the ugly slide deck to three other colleagues before it ever reaches you directly. Close to half of Czech employees admit they avoid giving critical feedback to their own bosses, according to a 2026 Employee Barometer survey commissioned by Up Benefity and reported by Expats.cz — not because Czechs lack opinions, but because the office has quietly decided that voicing them is bad for one's career. Poland, a few hundred kilometers east, runs on a different operating system entirely, closer to blunt instrument than diplomatic cable. If you are moving for work and your professional ego is allergic to either extreme, read on before you sign anything.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, sometimes blunt feedback on your work and treat it as data, not an attack | Mistake directness for hostility; it rarely is meant that way |
| Get senior sign-off before big decisions — hierarchy still has teeth | Assume a flat org chart just because the office has a ping-pong table |
| Prepare to defend your reasoning out loud in meetings | Expect praise to arrive unprompted; silence usually just means "fine" |
| Ask explicitly which approvals a decision needs before promising a deadline | Skip the chain to "move fast"; it tends to come back to you |
| Build a case with evidence before pushing back on a manager's call | Treat an informal hallway chat as the final word; written sign-off still rules |
| Learn who holds informal technical authority, even off the official chart | Assume seniority is ignorable in casual settings |

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read between the lines; "no issues" may not mean no issues | Expect your boss to volunteer criticism of your work unprompted |
| Build trust slowly before expecting candid feedback in return | Vent frustration only to colleagues and assume it reaches the right ears |
| Create private, low-stakes moments for someone to raise a concern | Ask for honest feedback in a group meeting and expect it on the spot |
| Notice what isn't said in a review as much as what is | Assume agreement in a meeting equals actual buy-in |
| Push gently and more than once if you genuinely need an honest answer | Take one calm "everything is fine" at face value for a big decision |
| Model giving feedback yourself first, since someone has to | Wait for the system to loosen on its own; it moves slowly |

### Poland

Poland's reputation for saying the quiet part out loud is not entirely folklore. Hofstede Insights scores Poland 68 on power distance, well above the United States' 40, which in practice means hierarchy is firmly respected — and that hierarchy earns the standing to be blunt about your work in a way a flatter culture would consider rude. Czechia, worth noting honestly, has no individually published full score on the same instrument, a gap in the data rather than evidence that Czech workplaces are simply flatter. A survey of 300 foreign professionals working in Poland, cited by the cross-cultural training firm Talaera, found 42 percent described workplace interaction as direct against 33 percent who called it indirect, a split suggesting the directness is real but not universal and depends heavily on the specific office. Statistics Poland (GUS) reported average gross wages up 7.5 percent year-on-year in the third quarter of 2025, a tight labor market in which, conveniently for management, candor is cheap: there is usually another candidate, and there is usually another job.

The approval chain is the other half of the story. Decisions a Western European office would let a team lead settle alone often still want a nod from someone upstairs, and that nod can take time; Talaera's research on Polish business culture notes that practices vary enormously company to company, so the only sane move for a new arrival is to ask, explicitly and early, who signs off on what. The reward for this friction is clarity: once a Polish manager has told you what is wrong with your deck, you generally know it, and you are rarely left guessing where you stand.

That clarity has a curious shelf life for the expat manager specifically. Research published in the Journal of World Business, cited by Expats.cz, found that employees rate equally qualified leaders from the United States and Western Europe more favorably than leaders from Poland, Czechia, or Albania, a pattern researchers term the "developed nation advantage." For a newly arrived boss with a Western passport, this bias is flattering at first and expires the moment the local team notices you don't actually know more than they do — at which point, this being Poland, they will tell you so, directly.

### Czechia

Just under half of Czech employees say they do not give their managers critical feedback directly, according to the 2026 Employee Barometer survey from Up Benefity, whose chief executive, Stéphane Nicoletti, told Expats.cz that staff "prefer to keep criticism to themselves or discuss it only with colleagues." Roughly 40 percent believe management filters information to suit itself, and only about one in ten trust leadership almost completely. The feedback loop in a Czech office is less a loop than a one-way valve, with opinions flowing sideways to colleagues and rarely upward to the person who could act on them.

This is not for lack of reasons to speak up, or the income to be complacent about it. The Czech Statistical Office recorded an average gross monthly wage of CZK 52,283 in the fourth quarter of 2025, up 7.4 percent nominally and a real 5.1 percent after inflation, a genuinely strong number. Yet a 2025 Job Satisfaction Index from the research group iSelect, reported by Expats.cz, found only 28 percent of Czech employees confident in their own leadership, while Czechs log roughly 1,766 working hours a year, the third-highest total in the European Union after Greece and Poland. Pay, in short, is rising faster than trust.

Expats.cz traces part of the reticence to a management culture shaped by decades of top-down, mistake-averse administration, in which the safest sentence in any meeting remains some version of "everything is ready and in order." For a manager used to hearing problems named out loud, the quiet can read as agreement. It is usually not agreement. It is more often a problem being carried, carefully, to the hallway, the lunch table, or an anonymous survey box, where it will eventually surface — just not from the person standing in front of you.

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the trade-off is almost mathematically clean. Poland offers clarity at the cost of comfort: you will typically know where you stand within the first ten minutes of a review, and that knowledge will occasionally sting. Czechia offers comfort at the cost of clarity: meetings end pleasantly, voices stay level, and you may spend six months wondering why a project keeps quietly losing resources to a team nobody has, as far as you can tell, criticized. The power-distance scoring cited earlier helps explain the Polish half of this contrast; no comparable figure exists for Czechia, which is a gap worth remembering rather than filling in with a guess.

Neither system is dysfunctional so much as differently calibrated. A Polish review functions as a diagnostic; a Czech review functions as a negotiation conducted mostly in subtext, with the real question — whether you are actually doing fine — handled off the record. If you need to be told things in order to fix them, Warsaw or Kraków will oblige, loudly. If you would rather sit through six months of ambiguous goodwill than ten minutes of direct critique, Prague or Brno will eventually feel like home, provided you learn to read a pause the way Poles read a memo.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Glassdoor — a manager overseeing a U.S.-based team under a Poland-headquartered company describes near-constant micromanagement from the Warsaw side: routine tasks required a formal request before anyone could start, and work was quietly reassigned to Poland-based staff rather than discussed with the people already doing it.*

> *Blind — on a thread about relocating to Prague, one commenter calls the move worthwhile mainly for the lifestyle and the open-source scene, while another warns the novelty fades within a year and that it only pays off once you have stopped caring much about career advancement; the split says as much about expectations as about Czechia itself.*

> *Businesstoday.in — Anirudh Sharma, an Indian banking professional who has spent roughly a decade working in Poland, says the rigid junior-senior hierarchy common in many Indian offices is largely absent there, which he credits with easier cross-team collaboration, a flatness that, per the Do's and Don'ts above, tends to be more social than structural.*

> *Expat.com forum — one contributor who worked a Polish factory job describes a shouting, pressure-heavy floor that wore him down over time, while another, in an office role, says his experience depended almost entirely on the specific employer, since Central European workplaces still range from old-school and demanding to newly "friendly and nurturing" depending on who owns the company.*

> *Expats.cz — Up Benefity's chief executive, discussing the firm's own Employee Barometer survey, suggests the fix for Czech workplace silence is not asking people to speak up but building an environment where speaking up does not feel risky in the first place, advice that applies as much to an incoming foreign manager as to a local one.*

### Conclusion

The practical calculus is this: if your career so far has run on explicit, occasionally uncomfortable feedback, Poland will supply it, loudly and roughly on schedule, inside a culture that still respects a chain of command enough to use it. If you would rather absorb months of ambiguous silence than minutes of direct critique, Czechia's workplace will suit your nervous system better, provided you accept that "fine" is rarely the whole sentence. Either way, budget real time to learn the local subtext: in Warsaw, that means learning whose sign-off actually matters; in Prague, it means learning what a long pause after "no issues" is actually saying.

Pack accordingly: a thicker skin for Poland, a sharper ear for subtext in Czechia. Either way, no one is planning to applaud you for solid work — only the zip code of the silence changes.
