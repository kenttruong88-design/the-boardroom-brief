**In Sweden and Norway, You Can Legally Look Up Your Coworker's Salary. Just Don't Ever, Ever Ask Them About It Directly.**

**🇸🇪 Sweden · 🇳🇴 Norway**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Picture the most aggressively transparent HR policy imaginable, then realize both Sweden and Norway have been running it since before your country had income tax. In Norway, anyone with a verified login can search a neighbor's, boss's, or ex's net income, net wealth, and tax paid — a system dating to 1863 — while Finland, just next door, publishes its figures on a single day every November that locals have nicknamed "National Jealousy Day." And yet negotiate a raise by citing a colleague's publicly searchable salary to their face, in either Sweden or Norway, and you will learn more about Scandinavian social etiquette in thirty seconds than a decade of HR training could teach you. The numbers are public. The conversation is not.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "we," not "I," when describing your achievements in reviews and applications | Lead with individual credit — it reads as un-Swedish self-promotion, not confidence |
| Look up salary benchmarks yourself via Skatteverket or a service like Ratsit before negotiating | Ask a colleague their salary directly over lunch — look it up instead |
| Treat your union's collective agreement as your real negotiating floor | Assume you're negotiating from scratch — a *kollektivavtal* probably already set your range |
| Bring market data and a calm, factual case to a raise conversation | Frame a raise request around what a specific named colleague earns |
| Expect negotiations to feel procedural, even bureaucratic | Expect a dramatic back-and-forth — it's not that kind of negotiation |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your pay to be genuinely, legally public once the annual tax list drops | Be shocked that colleagues (or their curious teenagers) can and will look you up |
| Let a union-negotiated *tariffavtale* anchor your expectations before you ask for more | Open with an ultimatum or an aggressive first offer — it reads as hostile, not confident |
| Negotiate the whole package — pension, holiday pay, flexible hours — not just base salary | Fixate on base salary alone and ignore everything else on the table |
| Expect multiple calm conversations rather than one high-stakes meeting | Expect an answer on the spot — asking for time to review an offer is completely normal |
| Get every agreed number in writing before you celebrate | Treat a verbal yes in a meeting as a done deal |

### Sweden: Radically Public Numbers, Radically Private Conversations

Sweden's income transparency isn't a quirky loophole — it's load-bearing civic infrastructure dating to a freedom-of-information law from 1766, with its modern version codified in 1949. Anyone's declared annual income is a request away from the Swedish Tax Agency, or a few kronor away from private aggregator sites like Ratsit.se, which bundle income data with addresses, ages, and property records. People genuinely use this for salary benchmarking before a review, and local reporting frames it — not entirely verifiably — as a contributor to lower corruption and fairer pay overall.

What that same culture will not do is talk about any of it out loud. Jantelagen, the informal "Law of Jante" that shapes Swedish social conduct, treats individual bragging as outright bad form; career workshops reportedly coach jobseekers to write "we" instead of "I" on applications, and even ABBA, one of the most commercially successful acts in pop history, was reportedly less adored at home than abroad — a professor's summary being that it's "not very Swedish to be so famous." Ironically, Sweden's own government is currently pushing back against the European Union's new Pay Transparency Directive — not because it opposes transparency, which it has had in spades for two and a half centuries, but because it argues the EU's job-evaluation and "equal value" rules clash with Sweden's union-led collective bargaining model. The country that invented searchable salaries is now the one arguing Brussels' version of salary transparency is bureaucratically clumsy.

### Norway: The Searchable Salary, the Un-Searchable Conversation

Norway's version is even more radical, and considerably more personal. The *skatteliste* has existed since 1863, moved online in 2001, and in 2014 stopped being anonymous — the person you look up can now see, refreshed hourly, that you looked. The effect was immediate: search volume, in researcher Ricardo Perez-Truglia's words, "went down like crazy" once lookups were no longer private. One academic analysis of roughly a million searches found about a quarter happened within someone's own household or workplace network, and that the same transparency measurably widened the happiness and life-satisfaction gap between richer and poorer Norwegians — by an estimated 29% and 21% respectively. Knowing exactly how you stack up against your coworker, it turns out, is not universally good for morale.

Negotiation itself runs on a different, calmer register. Collective agreements, or *tariffavtaler*, set real expectation ranges for most roles, unionized or not, and direct confrontation or aggressive first offers are, per relocation guides for incoming professionals, unlikely to land well — the expected posture is patient, factual, and collaborative, often spread across multiple conversations rather than one decisive meeting. One Blind commenter interviewing with Schibsted described Norwegian pay as "much lower than here" with correspondingly higher taxes, while recruiters leaned on "free healthcare" as a selling point that, as the commenter dryly noted, is simply prepaid through those same taxes rather than a bonus on top of them. Hofstede Insights scores both Norway and Sweden near-identically on individualism (around 69 and 71 respectively) and near the bottom globally on power distance — both flat, both egalitarian on paper, with the public tax list arguably the most literal power-distance-flattening device either country has ever built.

### The Reckoning

Here's the genuinely strange part: both countries built the most transparent salary infrastructure on earth specifically so nobody would have to ask each other about money directly. The tax list exists precisely to make the conversation unnecessary — you look it up yourself, silently, rather than asking a colleague to their face, which both cultures treat as faintly aggressive regardless of how publicly available the number already is. A newcomer's instinct is usually the opposite: if the data's public, surely bringing it up is fair game. It is not. Citing a named colleague's income in a negotiation, even one you found through entirely legal channels, reads as a breach of etiquette roughly on par with reading someone's diary and then quoting it back to them.

The other inversion is who actually benefits from all this openness. One relocating Spotify candidate in Stockholm, weighing a 660,000 SEK product-management offer against a $120,000 US salary, found the base number alone a confusing yardstick against a system of union floors, high withholding, and non-cash benefits baked into total compensation — the "openness" doesn't make the comparison easier, just differently complicated. Transparency here was never designed to help you negotiate harder. It was designed, going back centuries, to let the state and the public audit each other. You're just an incidental beneficiary of a very old accountability mechanism, not the target market for it.

### Conclusion

The practical lesson, if you're relocating for work: your salary will be far more knowable here than almost anywhere else on earth, and that knowability will do almost nothing to make negotiating it easier or more direct. Lean on your union agreement, bring calm market data instead of a named colleague's number, and expect the whole process to feel more like filing paperwork than striking a deal. If you're used to negotiating like it's a duel, recalibrate — in Sweden and Norway, the real power move isn't knowing what everyone makes. It's being too polite to ever say so out loud.
