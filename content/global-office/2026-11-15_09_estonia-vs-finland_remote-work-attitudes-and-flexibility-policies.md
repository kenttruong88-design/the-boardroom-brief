# Estonia and Finland Have Both Perfected Working From Home, and Disagree About Whether to Admit It

🇪🇪 Estonia · 🇫🇮 Finland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two neighbours separated by a ferry ride of roughly two hours share a language family, a fondness for saunas and an almost medical aversion to small talk. They also share a digital reputation that suggests the laptop was invented in a Nordic forest. Yet on the question of whether employees should actually be at their desks, Helsinki has settled into a calm, statute-backed shrug, while Tallinn's ministries are still negotiating over who gets a chair. For anyone weighing a move, the difference matters more than the cheaper rent.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Estonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask early which days the team gathers; some Estonian ministries hold kick-off meetings in the office on Mondays and Tuesdays | Assume a remote-friendly sector means a remote-friendly manager |
| Ask whether your role has a desk at all before you pack | Count on a permanent workstation in a government building |
| Use English freely in tech and startup settings, where it is often the working language | Expect the same in a traditional local firm, where Russian may be as common as Estonian |
| Offer a clear output rather than hours on a screen | Mistake a quiet, unhurried office for a lack of ambition |
| Check regional hiring practice if you want to live outside Tallinn | Treat Tallinn as the only address that counts |

### Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer to agree a flexible schedule and location in writing | Assume the right to remote work exists without employer agreement |
| Plan regular in-person contact with your manager and team | Disappear into full-time remote work and hope someone notices |
| Set a hard end to the day at home | Let the kitchen table become a second office |
| Book quiet spaces for calls when you do go in | Take video meetings in the open-plan office |
| Use sick days when ill | Treat remote work as a way to work while unwell |

## Estonia

Estonia sells itself as a country where the state lives in your phone, so it is mildly awkward that the state's own employees are the ones arguing about the office. In a November 2024 interview with ERR News, the secretary general of the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Communications, Ahti Kuningas, said his ministry favours presence, citing collaboration, mentoring and the difficulty of integrating newcomers remotely. Office occupancy there averaged about 90 percent at the start of the week and just over 70 percent across it. In the same piece, the Minister of Regional Affairs and Agriculture, Piret Hartman, argued that motivated staff perform wherever they sit, and noted that four of her five close advisers live outside Tallinn. The government, in other words, contains both positions and a floor plan that cannot seat either. The Superministry building holds 1,356 staff and 980 workstations.

The wider labour market has been more pragmatic than the memos suggest. According to Statistics Estonia, as reported by ERR News in late 2020, nearly a third of employed people worked remotely at the pandemic peak, a record 198,700, before the number fell to about 135,000 as restrictions eased. The split by job was sharp: 82.4 percent in information and communications, 57.2 percent of managers, and only 25.8 percent of office workers. Remote work is a function of what you do, not a national policy. The same agency noted that in 2019 about 7 percent of Estonians usually worked from home, against 5.6 percent across the EU, which is respectable rather than revolutionary.

What Estonia lacks is a statute that tells an employer to be reasonable. Flexibility is negotiated contract by contract, which suits an economy where English-speaking tech firms bid for scarce talent, and suits you less in a sector where nobody has to bid for you. With a population of around 1.37 million, according to the relocation newsletter Relocate Me, Estonian employers recruit abroad often, particularly for experienced tech professionals. They also still prefer local candidates where they can find them, and competition for jobs has risen. The remote-friendly Estonia of the brochure is therefore real, but it is a sector, not a country.

## Finland

Finland, characteristically, wrote it down. According to Mercer, the revised Working Hours Act that took effect on 1 January 2020 lets employees, with their employer's agreement, choose their working hours and workplace for at least half of their normal working time, and widened flexitime to as much as four hours more or less per day. The phrase to notice is "with their employer's agreement". It is a framework for flexibility, not a right to a sofa. The Act also keeps the ordinary limits in place: a 40-hour normal week, or 48 hours including overtime when averaged over four months, with exceptions for senior management and staff whose hours are not monitored. Flexibility, in Finland, arrives with a calculator.

The numbers followed the framework. Eurostat reported that 13.3 percent of Finnish workers usually worked from home in 2018, against 5.2 percent across the EU. Euronews, citing Eurostat's 2025 data, puts the figure at 20.5 percent. Statistics Finland's spring 2021 survey, summarised by Finnwards, found remote work had risen from 22 percent of employees in 2018 to 41 percent, with 91 percent at least relatively pleased and two thirds wanting more of it afterwards. The glow has dimmed since. A 2023 Kantar Public survey for the Federation of Finnish Enterprises, reported by Helsinki Times, found full-time remote work falling from 44 percent to 25 percent in a year, though about half of respondents would still consider leaving a job that banned it, and 54 percent found it harder to separate work from private life.

The Finnish Institute of Occupational Health concluded in May 2025 that hybrid work is not a threat to well-being. Its worry is the small group of fully remote staff whom nobody notices, which is a distinctly Finnish concern: not that you will be watched too closely, but that you will be left entirely alone. Its recommendation is that supervisors identify those employees and meet them in person, which in a country of famously comfortable silences may be the most radical item on any management agenda. The institute also notes that employees keep the right to stay home when ill, including when working remotely, and that remote work should not be used to work while sick.

## The Reckoning

On paper this is a contest between a country that legislated flexibility and one that digitised everything except the seating plan. Hofstede Insights scores Estonia at 40 on power distance versus Finland's 33, and 62 versus 75 on individualism. Both are low-hierarchy and fairly individualist, which is why neither has a culture of surveillance. The practical gap is therefore less about trust than about structure: Finland gives you a legal framework and a mature hybrid norm, while Estonia gives you a conversation with your manager.

If you work in software, either works, and Estonia's smaller scale, English-speaking tech sector and regional hiring make it easier to live somewhere cheap and cold. If you work anywhere else, Finland's norms are more predictable, and its employers have had longer to stop being surprised by laptops. Finland's own survey data adds a caution: the 2023 drop in full-time remote work suggests employers there are drifting toward hybrid, not toward the beach. Estonia's ministries show the same drift in miniature, with the office filling at the start of the week and thinning by Friday. Your mileage will depend more on your team than on your passport, a fact that applies to both countries and to most of the others in this series.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: An American working in Tartu described the local workplace as very relaxed, with little emphasis on getting any specific job done, and found the business atmosphere a bigger adjustment than general culture shock. Many firms, they added, operate in Russian about as much as in Estonian.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: A Dutch expat who moved to Tallinn in 2022 said colleagues, including international ones, were easy to get along with and socialised outside work. Two years on, they were looking for a role with more room to grow and said they were open to remote work or a digital nomad route.*

> *ERR News — paraphrased interview: A ministry secretary general in Tallinn said people are social animals who need other people, and that bringing new staff in remotely is hard. His ministry trialled meeting-free Fridays that staff could work from home.*

> *ERR News — paraphrased interview: A government minister said remote work does not reduce the quality or quantity of output, and conceded that distance can hurt team spirit and team building.*

## Conclusion

Choose Finland if you want flexibility to arrive with paperwork, and Estonia if you want it to arrive with a conversation. In both, the real policy is whatever your direct manager has decided this quarter, so ask for it in writing, ask about desks, and ask how many people sit in the office on a Wednesday.

Both countries have proven that work can happen anywhere. They are still working out where it is allowed to.
