# The Barong Outlasted the Suit: What Manila and America Actually Wear to Work

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Manila, the president sets the dress code by example: since 2022, the national leader has worn a barong tagalog to nearly every formal function, and the embroidered shirt has since displaced the dark suit as the default uniform of the Filipino boardroom. Some Philippine government offices go further than example — a 1998 Malacañang memorandum still on the books assigns specific fabrics to specific ranks on specific days, backed by uniform checkers and a graduated penalty schedule that tops out at sixty days' suspension without pay. In the United States, the country that invented the power suit, 79 percent of employers now describe their dress code as business casual, casual, or simply absent, according to a Randstad US survey reported by FashionUnited, and the closest thing to an enforcement mechanism is a raised eyebrow from Legal. One nation dressed up by decree, with paperwork. The other dressed down by attrition, with none. Neither seems to have noticed the irony.

In the Philippines, dress still functions as a visible marker of respect and rank, and the data backs the folklore. According to Expat.com's guide to Manila's work culture, most offices still run formal Monday through Thursday, with Friday reserved for a dress-down day that is itself often a company-issued polo rather than personal choice. The barong tagalog's rise is not incidental: Inquirer.net Business reports that the garment, woven from jusi or piña fiber, became the default boardroom standard after the president adopted it as his own formal wear in 2022, nudging executives — and by extension, everyone who reports to them — away from the imported dark suit and toward something unmistakably, proudly local.

That formality, though, travels with an asterisk. Bossjob.ph's workplace guides describe a four-tier ladder running from business formal through business professional and business casual down to plain casual, and note that the real rule in most companies is unwritten: BPOs and startups lean toward jeans and sneakers, while client-facing roles and government offices hold the line on tailored shirts and closed shoes. The result is a culture that looks relaxed on a hot afternoon commute and tightens considerably the moment a client, a boss, or a camera enters the room — dress as weather forecasting, in other words, with sudden fronts of formality rolling in without warning.

The 1998 memorandum mentioned above is not a historical curiosity; it is still cited in current compliance guidance, and its logic persists well beyond the specific agency it named. Women in listed ranks are assigned Filipiniana attire on set days; men are assigned barong fabric by seniority, down to which grade gets jusi and which gets a plain polo barong; and violations escalate from a reprimand to a fine of up to a month's salary to a two-month unpaid suspension, adjudicated by something called an Evaluation Committee. A separate 2017 Bureau of Customs memo bans tank tops, distressed denim, micro-minis, leggings, and slippers outright, and still expects "appropriate business attire" even on the designated free day. Transplant that level of codified specificity onto an American HR handbook and it would read as satire. In Manila, it is simply Tuesday.

The United States tells something close to the opposite story, and it has been telling it for a while. The Randstad US figures cited above split current dress codes into roughly a third business casual, a third fully casual, and a fifth with no policy at all, and a separate OfficeTeam survey found that half of senior managers believe their staff now dress markedly less formally than they did five years earlier. Remote work gets a share of the blame; Randstad's own CEO for non-technical staffing points to "open offices, remote work" as structural drivers of the shift, not a passing style trend. Yet the loosening is uneven — the same research shows a third of workers would decline a job offer over a formal dress requirement, while nearly two-thirds of respondents still consider a suit essential for the interview that gets them the job in the first place. Americans, in short, have not abandoned formality; they have simply confined it to the one day that matters and stopped pretending the other days require it.

What the national averages flatten is just how local the rules remain. OfficeTeam's survey still finds managers citing visible skin, tattoos, piercings, and unconventional hair as real violations worth flagging, which suggests "no dress code" functions less as an absence of rules and more as an unwritten one everybody is expected to infer correctly. A law firm and a software startup two blocks apart in the same American city can have functionally opposite norms, and nothing in federal or state law requires either one to say so in writing before a new hire shows up in the wrong outfit on day one.

Here is the part that should embarrass both countries a little: the nation stereotyped abroad as laid-back and tropical maintains more consistent daily office formality than the nation that built the three-piece suit and exported it to the rest of the world. Hofstede's individualism scores — 91 for the United States against 32 for the Philippines — explain the mechanism without excusing the irony: American dress increasingly signals personal comfort and individual choice, while Filipino dress still signals where you sit in a room's hierarchy, which is a harder habit to discard in five years of open-plan offices.

The practical upshot splits cleanly along that same line. A Filipino professional transferring to a mid-sized American company should expect less daily formality than home, not more, and should resist over-dressing out of habit. An American moving to a Manila office should expect the opposite shock: a culture assumed, from a distance, to be beach-casual that actually expects a pressed collar more days than not, with real consequences — sometimes literally a fine — for showing up underdressed in front of the wrong audience.

There is a second irony worth sitting with: the Philippines codifies its dress expectations in memoranda with annexes and enforcement committees, while the United States leaves the same decisions to vibes, Slack culture, and whichever manager happens to be in the room that week. Codification sounds oppressive until you realize it also removes the guesswork; American informality sounds liberating until you realize someone still loses a client meeting over the wrong shoes, with no memo to consult and no one to blame but their own judgment.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tales of the FSO Real Post Report, Manila — one diplomatic household arrived prepared for suits and ties, then learned within weeks that the real local marker of status wasn't the cut of the jacket but the fabric: a barong woven from piña, the pineapple fiber, read as far more formal than any imported wool suit.*

> *Tales of the FSO Real Post Report, Manila — another account described the capital as "much more casual than I'm used to," with embassy colleagues in barongs passing crowds in flip-flops and shorts at the mall minutes later, a split between office formality and street ease that took real adjustment to read correctly.*

> *Expat.com's Philippines forum — a long-term resident recalled that family holiday visits over Christmas and New Year were made in deliberately casual "home clothes," the reverse of the dress-up instinct they'd brought from abroad, and a quiet relief once they understood it wasn't a lapse in manners.*

> *ExpatExchange.com's Philippines archive — a parent living in Alabang learned fast that neighbors and strangers alike will comment directly on your outfit, your weight, or your child's appearance, something that would read as rude back home; once reframed as warmth rather than judgment, it stopped stinging.*

> *A Ling App office-culture post from a Manila-based writer — the author wore a full suit to the office on a day that, with no warning, turned out to be Casual Friday, then got caught underdressed weeks later when executives arrived unannounced in the opposite direction; the conclusion offered was blunt: no employee handbook tells you the whole story.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for anyone making this move in either direction is to pack for the exception, not the rule. If you're headed to the Philippines, bring one well-made barong or tailored piece and treat daily dress as a hierarchy signal that can tighten without notice — the casual tropical stereotype will mislead you the first time a client walks in. If you're headed to the United States, bring one genuinely sharp outfit and otherwise relax; the handbook's dress code almost certainly describes an office that stopped existing around the time remote work did, and overdressing will read as insecurity rather than professionalism.

The one line worth remembering over drinks: the country famous for fiestas dresses more carefully for Tuesday, memo and all, than the country famous for ambition dresses for Monday — pack accordingly, read the room before you read the handbook, and let the other person's office decide, not your assumptions.
