# Dutch Bros CEO Passes Barista Bootcamp; Staff Kept Correcting Her

Christine Barone, chief executive of Dutch Bros, has completed a two-week bootcamp as a barista, including six hands-on shifts in a shop, Fortune reported on November 8. So did the rest of the company's senior leaders. Then they had to pass a test. Those who fail take it again, which is more than most executives can say about a performance review.

![A barista preparing coffee in a cafe](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-25-02-dutch-bros-executives-barista-hero)
*Photo: João Saplak via Pexels*

Dutch Bros calls its baristas "broistas," and the exam, called Flow, covers recipes, company history, shop operations and culture. According to Fortune, the company has about 25,000 broistas, 37,000 employees across the system and 1,273 locations in 27 states. It reported record 2025 revenue of $1.64 billion, up 27.9 percent.

Barone told Fortune that on shift, "people knew I was the CEO, and our teams would always point out when I was doing something wrong." She called that "one of the magical signs of a great culture that I need to make sure I build upon."

Read that again. A chief executive was corrected by junior staff while holding a cup, and described it as a feature. In most offices, the equivalent is a reply-all that gets quietly recalled.

Consider the usual executive "day in the life" on LinkedIn: a sunrise, a stand-up desk, a caption about humility. Barone's version involves six shifts, an exam, and colleagues who do not defer to the title because there is a queue. No one has yet turned it into a carousel, and that is the best thing about it.

The scale makes the exercise harder to dismiss as a stunt. With 1,273 shops, the company cannot rely on a quarterly all-hands and a mission statement to tell it how the drinks are made. It can, however, put the senior team behind the counter for a fortnight and make them wait to be told they were wrong.

She also said the goal is "the ability to think through how someone else will experience something, more so than ourselves." That is the entire pitch for immersion programmes, delivered without a single breakout session.

Nobody has to invent a leadership framework here. The method is to do the job for two weeks, accept the correction, and pass the test. It is not scalable, which is probably why it works.
