# Half of Office Days Are Spent on Zoom, New York Fed Finds

Good news for anyone who sat through a long commute to attend a meeting on a laptop: you were not alone, and now there are statistics.

![Employee on a video call in an open-plan office](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-15-01-office-day-spent-on-zoom-hero)
*Photo: Jack Sparrow via Pexels*

A staff report from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, written by Natalia Emanuel and Emma Harrington, surveyed 1,835 full-time US workers in September. HR Dive reported the findings on November 12 under a headline that deserves a medal: "Remote work from the office? It's more common than you think."

The headline number is about half. On roughly half of in-office days, workers spent the entire day on Zoom calls or doing solo work, per HR Dive's account of the report. That is, to be clear, the same thing they were doing at home, except now with a parking fee.

The reason is not laziness. It is arithmetic. Seven in ten workers had at least one teammate absent from the building on their in-office days, and teams were missing about a third of their members on average. Which means somebody has to dial in. Which means everyone dials in.

Nearly half of respondents said their teams follow a "One Zoom, All Zoom" norm, in which one remote participant converts the entire conference room into a set of individual rectangles. For 37% of workers, in-office collaboration becomes remote because meetings are held online even when everybody is physically in the office. Picture ten colleagues, one floor, ten headsets, and a shared conviction that this is culture.

The authors' conclusion, per HR Dive, is delicate, as befits a central bank: the office has become less effective in delivering on its promise of co-location with colleagues. Translated: you can bring people to the building, but you cannot make them talk to each other.

None of this means offices are useless. Lunch still exists. So do hallway conversations, provided the hallway has not been turned into a phone booth. But the mandates were sold on collaboration, and the survey suggests the collaboration is mostly happening on the same software it was happening on before.

The fix is not complicated. If everyone is in on Tuesday, schedule the meetings that matter for Tuesday and keep them in person. If half the team is remote, accept that the office is a nicer place to take a call from, and stop calling it a culture.

Until then, enjoy the commute. The Wi-Fi is excellent.
