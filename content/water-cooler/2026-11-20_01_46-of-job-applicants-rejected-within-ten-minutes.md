# 46% of Job Applicants Rejected Within Ten Minutes, Survey Finds

The email says your application received careful consideration. The timestamp says it arrived before your tea cooled. According to a JobAssist survey of nearly 7,000 adults in the U.S., U.K., Canada and Australia, reported by HR Dive on November 18, 46% of people who applied for a job in the past year were rejected within ten minutes.

![Job applicant at a laptop reviewing a resume](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-20-01-46-percent-applicants-rejected-ten-minutes-hero)

*Photo: Lukas Blazek via Pexels*

Ten minutes is a long time to wait for a decision, if you are a spreadsheet. It is a short time to read a resume, consider a career, consult a hiring manager, and write a warm sentence about how difficult the decision was.

Applicants have noticed. Of those who got the lightning rejection, 81% believed an automated or AI system turned them down, and 76% did not believe a person meaningfully reviewed their resume. Overall, 68% said a rejection within minutes was too fast to represent a full review. A further 58% said it made them trust "careful consideration" messaging less, which is the correct response to a sentence that has been sent to thousands of people simultaneously.

The reactions are practical. Some 54% changed their resumes after a very fast rejection, rewriting themselves to satisfy a reader that may not be a reader. And 47% said they were less likely to reapply to that company, which makes the speed a little costly for an employer, though the cost will not appear in any dashboard.

Here is the twist. Despite all this, 73% said they would still rather get a quick rejection than wait a week or more with no reply. This is the lowest bar in modern professional life, and the market has cleared it at speed. People are not asking for a thoughtful no. They are asking for a no that arrives before they stop checking their inbox.

One caveat: JobAssist is a job-search vendor, and this desk found no second outlet covering the survey, so treat the figures as one company's reading of its own market. The company's report says a rejection arriving within minutes can leave applicants questioning whether their qualifications were ever reviewed. Applicants appear to be answering that question already.

The fix is not complicated. Send the rejection tomorrow. Let the machine read the application at lightning speed, if it must, and let the email wait until morning so that it looks like someone slept on it. Nobody is fooled. But it is a small courtesy, and the kettle deserves a chance.
