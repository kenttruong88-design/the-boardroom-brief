# Two-Thirds of Office Workers Hack Their Own Workspace, Survey Finds

Somewhere on your floor, someone is sitting on a cushion they brought from home, at a monitor propped on a stack of books, wearing a jacket they keep specifically for the thermostat. They are not eccentric. They are in the majority.

![A makeshift desk setup with a laptop](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-10-02-office-workers-hack-workspace-hero)
*Photo: Ila Bappa Ibrahim via Pexels*

Gensler's 2026 Global Workplace Survey, released in March, polled more than 16,400 office workers across 16 countries. Two-thirds said they "hack" their workspace to make up for gaps in it. One in four had made do-it-yourself fixes for ergonomics, temperature or visual privacy. Gensler is a design firm and therefore has views on the importance of well-designed offices, but the finding is hard to argue with from experience.

The expensive part of the corporate real-estate strategy, it turns out, is being finished by the employees.

The survey also tracked how the week is actually spent. Workers reported spending 55% of a typical week in the office, 18% at home and 26% elsewhere. They spend 39% of their time working alone and 27% collaborating in person. Read that against the usual return-to-office argument, which tends to rest on collaboration. For the average worker, the office is where they sit alone with their own cushion.

Gensler's researchers also found that the 30% of employees it labelled AI power users spent 37% of their workweek working alone, against 42% for late adopters. Janet Pogue McLaurin, Gensler's global director of workplace research, said "we often assume that more technology means less connection". The data suggest otherwise, though the vendor of the data sells offices, so a pinch of salt is permitted.

The same report found AI power users spending 12% of their week learning against 8% for late adopters, and socialising 11% against 9%. It also draws on a dataset of nearly 125,000 respondents collected over two decades, which means that for twenty years people have been telling employers about the thermostat.

The takeaway is practical. Before the next mandate, the next hot-desking scheme, or the next "collaboration hub" with beanbags nobody uses, someone might ask the two-thirds what they have already built for themselves. They have done the discovery phase. It cost nothing and involved a jacket.

Facilities teams, take note: the cheapest consulting engagement you will ever commission is a walk around the floor at 3 p.m.
