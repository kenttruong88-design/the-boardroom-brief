# Loud Working Costs Workers 131 Hours a Year, Survey Says

Quiet quitting had a good run. Its sequel, according to Vice, is "loud working": the unpaid second job of making sure everyone knows about the first. A new survey from the business platform Tide says UK workers spend an average of 2.8 hours a week making sure their efforts get noticed.

![Office worker raising a hand during a meeting](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-08-02-loud-working-self-promotion-hero)
*Photo: Andrea Piacquadio via Pexels*

Tide puts the annual cost at 131 hours, or more than 17 working days. That implies a working year of about 47 weeks, which means even the self-promotion budget respects annual leave.

Seventeen days is a lot of time to spend announcing what you did. It is roughly the length of a long holiday, if the holiday consisted of writing "excited to share" in front of a spreadsheet.

The cost is not just time. Per the survey, 27% of UK respondents have considered leaving a job because of the pressure to promote themselves, and nearly one in ten say they have already quit over it. One in ten. Employees are leaving not because the work is hard but because the publicity is.

The phenomenon is not new, only newly labelled. Vice cites Slack's 2023 State of Work survey of more than 18,000 desk workers, which found they spent 32% of their time on tasks meant to create the appearance of productivity. It also cites Microsoft's 2022 Work Trend Index, in which 87% of employees felt productive while only 12% of leaders had full confidence in their teams. Microsoft called the gap "productivity paranoia," which remains the best two-word description of a Monday status meeting.

Jim Moore of Hamilton Nash told Metro, as Vice reports, that office self-promotion has always existed and has simply been given a new name and a LinkedIn account. He is right. The workplace has always rewarded the person who says "I fixed it" over the person who fixed it. The difference now is that the former has a calendar invite.

The tidy conclusion is that managers should measure results rather than noise. They will not. Noise is easier to count. In the meantime, there is a modest proposal for those 131 hours: spend them doing the work, and mention it once, in a normal voice.
