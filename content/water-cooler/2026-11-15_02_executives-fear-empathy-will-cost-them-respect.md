# Executives Fear Empathy Will Cost Them Respect, Survey Finds

Somewhere in a corner office, a senior leader is rehearsing a sentence that begins "I understand how that feels." He is also checking over his shoulder.

![Manager and employee in conversation at a desk](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-15-02-executives-fear-empathy-hero)
*Photo: Thirdman via Pexels*

That, at least, is the picture painted by Businessolver's 2026 State of Workplace Empathy report on executives, covered by HR Dive on November 12. The benefits technology company surveyed 300 C-suite leaders across six industries, plus 1,000 employees. Businessolver sells software to employers, so read the results with the usual seasoning.

The findings are a small masterclass in corporate anxiety. More than half of CEOs and senior HR leaders expect their decisions to be challenged if they lead with empathy. Roughly four in ten fear that showing it would cost them respect. These are people who run companies, who sign off on budgets measured in millions, and who are apparently worried that being kind in a meeting will be used against them in the next one.

The employee side of the survey, meanwhile, reads like the other half of a very awkward couples therapy session. Two-thirds of employees said they would accept lower pay for a more empathetic employer. Not a nicer logo, not a free kombucha tap. Lower pay, for kindness.

Then there is the number that will be quoted in every slide deck this quarter: 70% of C-suite leaders in toxic cultures reported significant financial growth, compared with 36% in non-toxic ones. Please do not frame it. It is a survey of self-reported perceptions, not a business case for bad behaviour, and Businessolver's own message is the reverse.

"Performance and empathy were never mutually exclusive," said the company's CEO, Jon Shanahan, per HR Dive. It is a sentence that should not need saying, which is, of course, why somebody paid to survey 1,300 people so it could be said.

The report also finds employees worried about AI: 51% say they are concerned about falling behind, and 54% say it has disrupted their career progression. Leaders are under pressure to place winning bets on AI, which is hard to do while also being seen to care.

The takeaway is gentle. If the people at the top are afraid to show they are human, the people below will not be rushing to volunteer. And if your employees would take a pay cut for decency, the cheapest thing on the quarterly agenda is quietly sitting right there, waiting to be approved.
