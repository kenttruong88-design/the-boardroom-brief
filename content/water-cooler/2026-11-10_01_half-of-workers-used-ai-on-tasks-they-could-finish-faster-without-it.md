# Half of Workers Used AI on Tasks They Could Finish Faster Without It

More than half of workers have used artificial intelligence on a task they believed they could have finished faster on their own. That is the headline finding of a JobAssist survey, reported by TechRadar on Monday and summarised by Tech Insider, which put the figure at 52%. The sample size and methodology were not disclosed, so treat the numbers as indicative and the mood as accurate.

The survey's more interesting findings concern what happens next. Some 63% said their employers encourage or expect AI use for at least some tasks, which is the corporate version of "feel free, but we're watching". And 39% said they had highlighted their AI use to managers even though they did not think it improved the result.

Read that again. Nearly four in ten workers have made a point of telling the boss about a tool they did not think helped. This is not adoption. This is theatre, performed to an audience of one, with a slide deck.

It gets quieter. About 31% said they kept using AI after deciding it had improved neither speed nor quality. And 34% said they avoided giving negative feedback about AI because leadership had backed it. Here is the whole workplace in one sentence: the rollout is going brilliantly, because nobody will say otherwise.

Tech Insider cited a line from TechRadar's summary, which is hard to improve on: if employees hesitate to report disappointing results, managers may get an overly favourable account. Managers, for their part, will take that favourable account to the next steering committee, where it will become a case study.

None of this means the tools are useless. Plenty of workers surely found them useful on plenty of tasks. The finding is narrower and funnier. It is that when a company announces a technology with enough enthusiasm, the staff will use it on the stapler if that is what the dashboard rewards.

Management would do well to ask a different question from "are you using it?" Try "did it help?", and then, crucially, survive the answer.

Until then, the most reliable productivity gain in the office remains the oldest one: the meeting that gets cancelled because someone actually read the document.
