# Two-Thirds of Remote Workers Would Consider Office Days for Pay

A new survey has found what every employee already knew and no manager wished to hear: people will consider coming to the office if you pay them. Hubstaff's State of the Workweek 2026 report, based on 1,068 full-time US employees in computer-based jobs, says 67% of remote and hybrid workers would consider spending more time on-site for higher pay, according to Fair Play Talks.

This is a revolutionary finding in the sense that it is the finding of the invention of money. Commuting costs time, fuel and the will to live. The company's proposed compensation for these items has historically been a ping-pong table.

Meanwhile, 45% of fully office-based workers say they would accept a pay cut to work remotely at least some of the time. So the office has its own problem: nearly half the people already inside would pay to leave, and two-thirds of the people outside would need to be paid to enter. As a market, it has price discovery. As a workplace, it has a clearing price.

The mandates are working, after a fashion. Some 59% of remote and hybrid employees were asked or required to spend more time on-site in the past year, and 44% of those surveyed now attend more often. That is attendance. Whether it is engagement is a separate line in the survey, which also found that 58% of office-based workers think their employer sees half or less of their actual working week.

Read that again. Employees are in the building, in the chair, under the fluorescent light, and believe the boss still sees only half of what they do. The commute, it turns out, does not improve visibility. It only improves the quality of the podcast.

Earlier research cited by Fair Play Talks found that 30% of 978 business leaders expected to require five office days a week, yet only 16% thought five days was ideal. Leaders are therefore imposing a policy that most of them do not endorse, which is a management style normally reserved for annual all-hands meetings.

The solution is not complicated and will be ignored. Pay people for the commute, or stop requiring it. Do not offer a "collaboration-enhancing snack wall" as the third option. Employees are not asking for fruit. They are asking for the number to go up, and as of this survey, 67% of them have said so on the record.
