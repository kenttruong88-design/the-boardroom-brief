# 73% Check Email on Vacation. Only 8% Credit It With a Promotion.

You replied to the client from a sunbed. You said "quick one!" and meant it. Nobody noticed except your family and, apparently, the survey.

![Person working on a laptop while on a beach vacation](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-11-02-performative-availability-email-vacation-promoted-hero)
*Photo: Brunxs via Pexels*

Fortune reported Sunday on a Software Finder survey of 1,006 US employees, fielded in June. It found that 73% check work messages on vacation, and 51% have answered a work email while away. Fortune gave no further methodology, and Software Finder is a software-review company, so salt accordingly.

The return on all that availability is thin. Some 69% said after-hours availability has not meaningfully improved their careers. Only 8% credited it with a promotion, and just 12% with a raise or bonus. Meanwhile 40% said it hurt their work-life balance, 38% said it raised their stress, and 32% said it worsened burnout. Eight per cent got promoted. Thirty-two per cent got burnout. Hope that rounds nicely. The other 92% presumably received a warm feeling of being reachable.

Why do it, then? Because 49% worry that setting boundaries could hurt their careers. That's the trap. It runs on guesswork and mutual suspicion, which is also how most office seating plans work. Nobody's required to answer at 9 p.m., but nobody wants to be the first not to.

The survey did find a winner: the always-on crowd. Among them, 57% said availability helped their careers, compared with 8% of boundary-setters, and 17% credited it with a promotion versus 2%. So it works, a bit, for the people already doing it. That's what a loyalty scheme looks like.

Gen Z is pushing back. Some 65% feel pressure to respond after hours, against 72% of millennials and Gen X. Jeff LeBlanc of Bentley University, writing in Fast Company, called the old bargain "performative availability," and wrote that "Gen Z hasn't shown that they don't value hard work."

Deloitte's 2026 survey, which Fortune cited, found 48% of Gen Z feel burned out and only 6% name reaching leadership as their top goal. Turns out the incentive to reply instantly weakens when the prize is being the person who replies instantly.

The honest vacation auto-reply would read: "I am away. I will be reading this anyway. Please do not mention it."
