# UK Workers Spend 131 Hours a Year Announcing Their Own Work, Study Finds

The average UK worker spends 131 hours a year making their work visible, according to research from finance platform Tide reported by Fortune on November 6. That is about 2.8 hours a week, or roughly the length of a long meeting about whether to have fewer meetings.

![Colleague talking loudly in an open-plan office](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-27-01-loud-working-colleagues-hero)
*Photo: RDNE Stock project via Pexels*

The pastime has a name: loud working. Among 25 to 34-year-olds, nearly 70% do it, spending around five hours a week on self-promotion. Fortune's headline calls them Zillennials. They post about their successes at more than three times the rate of the over-55s, who presumably have lunch.

And it works. According to the research, 77% of loud workers had at least one career opportunity in the past year, whether clients, raises or promotions. Some 15% say speaking up won them awards. Another 17% say they were poached or landed a job because of it. The lesson is that attention is a currency, and the workplace is a market in which the coins are adjectives.

Then comes the bill. Some 27% of workers have considered leaving because of loud-working culture or vocal colleagues, and one in ten already has. Among 25 to 34-year-olds, nearly half considered quitting over it and one in five did. IT, telecoms and finance workers are the most weary, which suggests the sectors with the most dashboards are also the ones with the most applause.

So the maths is delightful. One worker spends hours announcing a win, a colleague quietly decides to leave, and the company pays to replace the colleague.

George Schmidt, Tide's CEO for the UK and Europe, was diplomatic. "Loudworking has mixed reactions in the workplace and it's easy to understand that a colleague who turns every small win into an announcement might be off-putting," he said, adding that "there's a difference between being performative and backing yourself." Both halves of that are true, and neither fits in a LinkedIn post, which is why nobody will read it.

A caveat: Fortune's article gives no sample size or method for the Tide research, so treat the percentages as indicative.

The lesson for managers is plain. If good people are quitting over the noise, being heard has begun to matter more than being right. The fix is not to ask anyone to be quieter. It is to notice the quiet ones, so nobody needs a megaphone to get a raise.
