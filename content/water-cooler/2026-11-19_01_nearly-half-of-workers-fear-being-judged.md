# Nearly Half of Workers Fear Being Judged for Not Using AI

Remember when the fear was that AI would take your job? That was last year. This year the fear is that someone will notice you haven't asked it to write your meeting notes.

![Office worker typing on a laptop at a desk](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-19-01-nearly-half-fear-judged-not-using-ai-hero)
*Photo: Ivan S via Pexels*

According to a report from the London School of Economics' Inclusion Initiative and consultancy Protiviti, published this week, 48% of professionals worry about being judged negatively for not using AI enough. Only 20% fear criticism for using it. The study, titled "Beyond the Technology: Who Really Captures AI's Gains at Work", surveyed 3,001 professionals across multiple countries between March and May 2026.

The workplace has, in other words, achieved a rare thing: a technology that is both optional and compulsory. Nobody has to use it. Everybody has to look like they are using it.

Adoption is no longer the question. Around three-quarters of respondents use AI at work, up from 29% in 2024. And it works. Some 82% say it makes tasks faster, 70% say it takes less mental effort, and 62% say it improves quality. The tools are, by the evidence, quite good.

Here is where it gets cheeky. About half of those who save time with AI say they take on new responsibilities or projects. The hour you got back has not been returned to you. It has been reallocated by a manager you never asked.

Matt Duncan of Protiviti said that simply absorbing the gains into existing workloads leaves much of AI's potential untapped. Dr Grace Lordan of LSE said where the time savings go matters as much as their size. Both are polite ways of saying the same thing: if the saved time disappears into the backlog, nobody has really saved anything.

The report also found no consistent link between how well someone performs and how much they use AI or how much training they get. Performance tracked conscientiousness, resilience, confidence in solving problems, and willingness to learn. So the secret to being good at work remains unchanged: be good at work. The prompts are optional.

For the average employee, then, the strategy writes itself. Use AI visibly. Save the time quietly. And when someone asks whether you have capacity for one more small thing, remember that "small" is the most expensive word in the office.

The tools are working. The workload creep is working harder.
