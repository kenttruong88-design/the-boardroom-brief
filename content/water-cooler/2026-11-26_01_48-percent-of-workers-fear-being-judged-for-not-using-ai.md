# 48% of Workers Fear Being Judged for Not Using AI Enough

Nearly half of workers are worried that their colleagues think they are not using AI enough, according to a survey of 3,001 professional employees by the London School of Economics' Inclusion Initiative and Protiviti. The figure is 48 percent. A further 20 percent worry about being judged for using it.

*Photo: fauxels via Pexels*

So the modern office has two anxieties and a shared meeting invite.

The report, the third annual GENERATIONS study, surveyed workers globally between March and May 2026 and was covered by Fair Play Talks on November 24. By generation, millennials were most likely to fear being judged for under-use, at 51 percent. Gen Z and Gen X were both at 47 percent. Baby boomers and older sat at 35 percent, which suggests that experience brings a certain immunity to other people's opinions.

Gen Z, meanwhile, was the generation most worried about the opposite: 27 percent fear being judged for using AI. These are the people who grew up with the technology and who now fear that visible use will be mistaken for laziness. They are right to worry. In many offices, the correct amount of AI use is whatever the person at the next desk is using, plus a little more, but not so much that anyone asks to see the work.

The adoption numbers explain the pressure. The report found that use of AI at work rose from 29 percent in 2024 to 70 percent in 2025 and 74 percent in 2026. Workers saved an average of 19 percent of the working week, and almost three quarters of that saved time was reinvested in additional work. Read that again. The tool gave back a fifth of the week, and the week politely took it.

Training matters too, though the details deserve caution. Fair Play Talks reported that 93 percent of employees who received AI training used AI at work, against 50 percent of those who did not. Training appears to work. So does being told, in plain words, that nobody is keeping score.

Which, apparently, they are. The survey found that nearly half of workers believe someone is.

The practical advice is a short list of things nobody wants to do. Say in public how you use the tools. Admit where they did not help. Stop treating a chatbot subscription like a gym membership and watching who turns up.

Until then, the office will keep running on a quiet performance: one worker types a prompt a little more visibly, another closes the window a little faster, and everybody agrees it is a very productive afternoon.

Ask what your colleagues think of AI. Then ask what they say. Prepare for a gap.
