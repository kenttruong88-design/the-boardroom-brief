# 65% of Workers Say Credit Stealing Is the Office's Worst Ick

FlexJobs asked 2,142 American workers what makes their skin crawl at work, and the winner was not the open-plan kitchen. Sixty-five percent named taking credit for other people's work, according to the job site's Workplace Culture Report, published in September from a survey run August 21 to 30.

*Photo: Yan Krukau via Pexels*

The rest of the podium is a tour of management. Micromanaging came second at 57 percent. Gossiping took 54 percent. Playing favorites got 49 percent, and talking over people in meetings 41 percent. Somewhere in that list is your manager. Possibly all of it.

Further down are the offenses that get their own calendar invites. Meetings that could have been emails annoy 34 percent. Acting differently around senior leadership irritates 33 percent, a polite description of a person with two voices and one raise. Not muting on video calls bothers 21 percent, a number that suggests the other 79 percent have given up.

Then there is the phrase "we're a family." It makes 29 percent of workers cringe, roughly three in ten people who have noticed that families do not usually lay you off by video call. Corporate jargon bothers 17 percent, the same share as mandatory cameras. Both groups are presumably in the "circle back" meeting right now.

One finding should comfort the remote workforce. Asked whether a coworker's video background had ever changed their opinion of that person, 76 percent said no. Of the 24 percent who said yes, two-thirds were negative, and the usual causes were barking pets (28 percent), clutter (25 percent) and children (25 percent). Your messy bookshelf is, statistically, fine. Your dog is the problem.

On oversharing, only 10 percent share quite a bit or almost everything. When people do overshare, the top topic is relationships and dating, at 34 percent, followed by politics at 15 percent. Money managed 4 percent, which says something about what we will discuss with the person at the next desk.

FlexJobs says it promoted the survey through its own social media and newsletters, so the results describe its audience rather than the entire workforce. Fair enough. It is also a group of people who care enough about work to complain about it in detail.

The takeaway is simple. The most disliked behavior in the modern office is not bad Wi-Fi or beige cubicles. It is a colleague saying "my project" about yours. The fix is free, and 65 percent of workers are still waiting for it.
