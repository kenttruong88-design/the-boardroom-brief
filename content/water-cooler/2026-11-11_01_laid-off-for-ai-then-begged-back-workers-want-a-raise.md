# Laid Off for AI, Begged Back, Now Wants a Raise

Somewhere in America, a manager is typing the most awkward email of the quarter: "Hope you're well! Quick question. Would you consider returning?"

![Office worker at a laptop looking thoughtful](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-11-01-laid-off-for-ai-asked-back-wants-raise-hero)
*Photo: Sora Shimazaki via Pexels*

According to a Fortune report published Monday, talent-assessment firm SHL found that 19.1% of US respondents said they had been replaced by AI and then asked to come back. Across the US and UK combined, the figure was 16%. Among 18-to-24-year-olds, it was 24%. Fortune gave no sample size or methodology, and the figures are self-reported, so treat them as a mood with decimals.

The returnees are not coming back at the old price. Of those asked, 51% wanted a substantial pay rise, 42% wanted job-security guarantees in writing, and 40% wanted transparency about how AI is used. Reasonable terms. The tool that replaced them did not negotiate any.

Lucy Beaumont, SHL's global senior vice president of product and design, told Fortune that "organizations are making fast, sometimes myopic, decisions about their people." She added: "The AI doesn't deliver, the gap becomes obvious." The gap, in this case, is a person who knew where everything was.

Robert Half's midyear outlook, cited by Fortune, points the same way. It found 32% of US hiring managers whose firms cut roles because of AI later reinstated the same or similar jobs. The reasons: institutional knowledge (40%), relationship management (39%), rising demand (39%) and more human oversight than expected (38%). Translation: the customers wanted a human, and so did the spreadsheet.

Meanwhile, Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 121,401 announced job cuts citing AI through October, against 54,836 for all of 2025. So the redundancy letter is arriving faster than the return invitation.

Klarna, a company that has talked up its AI customer service, told Fortune human agents were needed "for complex, sensitive cases." Which is a cautious way of saying: some customers have feelings, and some of them are on hold.

What happens now? A new genre of LinkedIn post, presumably: "Thrilled to announce I'm rejoining the team I was laid off from." With a hashtag, naturally, and a photo of the old badge.

The sensible lesson is that "replaced" and "replaceable" are different words. One is an HR plan. The other is a hope.
