# Nearly Half of US Workers Stressed at Work, APA Says

Forty-eight percent of American workers say they typically feel tense or stressed during the workday, up from 41% last year, according to the American Psychological Association's 2026 Work in America survey, as summarised by Blueprint and Stacker.

![Stressed office worker at a desk with a laptop](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-28-01-apa-work-stress-survey-hero)
*Photo: cottonbro studio via Pexels*

That is nearly one colleague in two. Look left, look right. Statistically, someone is clenching a jaw over a spreadsheet.

The survey's other numbers do not improve the mood. Thirty-seven percent say their work environment is hurting their mental health. Thirty-two percent worry about losing their job in the next few weeks or months. Thirty-six percent plan to look for a job elsewhere within a year, which is a plan, not a result, but a plan that tends to be pursued during lunch breaks and on personal laptops.

## The robot in the room

Then there is artificial intelligence. Forty-eight percent of workers worry that it could make some or all of their duties obsolete. Thirty-three percent feel pressure from their employer to use it.

Put those together and you have the modern workplace in two statistics: roughly a third of employees are being told to adopt a tool that roughly half of them suspect is coming for their job. It is the corporate equivalent of being asked to train your replacement, and then being invited to rate the training.

Somewhere, a manager is asking why the AI rollout isn't generating enthusiasm. The survey has provided a partial answer, and it has fewer slide animations than the one the manager is expecting.

## What the stress is for

The survey's authors, via the Blueprint summary, treat rising stress as a warning about burnout. That is the sensible reading. The less sensible, more common reading in management is that stress means people are working hard, and therefore that the organisation is doing well. These two interpretations have coexisted for decades, mostly because only one of them is on the quarterly agenda.

Employers hoping to lift morale will have noticed that "resilience workshop" has not yet appeared in the list of things employees say they want. Job security, clear communication and an honest conversation about AI are not new asks. They are, however, in short supply, and they cost less than the workshop.

In the meantime, the data suggests an easy first step for any leader reading this. Ask your team how they feel about the new tools, then listen to the answer, then avoid scheduling a meeting to discuss the answer. Nobody has ever felt less tense after a meeting about their tension.

The most reassuring finding is that nearly half of workers do not report feeling tense. They are either calm, well-led or very good at the face people make on video calls. Nobody should ask which.
