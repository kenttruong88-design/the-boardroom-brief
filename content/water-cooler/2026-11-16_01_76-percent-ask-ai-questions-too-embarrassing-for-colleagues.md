# 76% of Workers Ask AI Questions Too Embarrassing for Colleagues

Somewhere in your office, a person who has worked there for three years is quietly asking a chatbot how to attach a file to a calendar invite. According to new research from Clutch, reported on November 13, 76% of full-time US employees who regularly use AI have asked it a question they felt too embarrassed to put to a colleague.

![An office worker typing a question on a laptop](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-16-01-76-percent-ask-ai-embarrassing-questions-hero)
*Photo: Theo Decker via Pexels*

The survey, conducted in October, polled 669 workers. It also found that 45% now turn to AI first when they have a workplace question. Forty-three percent say they do the embarrassing asking often, which suggests the question "Is this email passive-aggressive?" is being workshopped with a machine at scale.

This is, in fairness, a rational response. A chatbot does not sigh. It does not say "I sent you that last week." It does not remember that you asked the same thing in March, and it will not tell your manager you did not know what a pivot table was.

But the findings are not all convenience. Fifty-seven percent say AI is creating information silos within their teams, and 76% worry that newer employees are missing the chance to learn from experienced colleagues. That is the subtler cost. The "quick question" was never only about the answer. It was how the new hire discovered that Karen in finance knew everything, and that the third-floor printer must never, under any circumstances, be trusted.

Clutch analyst Hannah Hicklen said, in the report, that AI is absorbing the quick questions that once connected coworkers, and that companies should plan mentorship on purpose rather than assume it will happen. That is sound advice, and also a sentence that would have seemed bizarre in 2019.

Some caveats. The survey covers only people who already use AI regularly, so it says nothing about the person who still asks Karen. The article does not say how the sample was recruited. And the same research found plenty of upside: most respondents said AI gives them faster answers and makes them more independent.

Still, the picture is clear enough. The modern workplace has found a way to remove the most mortifying moment of the working day, which is walking over to someone's desk and saying, "Sorry, this is probably obvious, but."

It has also removed the most useful one, which is when they answer, "Oh, honestly, nobody knows. Let me show you."

Ask a human something this week. Make it a small one. They may be waiting.
