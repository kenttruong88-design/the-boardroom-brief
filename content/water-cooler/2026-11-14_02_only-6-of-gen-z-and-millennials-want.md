# Only 6% of Gen Z and Millennials Want to Be Your Boss

Deloitte's 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey found that only 6% of respondents name reaching a leadership position as their top career goal, according to Fortune. The survey covered more than 22,500 people across 44 countries.

![Young professionals working at laptops in an office](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/water-cooler/2026-11-14-02-gen-z-millennials-leadership-boss-hero)
*Photo: Ivan S via Pexels*

The number is small. The context is why. The same survey found that 48% of Gen Z and 45% of millennials feel burned out, Fortune reported.

Put those together and a picture emerges. Nearly half of the workforce's youngest cohorts are exhausted, and almost none of them want the promotion that, historically, is the reward for being exhausted. They have looked at the job above them and read it correctly. It is the same job, plus meetings, plus the thing that happens at 9pm.

Companies will tell you this is a generational problem. It is closer to a product problem. Management has been offered as a prize for decades, and the buyers have inspected the product.

Fortune also cited a separate June survey of 1,006 US employees by Software Finder, in which 69% said being available after hours had not meaningfully improved their careers, and 8% credited it with a promotion. The bet, then, is not that young workers dislike hard work. Jeff LeBlanc, a lecturer at Bentley University, wrote that Gen Z has not shown that they do not value hard work, as Fortune quoted him. It is that the return on hard work, in the form of a corner office, has stopped looking like a good return.

One imagines the executive recruiters are nervous. The ladder is still there. The rungs are fine, and the view from the top is, apparently, not selling it. The only difficulty is that the people at the bottom have started to ask what is at the top, and the honest answer is a larger calendar.

Some organisations will respond with leadership programmes, a rebrand of "manager" and a pizza party. Others may notice that people are not refusing leadership. They are refusing what leadership currently costs.

Fewer than one in ten of those surveyed named leadership as their top goal. The survey does not say what they would rather have instead, which leaves the rest of us free to guess, and the guesses are not flattering to the corner office.

That leaves a gap at the top and a long queue of people politely holding the door.
