# Waller Wants a December Fed Hike and Leaves Room for Fifty

The Federal Reserve has not yet decided to raise interest rates on December 9, but one of its governors has already described the decision in some detail. Christopher Waller said on November 10, in London, that he favours a quarter-point increase when the Federal Open Market Committee meets on December 8 and 9, and that a half-point move "should be considered" if core inflation stays hot, according to a report from StockAnalysis. Two weeks later, the data he was discussing has not improved and the market has stopped pretending otherwise.

## The governor's conditions

Waller's trigger is specific. According to the report, he would consider the larger move if core consumer prices rise about 0.3 percent or more for a third consecutive month. His assessment of the September data was unadorned: "September's inflation data were bad. There was no silver lining." The Fed's preferred gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, was running at a core rate of 3.1 percent, StockAnalysis reported, a figure TrendForce also cited. The Fed raised rates in September, the first increase in more than three years, and held the target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent on October 28.

That decision was not unanimous. The vote was 10 to 2, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissenting in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise. In central banking, a dissent in favour of acting sooner is the closest thing to a fire alarm that the institution permits.

The minutes, released last week, confirmed the direction of travel. According to TrendForce, almost all participants judged that further increases would likely be appropriate in the months ahead to bring inflation back to 2 percent in a timely way. The committee also discussed financial stability risks, including borrowing for AI infrastructure, opaque financing and hedge fund leverage, which is the kind of list that gets written when a committee would like to be wrong about something.

## What the new data say

October's consumer price report did not help. State Street Global Advisors noted that headline CPI rose 0.4 percent in the month, a tenth above expectations, with core up 0.3 percent. Energy rose 0.7 percent and food 0.5 percent, while shelter rose 0.3 percent. State Street also noted that the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish an official annual inflation rate for October, because the government shutdown prevented release of the October 2025 index data. The Fed is therefore deciding how fast to tighten on a year-on-year figure that does not exist. It is a fitting detail for an institution that must be data dependent in a year when the data are not.

Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee described the data as "distressing" and "borderline disturbing," State Street reported, adding that the assessment was likely shared by others on the committee. The labour market offers little counterweight. TrendForce reported payroll gains averaging about 50,000 a month over the three months through September, with unemployment at 4.2 percent, a combination that argues for caution only if one forgets the inflation numbers.

## The market has made up its mind

Futures pricing reflects a committee with its mind largely made up. Reuters reported on November 16 that CME FedWatch put the probability of a quarter-point hike at the December meeting at about 95 percent, and a half-point move at about 5 percent. State Street goes further and calls a December hike "a done deal," though it does not expect a 50 basis point move. A survey by the New York Fed, cited by TrendForce, found that the median respondent expects one hike in December, another in March 2027 and no cut until early 2028.

The "one hike and done" camp, TrendForce wrote, is engaged in wishful thinking. It is an understandable hope. Investors prefer a central bank that raises rates once, issues a statement about patience and goes home. This committee has used the word "further" too often to qualify.

## Why the size matters

The gap between 25 and 50 basis points looks small and is not. A larger move would signal that the Fed believes its credibility, not merely its inflation target, is under pressure. It would also land on a housing market already in difficulty and on a corporate sector that has borrowed heavily to build data centres. Waller is saying that he would rather do too much than too little. When a governor begins to pre-announce a larger hike, the committee's centre of gravity has moved.

The most likely outcome, on the evidence, is the boring one: a quarter-point increase and a statement that carefully avoids describing it as the last. A central bank that once spent months calling inflation transitory now has a governor calling the data "bad." In macroeconomics, this counts as progress.
