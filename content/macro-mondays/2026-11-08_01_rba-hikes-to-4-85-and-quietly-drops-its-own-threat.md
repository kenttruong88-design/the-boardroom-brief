# RBA Hikes to 4.85% and Quietly Drops Its Own Threat

The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.85% on Tuesday, an 18-year high, in a 6 to 3 vote that Reuters described as surprisingly narrow. It was the fifth increase of 2026. The accompanying statement also deleted a reference to raising rates further if needed, which is the sort of omission that central banks make when they would like to be hawkish in the past tense.

## The arithmetic of a reversal

Five quarter-point moves amount to 125 basis points of tightening this year, which, Reuters noted, more than reverses the 75 basis points of easing delivered in 2025. Put plainly, the bank cut by three-quarters of a point, concluded it had been too optimistic, and then raised by considerably more than it had cut. The September hike to 4.60% was the fourth of the year, according to Newsquawk's preview, so the RBA has been moving at a pace that suggests the committee has stopped treating each meeting as a surprise.

The decision itself was not a surprise. Thirty of 32 economists in a Reuters poll expected a hike, and money markets had priced roughly 88%. What was unexpected was the vote. Three of nine board members preferred to hold, and Governor Michele Bullock said, per Newsquawk's account, that the dissenters placed greater weight on downside risks to growth, saw less acceleration in wages, and were more confident that slower growth would stop inflation expectations from taking root.

The statement said the board "judged that a further tightening in financial conditions is warranted" and would continue to do what it considers necessary to bring inflation sustainably back to target, according to Reuters. Missing was the earlier clause about lifting the cash rate again if required. Markets read the change correctly. The Australian dollar slipped 0.3% to US$0.6979, three-year bond futures fell four ticks to 95.13, and traders moved to price no change at the December meeting.

Central bank guidance is a language in which absence is a sentence. A board that wishes to keep the option of raising rates tends to say so. A board that omits it, after a split vote, with unemployment at a five-year high, is not announcing a pause. It is merely declining to rule one out, which gives it the comfort of denying everything later.

## The economists have views

Katherine Palmer of BlackRock Australia said she expected the RBA to hold from here. Stephen Smith of Deloitte Access Economics warned that the increase adds to the risk that growth will weaken by more than expected, according to Reuters. Westpac, per the Newsquawk review, still regards this as probably the peak of the cycle. When a consensus assembles this quickly around the idea that a central bank has finished, the central bank is rarely comforted.

That is the awkward feature of the RBA's position. It has raised rates five times in a year and still cannot say with confidence that inflation is under control. The bank's own reaction function, as presented, depends on slower growth doing the job that further rate increases would otherwise do. If that happens, the framing will be vindicated. If it does not, the next meeting will require a different form of words and possibly a larger move.

## The global context

Australia is not tightening alone. The Bank of England raised its rate to 4.00% on Thursday, the Federal Reserve held at 3.75% to 4.00% on October 28 with two dissenters wanting a hike, and eurozone inflation reached 4.0% in October. In that company, a 4.85% cash rate looks less like an outlier than the leading edge of a general reassessment of how much inflation is tolerable, and for how long.

What distinguishes the RBA is the tone of its exit signal. Fed and ECB officials are still talking, this week, about the next increase. The RBA has begun talking about the next decision. That may be sensible. It may also be premature, and the evidence on which it will be judged arrives on schedule: with inflation data, labour market data and, in due course, the arithmetic of mortgage repayments at a cash rate not seen since 2008.

Central banks like to say they are data dependent. The RBA has now made that literal, by removing the one sentence that was not.
