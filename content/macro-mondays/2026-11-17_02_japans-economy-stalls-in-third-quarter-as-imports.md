# Japan's Economy Stalls in Third Quarter as Imports Outrun Consumers

Japan's economy stopped growing in the third quarter. The Cabinet Office's preliminary estimate, published on Monday at 8:50 a.m. Tokyo time, put real gross domestic product at a decline of 0.1% on an annualised basis, according to a Zacks analysis syndicated by Yahoo Finance, the first negative reading in four quarters. On a quarter-on-quarter basis the figure was a decline of 0.02%, which RTTNews rounded to "unchanged." Economists had expected growth, with the quarterly consensus at about 0.1%.

## The anatomy of a stall

Second-quarter growth was revised to 0.4%, so the slowdown is real, if modest. The causes are less modest. Private consumption, which accounts for more than half of Japan's GDP, fell 0.3% on the quarter, the second consecutive decline, according to the Zacks report. Imports rose 2.5% while exports fell 0.2%, so net trade subtracted 0.6 percentage points from growth, according to the component table published by Admirals Markets. Consumption by inbound tourists dropped 2.4%.

Business investment was the bright spot, rising 1.6% on the quarter. Housing investment fell 1.1% and public investment fell 1.1%. Admirals' table showed domestic demand contributing 0.6 percentage points, which the drag from net trade cancelled out almost exactly. Admirals also cited Xinhua in pointing to an earthquake in Kumamoto Prefecture in July as a drag on activity.

Prices did not make the picture easier. Nominal GDP rose 0.4% on the quarter, and the GDP deflator rose 2.4% from a year earlier, below the 2.6% that economists had expected, Admirals reported. Japanese households are therefore spending less in a year when the prices of what they buy keep rising, which is not a recipe for a consumption recovery.

## Tightening into a stall

The awkward part is the Bank of Japan. According to Admirals, the central bank held its policy rate at 1.25% on October 30 by an 8-to-1 vote, with Hajime Takata dissenting in favour of 1.50%. The bank has raised rates twice this year, in June and September. A survey published on November 1 found that 83% of economists expect another increase to 1.50% in December, and the BoJ's next meeting is scheduled for December 17 and 18.

Wholesale prices rose 7.1% from a year earlier in October, according to the same source, and underlying inflation has reached the bank's 2% target, in the Zacks report's words, for the first time since the goal was set in 2013. The Bank of Japan therefore faces the classic dilemma of a central bank that has finally found the inflation it sought, in an economy that has just stopped growing.

The economics favours patience, but the currency favours action. The dollar traded at 159.53 yen at the Tokyo close on Monday and touched 160.19 on Friday, its weakest yen level since September 2, according to Admirals. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama has warned against "one-sided, rapid moves." Japan's Ministry of Finance has sold dollars to support the yen several times this year, including operations on April 30, July 30 and 31 and August 3, according to the ministry data reproduced by Admirals. The weaker the yen becomes, the more imported inflation reaches consumers who are already spending less. The interest-rate gap explains much of the pressure: Admirals put Japan's two-year yield at 1.87% against 4.83% in the United States, so holding yen continues to cost investors roughly three percentage points a year. A central bank can narrow that gap by raising its own rate, but only by tightening policy in an economy that has just reported a contraction.

## What officials will say

Nothing in this report requires the Bank of Japan to reverse course, and the officials will say so. They will note that the decline is small, that capital spending is rising and that the earthquake was a one-off. All three claims may be true. The Nikkei fell 2.4% on Monday, to 59,662.02, in a session led by chip-related stocks, with Advantest down 11.1% and Tokyo Electron down 7.1%, according to Admirals, and the 10-year government bond yield was about 2.89%.

The next estimate of third-quarter GDP is due on December 8, nine days before the Bank of Japan meets. If it is revised upward, the central bank will claim vindication. If it is revised down, it will point to the inflation data. A stalled economy and rising prices are exactly the circumstances in which any decision can be justified, and it is the central bank's rare privilege to be right in both directions.
