# US CPI Lands Tuesday With a December Fed Hike Riding on It

The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases October consumer price data on Tuesday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, according to Kiplinger's economic calendar, and the number arrives with an unusually large amount of policy attached. CME FedWatch puts the odds of a quarter-point Fed hike in December at 81%, per Kiplinger, a figure that Yardeni Research said stood at 78% immediately after Friday's jobs report.

## Three forecasts for one number

The first thing to understand about the October print is that no one agrees on what it will say. The Cleveland Fed's inflation nowcast, cited by Yardeni, projects headline CPI up 0.24% on the month and core up 0.21%, with annual headline inflation holding at 3.7%. Barclays economist Pooja Sriram, quoted by Kiplinger, forecasts a 0.2% monthly rise, roughly 40 basis points below September's increase, on a gasoline price decline of about 1%. Trading Economics, by contrast, expects headline to ease to 0.3% from 0.6% in September.

These are not large disagreements. They are the sort of disagreements that a central bank can convert into a policy decision in either direction, which is why they matter. Kiplinger describes September's headline reading of 3.7% annually as having come in "red-hot", driven by higher gas and fuel prices. Economists broadly expect October to moderate because gas prices eased, and expect core inflation to cool after two months of upside surprises.

If that is correct, Tuesday's number will be described as encouraging. It will also be 3.7% annually, according to the nowcast, which Yardeni notes would be the 68th consecutive month in which CPI inflation has exceeded 2.0%. Governments have been known to describe that kind of streak as stability.

## The hike that markets have already booked

The Federal Reserve raised rates in September and paused in October, according to Kiplinger. Minutes of the September meeting, reported by investingLive in early October, said most participants judged another hike likely appropriate by year-end, a cadence the site summarised as waiting in October and hiking in December. Friday's payrolls did nothing to slow the schedule: employers added 165,000 jobs against a consensus of about 65,000, unemployment fell to 4.1% from 4.2%, and average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month, per Trading Economics.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly told CNBC on Friday that the job market "remains stable and solid, but not booming", and that she expects the Fed will have to tighten policy further. Yardeni's own view is that the committee "still needs another 50bps of increases" to reach neutral, which is the sort of observation an analyst makes when no one has to vote on it.

The rest of the labour data was no more soothing. ADP private payrolls rose 86,000 against a consensus of 67,000, per Yardeni, and initial jobless claims were 189,000 last week, with the four-week average of 194,750 the lowest since 1969. The University of Michigan's preliminary November sentiment reading came in at 51.0 against a consensus of 48, according to Trading Economics. A labour market that tight, plus consumers marginally less gloomy, is not a combination that encourages a central bank to cut anything, including its own sentences.

There is a calendar problem, too. Yardeni counts ten Fed speakers this week, among them Goolsbee, Barkin, Waller, Logan, Schmid, Hammack, Daly, Williams and Jefferson, and says they are the first to appear since last week's election results. Kiplinger adds Michelle Bowman and Michael Barr. Taken together they will have more opportunities to comment on a single data point than the data point has decimal places.

## The producer-price footnote

Friday brings producer prices. Yardeni's chart has September PPI up 6.3% on the year, with transportation and warehousing services up 16.0% on soaring diesel costs, and the research firm expects October to run hotter. Trading Economics expects the monthly headline rise to slow to 0.4% from 1.3%. David Payne of The Kiplinger Letter expects annual CPI to end the year at 4% and core at 2.7%.

That is the quiet risk in Tuesday's report. Even an agreeable October leaves producer costs and fuel prices queuing up behind it, which is the economic equivalent of a polite meeting that ends with a longer meeting being scheduled.

For corporate finance teams, the practical question is not whether the Fed hikes in December but whether they have written a budget that survives either answer. Most have. Most budgets, however, also assumed that inflation would be a topic of declining interest by now.
