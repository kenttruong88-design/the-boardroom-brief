# US Adds 165,000 Jobs; Traders Now Price a December Fed Hike

The US economy added 165,000 jobs in October and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%, according to figures released on Friday and reported by Benzinga and Yahoo Finance. Economists had expected a gain of about 65,000 and an unemployment rate of 4.2%. In an economy where the 12-month average gain is only 48,000, the data provided evidence of rather more hiring than usual.

*Photo: Anna Shvets via Pexels*

The prior two months were revised up by a combined 84,000, Benzinga reported. The three-month average gain is now 137,000, against a 12-month average of 48,000, Yahoo noted. Yahoo also put the breakeven rate of job growth, the pace needed to keep unemployment stable, at about 30,000. By that measure, October did not so much beat the bar as clear it with the headroom of a tall man in a low doorway.

## The administration's reading

Kevin Hassett, director of the National Economic Council, said in an interview that "demand for labor is skyrocketing," according to Yahoo. The same Yahoo passage cited initial jobless claims at their lowest since the Second World War and a higher quits rate in September.

An economist reading the same data would suggest a more careful sentence. The three-month average of 137,000 is a meaningful improvement on the year's pace. The 12-month average of 48,000 is a reminder of how low the pace had been. Whether one month is a turning point, or merely an unusually good Friday, is a question the data cannot yet answer, and the usual caution applies: the figures are subject to revision, as the 84,000 upward revision to the prior two months demonstrates.

## The central bank's problem

The Federal Reserve is where good news becomes difficult. According to Benzinga, traders now price roughly an 80% chance of a Fed rate hike in December, up from about 70% before the report. That is not a typographical error: the market debate is no longer about when the Fed cuts but whether it raises.

![Federal Reserve building in Washington](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/macro-mondays/2026-11-07-02-us-adds-165000-jobs-fed-hike-body)
*Photo: Mark Stebnicki via Pexels*

Treasury yields moved accordingly. The 10-year yield rebounded to 4.86% after the data, having closed Thursday at 4.84%, its lowest since early September. The 2-year stood at 4.52% and the 30-year at 5.26%, Benzinga reported. A curve with that shape says the bond market sees a Fed that will not be able to relax any time soon, and a government that will be financing itself at expensive rates for the foreseeable future.

Wages are part of this calculation. Yahoo Finance's Jennifer Schonberger noted that wages had caught up with inflation, which grew 3.1%, a statement that is encouraging in direction and modest in ambition. Inflation is not the only reason for the Fed's caution; the data show a labour market that is neither collapsing nor overheating, which is the condition in which central banks are most tempted to fiddle.

## Sentiment, and what the consumer thinks

The University of Michigan's preliminary November consumer sentiment index rose to 51.0 from 48.0, comfortably above forecasts, Benzinga reported. Year-ahead inflation expectations fell to 4.3% from 4.7%, the lowest since August. Survey director Joanne Hsu said that "despite this month's improvement, sentiment is down 10% from February," and that consumer comments revealed anxiety about a possible AI bubble.

That anxiety is not unreasonable given the week's markets. The Nasdaq 100 had its worst session since March 2020 on Thursday, and the jobs report arrived on a day when equity investors were trying to decide whether a strong economy was a comfort or a threat. The answer, in the bond market's view, was the latter: strong jobs mean higher rates for longer.

## The conclusion

October's figures contain a useful discipline for anyone drawing conclusions from them. One strong month does not undo the previous year's weakness, and one weak year does not preclude a strong quarter. What it does do is remove the easy narrative. The labour market has not broken, inflation has not gone away, and the Fed's next move is, according to the market, more likely to be up than down.

Governments tend to describe a result like this as "resilience." The central bank, which has to do something about it, is likely to describe it as "data dependence," which is the polite phrase for waiting until you know what you are dealing with. The next opportunity to find out arrives in December.
