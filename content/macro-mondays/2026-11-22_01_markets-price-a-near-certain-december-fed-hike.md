# Markets Price a Near-Certain December Fed Hike Into a Stock Correction

Financial markets are now pricing a near-certain 25 basis point interest rate increase at the Federal Reserve's December meeting, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool, as cited by Reuters on Friday. Schwab put the probability at 92%. The same session saw the S&P 500 fall 3.70% into correction territory. A central bank that has been trying to tighten financial conditions is about to discover how much of that work the market is willing to do on its own.

## What the Fed said it wanted

The relevant background is the minutes of the October 27-28 meeting, which the Fed's website lists as last updated on November 18. The committee voted 10-2 and left the target range at 3.75% to 4%. The two dissenters, Governors Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, both preferred a quarter-point increase. The minutes say that almost all participants judged that further action to raise the target range would likely be appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 2% objective.

The staff review explains why. It said that inflation remained elevated: total personal consumption expenditures inflation rose to 3.6% in September, and the core measure edged up to 3.1%. The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2% in September, but the staff described labor market conditions as broadly stable with some signs of gradual tightening, and real GDP was expanding at a solid pace. This is the profile of an economy in which the central bank has a clear mandate problem on one side and very little evidence of a collapse on the other.

Reuters reported on Friday that Cook, who dissented in October in favor of a hike, said the central bank's policy rate is not high enough to deal with inflation levels. It added that her remarks followed a hotter-than-expected October inflation report last week. The committee's one consistent message since the autumn has been that it intends to raise rates again, and the Desk survey described in the minutes expected a hike in December followed by another in March 2027.

## The stock market as an unelected policymaker

Monetary policy works through financial conditions, and equity prices are among the more visible of those conditions. A falling stock market reduces household wealth, lifts the cost of raising capital, and discourages the expansion that the Fed's tightening is meant to restrain. On Friday, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% and the S&P 500 3.70%, Reuters reported, and Schwab said bank shares also fell, with Citigroup down 4%. The VIX closed above 37.

It would be a mistake to read this as the Fed's design. The central bank did not aim to engineer a correction in AI-related equities, and the minutes suggest it saw the risk coming: almost all participants commented on risks associated with a scenario in which the returns on AI investment turned out to be disappointing to investors. That is a rare event, an institution identifying a problem in writing and then getting a front-row seat to it.

Axios, reporting on the same session, cited fears that AI spending is unsustainable and credit is tightening among the reasons for the sell-off. If credit conditions tighten because of the market rather than the Fed, officials face a measurement problem. Raising rates when financial conditions have already tightened on their own risks over-delivering the tightening. Declining to raise them when inflation is 3.6% risks under-delivering. The committee has to decide which error it prefers.

## The unhelpful arithmetic

Kevin Gordon, head of macro research at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, described the setting for stocks as a "rock-and-a-hard-place backdrop." The same phrase could apply to the Fed. Inflation that is above target gives it a reason to move. A market that has lost more than 10% from its peak gives it a reason to hesitate. Neither reason cancels the other.

Historical comparisons are tempting and mostly unhelpful, because the shocks are different. What matters is the transmission. A fall in equity prices of this size tends to feed into spending slowly, through confidence and wealth effects, while inflation data arrive monthly and have been moving the wrong way. Officials will therefore have the October price report in one hand and a screen of red numbers in the other, and only one of those documents has a statutory mandate attached.

The next decision comes at the December 8-9 meeting. Between now and then, officials will receive another set of labor and inflation data, and the stock market will have a further two weeks to make its own case. If it continues to fall, the debate will shift from whether the Fed should hike to whether it can afford to say so.

## The government's description of the situation

Central banks have a particular way of describing awkward developments. The minutes call inflation "elevated" and labor conditions "broadly stable." Neither is wrong. Both are calibrated to avoid starting an argument. The market's own description arrived on Friday in the form of a 3.70% fall, which is less diplomatic but admirably clear.

The honest reading is that the Fed set out to restrict demand and has now acquired an assistant in the form of a falling stock market. Assistants are helpful until they take initiative. The institution that wanted to tighten conditions in a controlled way now has to decide what to do about tightening it did not schedule.
