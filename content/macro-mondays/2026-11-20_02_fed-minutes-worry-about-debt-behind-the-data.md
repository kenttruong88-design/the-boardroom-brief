# Fed Minutes Worry About Debt Behind the Data Centres

The Federal Reserve's minutes from its October 28 meeting, published on Wednesday, November 18, show officials holding rates steady by a 10-2 vote while spending a surprising amount of time worrying about how the artificial-intelligence boom is being paid for. The committee kept its target range at 3.75% to 4%, with interest on reserve balances at 3.90%. Governors Cook and Hammack dissented in favor of a 25-basis-point increase, according to the Federal Reserve's own minutes.

Much of the early coverage focused on the question of a larger December move, which this desk has covered separately. The newer material sits in the financial-stability discussion, where staff and most participants flagged rapid growth in AI-related debt, "much of it" arranged through complex and opaque financing, per the minutes. Credit spreads widened, particularly on high-yield bonds that finance data-center construction. Many participants also saw the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk.

## A boom with a funding problem

The distinction matters. An investment boom paid for out of retained profits is a story about productivity. One paid for by borrowing, through structures that regulators cannot easily see, is a story about leverage, and leverage has a long record of behaving well until it does not. The minutes do not claim a crisis is at hand. They record that the people responsible for financial stability have noticed where the money is coming from and would prefer to understand it better.

There is an inflation angle as well. Data-center demand, according to the Breitbart report on the minutes, is straining supplies and pushing up the prices of some consumer electronics. The report is secondary, and this column cites it only for that detail. If the boom raises prices for the goods it needs, then it adds to inflation on the way up, and the Fed has to tighten against it. If it falters, the debt becomes the problem. This is the sort of thing economists call two-sided risk, and everyone else calls a bad week.

## Elsewhere, an old worry in a new currency

The minutes also recorded concern about sovereign bonds. The spread between French and German 10-year yields reached its highest level since 2011 as French yields rose sharply. Some participants worried that high government debt, rising long-term yields, and limited fiscal room would make it harder for governments to absorb adverse shocks.

In other words, officials of the world's most powerful central bank looked at Paris and Frankfurt and noted that one of them is paying considerably more to borrow. They did not name a villain. They did not need to.

## What the market made of it

The market's reaction the next day was not gentle, though it would be a stretch to blame the minutes alone. On Thursday, November 19, the S&P 500 fell 2.00% and the Dow lost about 1,100 points, according to the AP and Motley Fool, with the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.05% against 5.07% a day earlier. The yield barely moved, which means investors did not read the minutes as a rate shock. They read them, if they read them at all, as a reminder that the funding of the AI buildout is a question with a debt answer, and that the answer is not something the Fed can be expected to underwrite.

## Still pointed toward a hike

None of this softens the policy message. Almost all participants judged that further rate increases were likely to be appropriate, and a couple said a move larger than 25 basis points could be considered. The median longer-run estimate of the policy rate rose to about 3.4%, which means the committee's picture of "normal" has shifted upward again. The next meeting is on December 8 and 9.

The minutes thus contain a rare double warning. Officials expect to raise rates because inflation is still too high, and they are worried about what higher rates will do to a corner of the credit market that has grown quickly in the shadows. A central bank can do a great deal. It cannot easily tighten policy and protect the borrowers who made the tightening necessary.

What happens next will depend on which worry arrives first. If the data stay hot, the Fed hikes, and the AI-debt problem gets harder. If the AI-debt problem erupts, the Fed's room to hike shrinks. Seen from the committee room, the plan is to keep an eye on both, and the minutes suggest that is exactly what they are doing.
