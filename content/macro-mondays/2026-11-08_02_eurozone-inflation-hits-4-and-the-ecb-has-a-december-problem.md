# Eurozone Inflation Hits 4% and the ECB Has a December Problem

Eurozone inflation rose to 4.0% in October from 3.8% in September, Eurostat's flash estimate showed on Wednesday, matching forecasts and reaching the highest level since September 2023, according to Euronews. Prices rose 0.4% on the month. The European Central Bank, which held its deposit rate at 2.5% on October 29, now has six weeks to decide how to describe a problem it has been describing as manageable.

## Energy does the work

The culprit is not subtle. Energy prices rose 20.8% over the year in October, up from 18.9% in September, and 1.4% on the month, Euronews reported. Energy carries a weight of roughly 9% in the index and contributed about 1.9 percentage points of the 4.0% headline. Strip it out and the picture is less alarming: core inflation held at 2.5%, as forecast, and services inflation edged up to 3.3% from 3.2%. Food, alcohol and tobacco rose 1.6%, from 1.3%.

Analysts at investingLive observed that soaring energy prices were putting the ECB in a tight spot as stagflation risks crept in. That is a fair summary and an unusually polite one. The ECB's mandate is price stability. It does not get to choose whether the instability arrives through wages, which it can influence, or through a barrel of oil, which it cannot.

The headline average conceals a wide spread. Italy had the largest jump, with inflation at 5.6% from 4.2%. Lithuania was highest at 6.1%, followed by Bulgaria at 6.0%. France came in at 3.8% from 3.4%, and Portugal at 3.9%. At the other end, Malta recorded 2.3% and Germany 2.8%, down from 3.3%, Euronews said, after a fuel tax cut took effect on October 1.

The German figure deserves a moment. The largest economy in the currency union lowered its inflation rate by cutting a tax, which is a legitimate policy and also a reminder that fiscal choices are now moving the euro-area average in a direction the central bank would otherwise have to buy with interest rates. A currency union of this many countries cannot easily have twenty fuel tax regimes and one monetary policy and expect the two to agree.

## What the ECB has already done

The deposit rate stands at 2.5% after increases in June and September, according to Euronews. At the October 29 meeting, President Christine Lagarde said policymakers discussed another increase but agreed unanimously to wait for more information. The next meeting is on December 16 and 17, when new staff projections will be published, and markets assign about an 84% probability to a 25 basis point increase. Analysts at Pantheon, cited by Euronews, expect that hike plus another in March 2027.

Nicola Nobile, chief Italy economist at Oxford Economics, said the print adds to the already strong case for an ECB rate hike in December. The case is strong enough that the relevant question has become whether the bank can still describe its position as an assessment of incoming data or must admit that it is following a path.

Officials have started telegraphing their worry. Philip Lane, the ECB's chief economist, said on Friday that energy inflation would feed into wages and prices, and pointed to euro-area producer prices rising 11.1% in September, according to Reuters coverage carried by TMGM. Lagarde said on Thursday that the energy shock and artificial intelligence were pulling the economy in opposite directions, and that data before December would decide.

That is the central banker's dilemma in miniature. The input costs are rising, which pushes inflation up and growth down. The technology investment is rising, which may push productivity up and prices down. Neither effect is measured with precision. A central bank must set a single interest rate on the strength of both.

## A transition, meanwhile

Euronews also noted that Lagarde is due to step down in February 2027. Her successor will inherit the policy rate, the staff projections and whatever decision December produces.

For now, the data are unambiguous and the response is not. Inflation at 4.0% with core at 2.5% is a number that tells the central bank two things at once. The first is that the underlying economy is not overheating. The second is that nobody told the energy markets. Policymakers get to choose which fact they act upon, and the minutes will explain, in due course, why they chose the one they did.
