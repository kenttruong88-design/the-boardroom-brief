# October PCE Hits 3.8% as Medicare Rebate Flatters Incomes

The Fed's preferred measure of inflation rose 3.8% in October from a year earlier, up from 3.6% in September, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Excluding food and energy, the core price index was 3.3% higher than a year ago, against 3.1% the month before and 3.2% that economists had expected, investingLive reported. Both measures rose 0.3% on the month.

The release, published on 25 November, is worth reading for what it says about incomes and spending as much as for its inflation figures. Personal income rose by $201.8 billion, or 0.7%, well ahead of the 0.4% economists had forecast. Spending rose 0.3%, short of the 0.4% expected. After adjusting for prices, spending was essentially unchanged: the BEA put the change in real consumer spending at a decline of $5.8 billion, which it rounds to zero.

## An income gain with a footnote

The footnote is the interesting part. The BEA said the increase in government social benefits was led by one-time payments associated with the Medicare Improvement Fund premium rebate. Compensation and dividend income also contributed. Disposable income rose 0.8%, and real disposable income, which had fallen 0.2% in September, rose 0.5%.

A rebate is a perfectly respectable thing to receive. As a measure of the underlying strength of household finances, it is rather less respectable. The people who got it are, one assumes, grateful. They have not, on the evidence of the spending figure, rushed to the shops. The saving rate stood at 4.1% of disposable income, and personal saving reached $1.00 trillion, according to the BEA.

Economists have a technical term for income that arrives once and is saved: not a trend. Readers are encouraged to adopt it before the next set of forecasts is circulated.

## Inflation: the monthly number slowed, the annual one did not

On the month, the headline index rose 0.3% after 0.5% in September. On the year, it climbed, because the months being dropped from the comparison were milder than the ones being added. This is how inflation can slow and worsen in the same sentence, a feature of annual rates that officials describe with the patience of those who must explain it regularly.

Core goods were the firm spot. investingLive reported that core goods prices rose about 0.4% on the month, the strongest since February. Current-dollar spending rose $63.8 billion, of which $11.8 billion was goods and $52.1 billion services, per the BEA.

The report was expected. Oxford Economics had published a nowcast on 16 November predicting that headline PCE inflation would rise to 3.8% in October, up from 3.6% in September, and that high energy prices were bleeding into core inflation. The estimate arrived at the right number nine days before the official release, which rather undermines the case for waiting for official releases, a case the BEA is professionally obliged to defend.

It is also worth recording how quickly the information travelled. The October figure reached forecasters in advance, reached the market on release and will reach households, in the form of higher prices at the shops, at roughly the speed of the monthly bill.

## What the Fed will read into it

The policy implication is not subtle. Oxford Economics said it expected a 25 basis point rate increase at the December meeting and thought markets were overpricing the odds of a 50 basis point move. Fox Business reported in October that the Fed, led by Chair Kevin Warsh, held rates at its 28 October meeting with two dissents in favour of a hike, and that a December increase would take the target range to 4% to 4.25%.

Nothing in the October data makes that outcome less likely. Core inflation of 3.3% sits some distance above any definition of target. Real spending has stopped growing. Households have, for two months in a row, declined to behave as though prices are anyone's friend.

The tension for policymakers is that the data are consistent with both a slowing consumer and a persistent inflation problem. A central bank can raise rates in response to the second without being entirely sure about the first. It is the sort of decision that is easier to defend in minutes than to explain at a dinner table.

The next release, covering November, is scheduled for 23 December, by which time the rebate will have been spent, saved or forgotten, and the inflation rate will have reasserted its claim to being the main character.
