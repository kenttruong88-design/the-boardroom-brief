# German Ifo Index Rises to 90.6, but Factories Lose Momentum

Germany's ifo Business Climate Index rose to 90.6 in November from a revised 90.1 in October, the seventh consecutive monthly gain, Reuters reported from Berlin on Tuesday, November 24. A Reuters poll of economists had expected 90.5. The survey, which asks German firms how they rate their present business and what they expect from the months ahead, thus beat the consensus by a tenth of a point, a margin that in most other disciplines would be called a rounding error and in macroeconomics is called momentum.

## The headline and its footnote

The two components moved in the same direction. The assessment of current conditions rose to 89.4 from 88.8, and expectations rose to 91.9 from 91.5, according to Reuters and Investing.com. Expectations were just under the 92.0 that economists had forecast. Some other outlets quoted slightly different consensus figures for the current assessment, so I will say only that firms were more satisfied with their present business than a month ago.

Clemens Fuest, the president of the ifo Institute, said as much and then qualified it. "The recovery of the German economy is proceeding, but it lacks breadth," he said, according to Reuters as carried by RTE. The sentence works as a complete economic outlook: a recovery that exists, and an economy that has not fully joined it.

The breadth problem is visible in the sector breakdown. Reuters reported that the index fell in manufacturing and construction and rose in services and trade. ActionForex, which published the balances, put manufacturing at minus 1.2 in November from plus 0.9 in October, and services at plus 2.4 from minus 1.2. I have seen only one source for those specific balances, so treat the decimals with appropriate suspicion and the direction with more confidence. In plain terms, the sector Germany is known for is the one that lost ground this month, and the sector it is less known for is carrying the survey.

## An upswing with an asterisk

The professional optimists are, nevertheless, being optimistic. Dirk Schumacher, chief economist at the state development bank KfW, told AFP that the trend remains upward and that "the recovery is proving to be very robust." He added: "Risks obviously remain. But it would now take a significant shock to derail the recovery." That is the type of sentence economists write when they have decided to be reassuring and also to be quoted accurately in the event of a shock.

Jens-Oliver Niklasch of LBBW was more modest in the same AFP report, describing an economy "only edging upwards in tiny steps." The two descriptions are compatible: a robust recovery proceeding in tiny steps is a recovery with excellent posture.

## The hard data are more modest

The survey describes a mood. Destatis, the federal statistics office, supplied the measurement on October 30: German GDP grew 0.2 percent in the third quarter from the second and 1.3 percent from a year earlier, price-adjusted. Consumption edged up, investment fell and export growth slowed. A more detailed breakdown of the third quarter was scheduled for November 25, and I could not find it published as of this writing, so I will not guess at what it says.

A seventh monthly improvement in sentiment sitting beside it is a reminder that surveys measure what businesses expect, and national accounts measure what they have done, and the two are allowed to differ for a while.

The flash purchasing managers' surveys on Monday, according to a single source that cites S&P Global, put the German composite index at 55.0, up from 53.4 in October and the highest since March 2022, though France was reported to be contracting. Chris Williamson of S&P Global was quoted describing "steady growth" that "masks worrying divergences." That phrase would work as the epigraph of this month's ifo survey.

## The central bank's view

The eurozone's central bank has its own opinion about whether the recovery is too much of a good thing. In an interview with the Japanese newspaper Yomiuri Shimbun conducted on November 4 and published on November 7, ECB President Christine Lagarde said that "it would take significant changes for the direction of travel to be different from what I expect," which the report that carried it read as a signal that a December rate increase is the expected course. The interview was reported by a Danish legal information site and I have not been able to confirm it elsewhere. The same report put the ECB's deposit rate at 2.50 percent after a September rise and eurozone flash inflation for October at 4.0 percent, with energy prices up 20.8 percent and core inflation at 2.5 percent.

If those figures are right, the ECB is watching a recovery of modest depth and an energy-driven price rise that is not a German problem alone. That is a delicate position. It tightens policy into an economy whose manufacturing sector just lost ground, in the hope that the services sector does not notice.

## The verdict

The ifo index rises, and the ifo president says it lacks breadth, and both statements are accurate. The economy is recovering in the places that report to surveys with the most enthusiasm and hesitating in the places that make things. The manufacturing sector now has a month to decide whether to rejoin it. Germany has an economy that is getting better. It is simply not yet getting better everywhere at once, which, for a country that prizes order, must feel like an administrative failure.
