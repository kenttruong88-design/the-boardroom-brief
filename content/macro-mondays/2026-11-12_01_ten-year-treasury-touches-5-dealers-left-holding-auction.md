# Ten-Year Treasury Touches 5% and Dealers Are Left Holding the Auction

The yield on the 10-year US Treasury note crossed 5% intraday on Tuesday after the Labor Department reported that consumer prices rose faster than forecast in October. It then eased to 4.992%, up 13 basis points on the day, according to market reports. The 30-year yield rose to 5.352%, and the two-year, the maturity most sensitive to the Federal Reserve, rose 18 basis points to 4.736%, the largest one-day increase since April 2025.

The inflation report was the match. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October against a forecast of 0.3%, following a 0.6% rise in September, while the core index, which excludes food and energy, rose 0.3% against an expected 0.2%. A month of forecast-beating figures is called a trend. Two are called a regime.

## The auction

The more instructive event of the day came in the auction room. The Treasury sold $42 billion of 10-year notes on Tuesday, and primary dealers, the banks obliged to bid at every auction, kept about 10% of the offering, the highest share since May, according to market reports. A dealer is not meant to be a buyer. Its function is to be a conduit, taking supply at the auction and passing it on to pension funds, insurers, foreign central banks and asset managers who actually intend to hold it. When the dealers end up keeping a bigger share than usual, it means that the end investors wanted a better price than the one on offer.

This is the point at which a government learns the difference between selling debt and having it bought. The Treasury sets a schedule and publishes it in advance; investors then decide what yield they require to fit the schedule into their own books. On Tuesday, they required a little more than the market had priced beforehand, which is how a hot inflation number turns into a higher borrowing cost for everyone from the federal government to the person with a mortgage application in process.

Another sale follows on Thursday, when the Treasury offers 30-year bonds. A weak 10-year auction is not a verdict; a weak 10-year auction followed by an uncertain 30-year one starts to resemble a conversation.

## The policy backdrop

The Fed meets on December 8 and 9. The prediction market Polymarket gave a quarter-point increase roughly 85% odds, with a further 10% or so on a half-point move and under 4% on no change, according to the Rio Times. The half-point scenario has an advocate: Governor Christopher Waller was quoted as saying that "a 50-basis-point increase could be considered in December if inflation remains elevated," according to the Free Press Journal. Lindsey Rosner of Goldman Sachs Asset Management said that persistent inflation could keep rate increases on the table through 2027.

I would draw attention to the grammar. "Could be considered" is how central bankers say that something is being considered. "If inflation remains elevated" is how they say the thing that has just happened. The entire sentence translates to a warning wrapped in two conditionals.

## What the mood was

On Wednesday the S&P 500 rose 1.3%, according to AP. A report on the previous day's bond moves described the mood among investors as relief rather than euphoria. It is a precise formulation. Relief is what one feels on learning that a bad thing is merely bad. Euphoria would require it to be good, and the arithmetic does not support that. A 10-year Treasury at 5% is a risk-free return that competes directly with equities, with real estate and with gold; the metal fell about 1.5% on Wednesday, and one summary of the move observed that a 5% Treasury is tough competition.

US bond markets were closed on Wednesday for Veterans Day, so the verdict on whether 5% had been a ceiling or a waypoint is deferred until Thursday. The institutional memory here is not encouraging. Round numbers matter in markets because people anchor on them, and a rate that has been touched once is easier to touch again.

There is an old macroeconomic observation that governments describe a rise in their own borrowing costs as "a market adjustment" and a fall as "confidence." Neither description is wrong. Both are what economists call framing. The 10-year yield is the price at which the world is willing to lend to the United States for a decade. On Tuesday it stood at 4.992%, and the people paid to buy the debt kept more of it than they had planned.

The Federal Reserve has under four weeks and another inflation report, producer prices on Friday, before it decides what it makes of that price. The bond market has already decided what it makes of the Fed, and has been charging for the opinion.
