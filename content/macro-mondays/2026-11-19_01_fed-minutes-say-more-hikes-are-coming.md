# Fed Minutes Say More Hikes Are Coming, and a Couple Want Bigger

The Federal Reserve's committee voted 10-2 to hold interest rates on October 28, but the minutes published on Wednesday suggest the holding was a matter of timing and not of conviction. According to the minutes, almost all participants judged that further action to raise the target range would likely be appropriate in the months ahead. The target range currently stands at 3.75% to 4.00%.

The dissents are the better guide to the mood. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack voted against the decision because they preferred a 25 basis point increase at that meeting, according to the Fed. The minutes record that some participants favoured an immediate hike on the grounds that policy was not sufficiently restrictive. A few said they could have accepted no change if markets clearly understood that further tightening was likely. Most supported holding while more information arrived.

## A committee in agreement about disagreeing

A central bank can say a good deal about its intentions without saying anything. The structure of the language matters: "some", "a few", "most", "a couple" and "almost all" are terms of art, and each denotes a different slice of the committee. On this occasion the slices add up to an institution that has largely decided to raise rates and is arguing about the calendar.

One passage deserves particular attention. A couple of participants said a move larger than 25 basis points could be considered at the next meeting, on December 8-9, if inflation data were unfavourable. The minutes do not name them. Markets, according to reports citing CNBC, still regard a 25 basis point rise in December as highly likely and a 50 basis point rise as a low probability.

## The inflation the minutes were worried about

The staff estimated that 12-month PCE inflation was running at 3.6% in September, with core PCE at about 3.1%, according to the minutes. The Fed's target is 2%. Officials told themselves, with some justification, that this is not merely a story about energy. Breitbart's summary of the minutes records that price increases in the October report were spreading across goods and services, and that some officials said tariff effects were fading while high energy prices continued to push inflation up.

There was also a new villain. According to the same account, officials noted that demand for data-center equipment was straining supplies and raising prices for some consumer electronics, and flagged AI infrastructure investment as a possible source of inflationary demand and a financial-stability risk. It is a rare occasion on which a central bank minute lends itself to a stock-market story, and the market took the hint: shares in the artificial intelligence trade were already unsettled, as the previous week's trading had shown.

## Waller wants more

The most vivid reaction came from Fed Governor Christopher Waller, who voted to hold in October. After the minutes, Waller said he favoured a 50 basis point rise in December, according to reports relayed by Breitbart and KuCoin. He was quoted as being deeply concerned about inflation moving in the wrong direction and spreading beyond a few sectors. He also suggested that a market shock might be needed to break the belief that 3% inflation is normal.

That last remark is the one a macroeconomist would underline. The costs of a central bank accepting 3% are not found in the first year, when households barely notice, but in the fifth, when wage contracts, rents and loans have all been repriced on the assumption that it is permanent. Expectations are an asset the Fed cannot print.

There is a counterweight. New York Fed President John Williams said on Tuesday that he saw no need to consider a move that large, according to Breitbart. The committee therefore enters December with a hawk wanting 50, a centre wanting 25, and a market that has already priced most of it.

## The cost of waiting

The bond market did not need a translator. The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.08% on Wednesday morning, according to the Associated Press, and the dollar reached a 19-month high, Reuters reported. Higher borrowing costs for governments and households are the mechanism through which the minutes will reach ordinary lives, and the mechanism has begun to operate before the decision is formally taken.

For the record, the Fed will describe all of this as data-dependent. It is worth noting that the data it depends on have been moving in one direction for several months. When a central bank says it will adjust policy as appropriate, and the committee's minutes spell out which direction is likely to be appropriate, the forward guidance is not subtle. It is simply expressed in the passive voice.
