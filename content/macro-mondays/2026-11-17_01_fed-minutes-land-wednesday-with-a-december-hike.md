# Fed Minutes Land Wednesday With a December Hike Priced In

The Federal Reserve will publish the minutes of its October 27-28 meeting at 2 p.m. Eastern time on Wednesday, and the main suspense is whether they say anything the market has not already priced. According to CME FedWatch data cited by Reuters on Monday, traders put the odds of a quarter-point hike at the December 9 meeting at 91.1%, with an 8.9% chance of a half-point move. The remaining probability of no change is small enough to be filed under courtesy.

## A hold that did not feel like one

At the October meeting the Fed left its target range at 3.75% to 4.00%, after raising it by a quarter point in September. The vote was 10 to 2, with Cook and Hammack dissenting in favour of another quarter-point increase, according to Yahoo Finance's coverage of the decision. The statement upgraded its description of economic growth from "solid" to "strong."

Economists at Bank of America told Reuters that the decision to hold revealed a deeper divide than the 10-2 count suggests. That is the usual function of minutes: to reveal how much disagreement lies beneath a formal vote, and in what vocabulary it was conducted. The vocabulary this time is likely to be inflation, since the data since the meeting have been unhelpful. October consumer prices rose 0.4% on the month against an expected 0.3%, with the core measure up 0.3% against 0.2% expected, according to figures cited by Admirals Markets. Producer prices, reported on November 13, were up 6.5% from a year earlier.

## The chair who dislikes guidance

Kevin Warsh, the Fed chair, has been unusually clear that he dislikes being clear. At his October press conference, according to the Fed transcript quoted by Admirals, he said "I don't believe in forward guidance," except in a crisis. He also said that politics played no role in the decision and described inflation as elevated. From 2027, the Fed will hold one of its eight annual meetings as a strategy session with no vote and no statement.

On Monday Warsh spoke at a Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco conference on productivity and jobs. According to the sources reviewed by Admirals, the remarks concerned artificial intelligence and productivity, and did not address near-term interest rates.

The market, abhorring a vacuum, filled it. Governor Christopher Waller said in London on November 10 that a half-point hike "should be considered" in December. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan has said at least two more quarter-point increases are needed, according to a commentary summarized by Admirals. Reuters reported on Monday that Fed funds futures price four hikes by September 2027. The Fed chair prefers not to guide the markets, and the markets have taken that as an invitation to guide the Fed.

## What the minutes can and cannot do

The case for a firm December hike is not hard to assemble. According to the Admirals preview, core consumer prices have risen at a 3.7% annualised rate over three months, September core PCE was 3.1% from a year earlier, and third-quarter growth was 3.4% annualised. Payrolls rose 165,000 in October and unemployment was 4.1%. That is a labour market that is not asking for relief, and a price level that is not offering any.

The cost of waiting is also visible. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.12% on Monday, according to Admirals, and was 5.15% on Friday's close. The two-year yield, which is the market's most sensitive reading of the policy path, was 4.84%. Mortgage rates near 7.4%, as Realtor.com's Danielle Hale told Yahoo Finance after the October decision, mean the rate increases are being felt by borrowers well before they are being debated by economists.

The minutes will be read for three things: how many officials favoured a hike in October, how many are willing to accept a half-point increase in December, and whether anyone is worried about the effect on the labour market. The first number matters for the vote count. The second matters for the market. The third matters for the textbook, and no one has opened the textbook since September.

What will not be in the minutes is any assurance about 2027. The Fed has decided that it would prefer to be a quieter institution, which is a respectable philosophy for a central bank in a calm economy. In an economy with inflation at these levels, silence from the chair is not neutral. It is an instruction to read everything else more carefully, and Wednesday's document is, by design, a great deal of everything else.
