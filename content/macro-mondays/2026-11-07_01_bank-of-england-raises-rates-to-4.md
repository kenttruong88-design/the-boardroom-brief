# Bank of England Raises Rates to 4% With One Dissenter

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1 on Thursday to raise Bank Rate by a quarter of a point to 4%, with one member, Catherine Mann, voting for a half-point rise to 4.25%. The Bank's published summary and minutes show a committee that agrees on the direction and disagrees, with some candour, on how long the journey is.

The reason is inflation, and the forecast is not shy about it. UK consumer price inflation rose to 3.5% in September. The minutes say staff expect it to reach around 4½% in the first quarter of 2027 and around 5% in the second, roughly a percentage point and a quarter above the Bank's July projection for the earlier of those quarters. Mann's own projection reaches 4.9% by the middle of next year.

## An energy shock, not a demand boom

The explanation is not exotic. The minutes say direct energy effects contributed around one percentage point to September's 3.5%, mostly through fuels. Brent crude futures averaged $108 a barrel and UK natural gas futures averaged 216 pence per therm over the 15 working days to 27 October, which the Bank put at 38% and 81% above its July Report assumptions respectively. The conflict in the Middle East, the minutes note, is in its ninth month.

Economists will recognise the textbook difficulty. A central bank cannot raise the price of gas by lowering it, but it can decide whether households and firms treat a temporary price shock as a permanent change in what things cost. The committee has concluded that, with inflation above target for years, the second thing is now a live risk. Mann pointed to a risk premium in the UK yield curve, and noted that five years of above-target inflation would average 4.7%.

Not everything is bleak. The minutes say activity has been stronger than expected, though the committee judges that recent strong GDP growth is likely to fall back somewhat in coming quarters. The labour market is described as stabilising, with Governor Andrew Bailey saying so and Dave Ramsden noting that private-sector wage growth has stopped declining. Megan Greene put underlying wage growth as "stuck around 3.5%."

## Eight votes, six different sentences

What distinguishes this decision is the commentary. The members who voted for the same quarter-point rise gave remarkably different accounts of what they had done.

Alan Taylor said it was "not the start of a hiking cycle" and called it a "one-for-now" insurance hike, adding that the next move could be up, down or sideways. Huw Pill, on the other hand, judged that a single 25 basis point hike was "unlikely to be sufficient" and that more tightening was likely needed, including in the near term. Greene said that "additional hikes will likely be required going forward." Dave Ramsden called the rise "necessary now." Swati Dhingra supported it as "additional insurance," noting weakness in consumer-facing services.

Sarah Breeden said the inflationary pressure in the pipeline was now sufficient to warrant a rise, while judging that current financial conditions were restrictive enough for the moment. Clare Lombardelli said there was likely to be a case for another near-term rise unless inflation pressures eased. Bailey, for his part, said the case for 25 basis points now was clear, and that he could see the argument for a further rise absent news that softens inflation risks.

The committee's official line is that monetary policy "is not on a pre-set path." The unofficial line, visible across the minutes, is that markets would be unwise to read the word "insurance" as a promise to stop.

## What it means for the household

The practical effects are already arriving ahead of the decision. According to the minutes, benchmark two-year mortgage rates have risen by nearly 50 basis points since the start of September. The Bank's Market Participants Survey median pointed to Bank Rate of 4% in November and 4.25% in February 2027, which sits below the path implied by the short-term rate curve.

There is also fiscal news in the background. The minutes say the Budget's new measures are expected to lower CPI by about a quarter of a percentage point by the second quarter of 2027, and that Mann's inflation forecast would be roughly 25 basis points higher without them. It is one of the rarer moments in British policymaking in which the Treasury and the central bank are observed pulling the same lever from different ends.

The next decision is due on 17 December. By then the committee will have seen another inflation print, another month of oil prices and, presumably, another round of explanations for what "one-for-now" meant. The most reliable conclusion from Thursday is also the least comfortable: the Bank has told the country it has raised rates once, may raise them again, and is not yet sure. Governments often describe such candour as "uncertainty." Economists prefer to call it a forecast.
