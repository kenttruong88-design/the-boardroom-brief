# America's October Inflation Report Arrives Without a Year-on-Year Number

The Bureau of Labor Statistics is due to publish October consumer price data at 8:30 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, and it will do so without the figure most commentators quote first. According to Kiplinger, the bureau collected no price data in October 2025 because of the government shutdown, and so it cannot calculate, and will not release, a year-on-year inflation rate this month. Wells Fargo put it plainly: with no price collection a year ago, there is nothing to compare against.

*Photo: Mark Stebnicki via Pexels*

That is a new kind of problem for the Federal Reserve, which is preparing to decide on rates on December 9 with a headline inflation reading that comes with an asterisk and a footnote. Economists have improvised. BMO's Sal Gautieri calculated that if one averages the September and November 2025 price indexes to stand in for the missing month, annual headline inflation would rise to 3.9% from September's 3.7%, and core inflation would come in at 2.7%. This is a reasonable workaround. It is also a number that the statistical agency itself declines to stand behind.

## The monthly figures will have to do

The forecasts for the monthly change cluster tightly. The consensus compiled by Bloomberg economists, cited by TheStreet, is for a rise of 0.3% in headline prices, down from 0.6% in September, and 0.2% for core. Wells Fargo expects 0.28% on the headline and 0.21% on core. UBS expects 0.26% against a Bloomberg consensus of 0.29%, with core at 0.23%. Barclays sees both at 0.2%, with gasoline prices down about 1%. RBC is at 0.3% for both. A rare consensus is a risky thing in macroeconomics, because it implies that the surprise, when it comes, has nowhere to hide.

UBS's Alan Detmeister noted that energy added 0.39 percentage points to September's reading and is projected to add only 0.04 points in October, which explains most of the expected cooling. He also flagged that distortions to year-on-year rent figures, linked to procedures the bureau adopted in response to the shutdown, will run from October through March. The gap in the data is therefore not a one-month curiosity. It will leave a mark on housing inflation for half a year.

## A Fed that hikes and hesitates

The backdrop is not one of calm. Kiplinger notes the Fed raised interest rates in mid-September for the first time in three years, then paused in late October, with Barclays observing two hawkish dissents at that meeting. The October jobs report showed 165,000 jobs added and unemployment at 4.1%, ahead of consensus. According to TheStreet, the CME FedWatch tool put the probability of a quarter-point hike in December at 83.4%, which would take the target range to 4.0% to 4.25%. UBS Wealth Management expects that move and another in March 2027.

The Fed speakers are not offering comfort. Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin said in prepared remarks on Monday that artificial intelligence feels "particularly consequential", noting that AI infrastructure spending is adding to near-term inflation, while a pullback in investment, if the technology underdelivers, could drag stock prices down with it. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee, via Bloomberg, said the central bank has to "come to terms" with an inflation-side failure in its mandate. These are the remarks of officials who are preparing the public for a decision, not debating one.

The underlying trend is not reassuring either. BMO's Mr Gautieri noted that core inflation was 2.6% annually in September, but ran at 3.4% annualised over three months and 2.9% over six. Airfares were up 22% on the year and rent 3.2%. Mike Fratantoni, chief economist of the Mortgage Bankers Association, said in October that the Fed would likely have to hike "a couple more times", with increases running into the first quarter of 2027. The monthly figure may cool; the direction of travel is a separate question.

The chair, Kevin Warsh, is six months into what TheStreet calls his regime change, and he will have to explain a December hike with data that cannot say where inflation is relative to a year ago. Kiplinger's own forecaster, David Payne, expects annual headline inflation to end the year at 4% and core at 2.7%, which reads as consistent with the hawks.

An economist's instinct, confronted with a missing data point, is to distrust the nearest estimate. A policymaker's instinct is to proceed regardless. The Fed has a mandate, a calendar and an inflation rate that is everywhere except on the page. It will hike, if it does, on a number that exists only in a footnote, and then explain to Congress that the footnote was thoroughly considered.
