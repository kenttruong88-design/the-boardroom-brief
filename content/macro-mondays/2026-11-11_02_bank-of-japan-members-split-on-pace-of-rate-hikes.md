# Bank of Japan Members Split on Whether to Hike Faster or Wait

Central banks publish summaries of their meetings so the public can see how decisions were made. The Bank of Japan's latest summary, released on Tuesday, shows the usual process: some members think the bank should hurry, others think it should check whether its last hike has worked yet, and everyone describes their position as prudent.

According to Reuters, the summary covers the BOJ's October 29-30 meeting, at which the bank held its policy rate at 1.25%. That rate follows an increase in September, which came after one in June. So the bank is mid-cycle, which is the interval during which every statement can be read as either "we are nearly done" or "we are only beginning."

## The case for speed

Some board members, Reuters reported, argued for faster hikes. The summary does not name them, which is the tradition. One member said the next increase should come "without becoming overly cautious," and another said that "the effects of past rate hikes have yet to materialise and financial conditions remain accommodative." Read quickly, the second quote is an argument for patience. Read twice, it is an argument for hiking: if conditions are still accommodative after two increases, then the policy rate is still not doing much work.

The yen offers some context. Per Cappnotes' Tuesday market closing report, the dollar traded at 158.77 yen after gaining 0.55% on the day. A currency at that level makes imported goods dearer for Japanese households and companies, and it is one reason a central bank might want to move before it is forced to.

## The case for waiting

Other members, according to the summary, argued that policymakers should assess the effect of each hike "over the course of a few months" before deciding on the next. Reuters noted that members cited soft consumption and uncertainty over demand for artificial intelligence. Official data are on their side: household spending fell by more than expected in September, partly because of weather, Reuters reported.

Weather is a charming explanation for weak spending, and a central banker's favourite kind of explanation, because it implies the problem will go away on its own. Whether it does so before the next meeting is a question for meteorologists and, in due course, the committee.

The summary also records disagreement over next year's wage negotiations, though Reuters described this in paraphrase, without direct quotation. That detail matters more than it appears, because the Bank of Japan's whole strategy rests on a virtuous cycle in which wages rise, prices rise, and rates rise gently behind them. If the first link is unclear, the rest of the chain is a hypothesis.

## The neutral rate problem

The deeper dispute is about where the rate should stop. Reuters reported that BOJ staff estimate the neutral rate, the setting that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, at between 1.1% and 2.5%. That is a range wide enough to drive a policy mistake through. Board member Naoki Tamura has said he thinks neutral is probably around 2%, which would put the current 1.25% policy rate well below it.

Markets are drifting toward the hawks. A Reuters poll expects the BOJ to raise its rate to 1.5% in December, and most analysts see the cycle ending at 1.75% or 2%. This leaves the central bank in an unusual position for a Japanese institution: the market expects it to hike, and the only question is whether the bank will oblige before or after it has finished explaining why it might not.

Politics adds colour. Reuters noted that two board members appointed by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi dissented from the September hike. The government that picks the board therefore includes two people who, in September, preferred the interest rate lower than their colleagues did. This is not a scandal. It is how central-bank independence looks when viewed from a close distance.

There is a comparison worth making with the United States, where Federal Reserve officials spent Tuesday debating whether to hike faster because prices are broadening. Japan's debate is over whether inflation has truly arrived or is merely running late. One central bank worries that the guest came too early. The other is not sure the guest is on the train.

The summary ends, as such documents do, without a conclusion. In central banking this counts as transparency. In any other organisation it would be called the minutes of a meeting that should have been an email.
