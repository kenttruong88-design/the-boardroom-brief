# US Producer Prices Rise 6.5% in a Year, Fastest Since 2022

The prices American businesses receive for their output rose 0.3% in October and 6.5% from a year earlier, the biggest annual gain since November 2022, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data reported on Friday by Xinhua. Economists polled by Reuters had expected a monthly rise of 0.4%. The monthly figure therefore came in a little soft, and the annual figure came in as a thing.

*Photo: ELEVATE via Pexels*

Governments and markets enjoy a monthly miss. It permits the phrase "cooler than expected" to appear in a headline, which is helpful on a day when the underlying number is the highest in nearly four years. The BLS also revised September up to a monthly gain of 1.4%, according to the same report, so October's 0.3% is a deceleration from a very high base. Slowing down from a sprint is still moving quickly.

## The anatomy of the number

Final demand goods prices rose 0.5% on the month and 10.2% on the year, the largest annual gain since October 2022, Xinhua reported. Services rose 0.2%, the fifth straight monthly increase. Food prices jumped 1.3%, and goods excluding food and energy rose 0.5%. Energy fell 0.1%, with diesel down 7.9% and crude petroleum down 10.3%.

That last pair deserves attention. Fuel prices fell and the index still rose, which means the pressure is broad rather than a single commodity. Basic organic chemicals gained 3.9%. Transportation and warehousing services rose 2%, and truck freight alone climbed 3.9%. Every product that moves from one place to another costs more to move, and the bill is eventually passed on to someone who is not at the meeting.

Prices for processed goods used as intermediate inputs rose 14.6% from a year earlier, the biggest annual rise since July 2022, per Xinhua. These are the prices of things that become other things. They tend to appear on shelves a few months later, with a markup, and without an apology.

The measure that excludes food, energy and trade margins rose 0.5% on the month for the second month running and 4.9% on the year. Strip out the noisy bits and the inflation is still there. That is what makes economists uneasy: the noisy bits were supposed to be the explanation.

## The Fed reads it

The report landed after the consumer price index for October, which rose 0.4% on the month, with the core measure up 0.3% for a third straight month, both 0.1 percentage point above forecasts, according to Eurasia Business News and Xinhua. CNBC noted that a comparable annual CPI figure could not be published because of missing October 2025 data, according to Eurasia Business News. America's flagship inflation statistic is currently unable to say how much prices have risen over a year, which is the one thing most people want from it.

Central bankers have not been shy about their reading. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called the inflation data "borderline disturbing", as reported by Xinhua, and told CNBC that the central bank "has definitely got work to do", as quoted by TheStreet. Xinhua also reported that Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said before the CPI release that a 50-basis-point hike should be considered in December if core prices rose 0.3% or more. They rose 0.3%.

Markets agree. According to CME FedWatch figures cited by Xinhua, traders see a December increase as near certain, with the debate confined to quarter-point or half-point, and another increase priced for January. Chris Zaccarelli of Northlight Asset Management said, in Xinhua's paraphrase, that the PPI showed some improvement but remained too high to alter the Fed's December approach.

## What it means

The honest summary is that a producer price index which rises 6.5% over a year is a sign of an economy in which businesses are passing costs on, and finding that customers will pay them. That is the defining condition of inflation that persists. It can be broken by tighter policy, which is what the Fed is preparing, or by demand giving way, which is what the Fed is hoping not to cause.

Next week offers two more data points. Retail sales for October are due on Tuesday and the minutes of the Fed's October meeting on Wednesday, according to Xinhua. The first will show whether households are still willing to absorb the price increases. The second will show how the committee itself weighed the evidence before this month's data arrived. Both are likely to be described as informative, which in this context means unwelcome in the usual way.
