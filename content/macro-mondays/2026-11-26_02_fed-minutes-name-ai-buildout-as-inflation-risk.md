# Fed Minutes Name the AI Buildout a Material Inflation Risk

The minutes of the Federal Reserve's October 27-28 meeting, released on November 18, contain a sentence that deserves more attention than it has had. According to Newsquawk's summary of the document, "many participants" viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk. The central bank, in other words, has concluded that the technology expected to lift productivity for a generation is also, in the short run, an expensive thing to construct.

## A committee with one worry and several accents

The minutes record a committee that held its target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent in October, after a hike in September, and is now almost uniformly preparing to raise it again. Newsquawk reported that "almost all participants" judged further rate increases, broadly consistent with the September projections, would likely be appropriate to return inflation to 2 percent in a timely way. The Associated Press, in a story carried by Advisor.ca, put it more plainly: nearly all officials expect a quarter-point hike in December, and two floated a half-point increase.

On the inflation diagnosis, the minutes are less a debate than a chorus. Newsquawk said "most participants" expected inflation to stay elevated in the near term before declining toward 2 percent, and that a "vast majority" saw upside risks, citing higher energy prices and refined fuel supply constraints. The AP added that the vast majority worried inflation could persist even as gasoline prices fell and the effects of tariffs and the AI buildout faded. Some participants said inflation had remained too high with few signs of abating. A few said tariff effects appeared to be waning, which is the closest the document comes to good news.

## The AI line, read carefully

The phrase "AI buildout" covers data centres, power generation, chips, cooling, land and the labour to assemble all of it. All of these are bought with money, and money spent is demand. The Bureau of Economic Analysis gave an indication of how much demand last week: business investment rose 16.1 percent in the third quarter, with business equipment up 21.8 percent, according to the United States Real Estate Investor, citing Reuters. Real GDP was revised up to 4.3 percent.

Nothing in the minutes says that this investment is a mistake. The staff outlook for growth was "a little stronger," and activity was described as expanding at a strong pace, with resilient household spending and robust business fixed investment. The argument is subtler: that a surge of investment, financed in an economy already at strong growth, adds to price pressure before it adds to capacity. That is an ordinary macroeconomic observation, and it is amusing only because the same enterprise is simultaneously described by its sponsors as deflationary.

Ingrid Holt, who spent years at the European Central Bank writing memos on this sort of tension, notes that the productivity gain and the price pressure arrive on different dates. The invoice comes first.

## Dissent, tone and a missing guide

Two policymakers, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, dissented in October in favour of a quarter-point hike, according to the AP. The minutes do not identify who held which view. AP also reported that Chair Kevin Warsh offered little guidance after the meeting.

The guidance came from elsewhere. Governor Christopher Waller told CNBC that inflation is "headed in the wrong direction and it's spreading out," and backed a half-point move in December, per the AP. Governor John Williams said he did not think the situation calls for dramatic action. The CME FedWatch tool, the AP noted, put the odds of a December hike near 95 percent.

Newsquawk also noted that "many" participants stressed the need to communicate that not hiking in October did not mean a weaker commitment to returning inflation to 2 percent. Central banks spend a considerable amount of effort explaining that a decision to do nothing is, in fact, something.

The Fed's next decision is on December 9. Between now and then the committee will receive more inflation data and, presumably, more earnings calls explaining the scale of AI spending. The two are not independent.

The closing observation is one for the CFO who hasn't read the report. The central bank has decided that the future is inflationary, and has responded by making the present more expensive to borrow against. If that sounds like a contradiction, it is. It is also the job.
