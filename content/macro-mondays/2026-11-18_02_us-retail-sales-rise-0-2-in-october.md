# US Retail Sales Rise 0.2% in October as the Cautious Consumer Returns

US retail sales rose 0.2% in October, the Commerce Department said on Tuesday, a slowdown from a 0.8% gain in September, as shoppers eased back ahead of the holiday season, the Associated Press reported. The figure is a nominal one. It is not adjusted for the fact that prices were rising at the same time.

The composition of the number is more interesting than the number. Sales at gas stations rose 1.5% in October after spiking 30.3% in September, according to the AP, and excluding gas stations, retail sales rose just 0.1%. Motor vehicles and parts dealers fell 1.6%, reversing a 1.3% increase in September, which the AP identified as the main drag on the total. Excluding auto-related businesses, retail sales rose 0.6%.

## A weak headline over a firm core

The measure that feeds most directly into the national accounts, the so-called control group, which excludes food services, autos, building materials and gas stations, rose a solid 0.6%, the AP reported. It is a curious month in which the headline looks tepid and the number that economists use to forecast GDP looks firm. It suggests that the people who set the headline are not the people who set the control group, and that neither of them is in a position to discuss it.

By category, online retailers rose 1.2%, a gain the AP linked to a two-day Amazon Prime Day event in early October. Restaurants, the only services category in the report, rose 0.2%. Furniture and home furnishings stores fell 2.3% and clothing and accessories stores fell 0.7%, according to the AP's account carried by BNN Bloomberg, and sporting goods, musical instruments, book and hobby stores also declined. In short, consumers spent on things they could order at midnight and economised on things that require a sofa to be carried through a door.

## The price of caution

The AP noted that consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, hotter than expected, with grocery prices also up 0.4%, according to the Labor Department. Zacks separately reported that producer prices rose 6.5% in the year to October, the fastest annual rate since November 2022, which suggests that more of this is to come through the pipeline. The AP also reported that airfares, public transportation and shipping costs were rising, and that investment in artificial intelligence has exacerbated inflation by driving up computer chip prices, which feeds through to laptops, phones and other electronics. Oil prices have been volatile, the AP said, as the flow of exports from the Middle East has risen. Gasoline prices were a relief: AAA put the national average at $3.78 a gallon on Tuesday, down 12% from a month earlier, though still well above what Americans were paying a year ago, the AP noted.

Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, wrote on Tuesday: "The cautious consumer is back. October retail sales were tepid. Budgets are tight for many households." She noted that spending adjusted for inflation was negative, and that middle-class and moderate-income households are "feeling the strain."

There is a counter-narrative, and the AP gave it space. Stephen Lebovitz, chief executive of the mall owner CBL Properties, said: "People are working and earning, and they're still spending." The financing of that spending is visible in the credit data. TransUnion reported that outstanding credit card balances reached $1.17 trillion last month, up 5.1% from a year earlier, covering 178.5 million consumers. Charles Wise, TransUnion's head of global research and consulting, said: "More Americans are carrying credit cards than ever, and they're handling them well."

Both assessments can be true, which is what makes the retail report so useful to anyone who wishes to be right. The consumer is earning and spending, and is also borrowing more, buying less furniture, and shopping online in the middle of the night. When prices rise by 0.4% in a month and sales by 0.2%, households are paying more for less, a transaction known in macroeconomics as "resilience" and in the household as "the end of the month".

Consumers are, in other words, doing what governments have long asked of them: absorbing price rises quietly and financing the difference on credit. The statistical agencies will report this as spending. The bank statements will report it differently, and are due, conveniently, after the holidays.
