# Fed Officials Float Faster Hikes After Record Smartphone Price Jump

The most reassuring sentence in monetary policy, "this is a one-off", has been retired. After October's consumer price report landed on Tuesday, several Federal Reserve officials said publicly that price pressures are spreading, and one, Dallas Fed president Lorie Logan, said the central bank could "move more rapidly this winter."

*Photo: Quang Vuong via Pexels*

Start with the data, which is where economists are supposed to start and where governments prefer not to. According to TheStreet, headline consumer prices rose 0.4% in October against an expected 0.3%, following a 0.6% rise in September. Core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3%, according to the Associated Press. That figure matters because Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said in advance that a 0.3% core reading could justify a half-point rate increase. The number arrived on the threshold, as numbers sometimes do when everyone has announced the threshold.

## Where the prices are rising

The AP reported that smartphone prices rose 1.4% in October, the largest monthly increase on record. Televisions rose 3.3%, and computers 0.5%. The AP also noted rising costs for streaming video, beef and auto insurance, and for chips, power equipment and copper tied to data-centre construction, though it gave no figures for those. The common thread is that much of the pressure is not from a single supplier or a single commodity. It is arriving from several directions at once, which is the technical meaning of "broadening."

A word on the annual rate, since the sources disagree about whether one exists. The AP put annual inflation at 4%, up from 3.7% in September. TheStreet, summarising the same release, reported that year-on-year comparisons were unavailable because of last year's government shutdown. Both cannot be a complete description of the statistical record. For the purposes of this column, the monthly numbers are the ones that hold up across sources, and the disagreement is a reminder that even the measuring tape is under pressure.

## The officials' view

Logan, a voting member of the rate-setting committee this year, backs two more quarter-point increases and said "the broader inflationary trend is more likely to persist," per the AP. Anna Paulson, president of the Philadelphia Fed, said in a speech late Tuesday: "Underlying inflation is above target, and it hasn't come down." She favours gradual hikes. Governor Michelle Bowman told the Council on Foreign Relations that price pressures "may be broadening."

Waller, speaking in London, said 70% of services prices rose at least 3% in September from a year earlier. He also said that tanker insurance costs linked to the Strait of Hormuz were adding $20 to $30 a barrel to oil, according to the AP. The Fed's chair, Kevin Warsh, declined to say whether more hikes are likely, a position that is professionally cautious and strategically unhelpful.

The federal funds rate is around 3.9%, after the Fed hiked in September and held in late October. The AP reported that the CME Group's FedWatch tool put the odds of a December 8-9 hike at about 90%. Reuters described a hike as effectively certain, with an 11.5% chance of a half-point move, and said the probability of a January increase had risen from under 30% on Monday to more than 64% on Tuesday. Two-year Treasury yields had their largest one-day jump since April 2025, according to Reuters.

## Why this should worry a CFO

There is a serious point under the sardonic one. Oil is down more than 5% over 30 days, the AP said, but remains about $30 a barrel above its level a year ago. A central bank that has been told for months that inflation is coming from outside the building now has to decide how much of it has moved indoors. Rate hikes are a blunt tool against a supply shock, but they are a sharp one against second-round effects, and the Fed's concern, as voiced by Logan and Bowman, is that the second round has begun.

The costs are not abstract. A higher-for-longer rate path raises the price of the credit that companies use for investment, inventory and the occasional ill-advised acquisition. Households with variable-rate debt will feel it within a quarter. Governments with large deficits will feel it at the next auction.

The tradition in these moments is for officials to say they are "data dependent." This is accurate, in the way that a sailor is weather dependent. The data have now spoken, and several of the people who read them for a living are suggesting the answer is to move faster.

It would be unfair to say the one-off explanation had no merit. It explained a great deal for a while. It has simply outlived its usefulness, like most things described as temporary.
