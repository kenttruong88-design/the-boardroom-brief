# UK Economy Grows 0.7% in Third Quarter, Led by Cloud Computing

The UK economy grew by 0.7% in the third quarter of 2026, the Office for National Statistics said on Thursday, up from 0.5% in the second quarter and the strongest quarter in the ONS's table, which begins in early 2025. Real GDP was 1.9% higher than a year earlier. Real GDP per head also rose by 0.7% on the quarter and 1.7% on the year, according to the same release.

It is a good number, and it deserves to be described as one before the qualifications begin. Britain's recent quarterly record, according to the ONS table, reads 0.6%, 0.0%, 0.2%, 0.0%, 0.6%, 0.5% and now 0.7%. A country that registered flat quarters twice in 2025 has stopped doing that, at least for now. A finance minister may be forgiven for mentioning it.

## Where it came from

The services sector grew by 0.9% in the quarter, up from 0.6% in the second, and 11 of its 14 subsectors expanded. The ONS said information and communication has been the largest driver of GDP growth across 2026, and that many of the businesses with the largest rises in turnover are in artificial intelligence and cloud computing. Within that, computer programming and consultancy rose 4.4%. Administrative and support services rose 4.1%, helped by a 6.6% jump in rental and leasing.

The rest of the economy was less athletic. Production output rose just 0.1%, with mining and quarrying down 2.2% and electricity and gas down 2.1%, though manufacturing grew 0.9% on a 4.6% gain in computer and electronic products. Construction fell 0.7%, with new private housing work down 4.7%. Education and accommodation and food services both shrank by 0.5%, and food and beverage services fell 1.9%.

Put plainly, the economy's growth was delivered by people who work on computers for businesses, and restrained by people who build houses and serve food. Any government would take that trade. It would also like to know whether it will last.

## What the statisticians will not say

The ONS is notably candid on one point. It says it is difficult to quantify the exact impact of the AI and cloud firms behind the information and communication growth. That is an unusual admission for a statistics office, and a useful one. The biggest contributor to national output is a sector whose output the national accounts cannot yet cleanly measure.

A second caveat sits in the technical notes. The ONS said it lacks up-to-date quarterly gross trading profit data from HMRC, which runs roughly two years behind, so for non-financial corporations it relies on contextual data. The ONS also reported that, since 2000, the mean revision between its first estimate and the estimate three years later is 0.08 percentage points, and the mean absolute revision is 0.27 points. In plain English, a first estimate of 0.7% is a good guess with a margin of roughly a quarter of a point on either side. The figure arrives with three significant digits and the confidence of one.

## Demand, trade and the household

On the expenditure side, growth was driven by households and by investment. Household consumption rose 0.6% on the quarter and 1.8% on the year, according to the ONS. Government consumption also rose 0.6%. Gross fixed capital formation increased 1.7%, with business investment up 0.4% and 3.0% on the year.

Trade added little. Exports rose 1.6% and imports 1.8%, so the external balance was a slight drag, and the goods and services deficit stood at 1.3% of nominal GDP. Taxes less subsidies fell 2.6% on the quarter, with taxes down 2.1%, mainly VAT, a detail that fiscal planners will read more closely than the headline.

The monthly figures were less tidy. September GDP rose 0.2%, but August was unchanged after being revised down from 0.1%, and July grew 0.4%, according to the ONS. A quarter built from a strong July, a flat August and a modest September looks, on close inspection, like one good month and two passable ones.

## The reading

The cautious interpretation is that Britain has found a new engine and cannot yet describe it. The less cautious interpretation is that it has found a new dependency. A sector that supplies most of a quarter's growth is also a sector whose slowdown would be felt everywhere.

For policymakers, the number complicates the argument about how quickly demand needs support. For a finance ministry, it is a rare quarter in which the headline and the mood agree. The ONS, which publishes its next estimate on 22 December, will go on measuring, as it always does, with a straight face and a footnote.
