# German ZEW Jumps to 35.6 as Euro Area Q3 GDP Is Revised Up to 0.6%

German investor and analyst sentiment rose more than forecast in November. The ZEW economic sentiment index climbed to 35.6 from 28.1 in October, against a consensus of 29.0, according to figures published on Tuesday and reported by FXStreet. It is one of the few indicators this week to have gone in the direction the optimists had in mind.

## What the survey measured

The ZEW survey asks financial analysts and institutional investors for their expectations of the economy over the next six months, and its sentiment index reports the balance between optimists and pessimists. It is a measure of mood, not of output. The detail matters here: the separate assessment of current conditions remained deeply negative at minus 33.2, against a forecast of minus 35.0 and a previous reading of minus 35.9, FXStreet reported. In other words, the people surveyed think that Germany is in poor shape and that it will probably get better. That is a respectable position for an economist, and a less respectable one for a business planning a hiring round.

The wider region followed. ZEW's euro area expectations index rose to 26.5 in November from 18.5 in October, FXStreet reported. The euro was little changed at about 1.0995 against the dollar during the release, which suggests that currency traders regarded the number as encouraging rather than transformational.

## The growth data

The sentiment reading arrived four days after Eurostat's revision of third-quarter growth. The euro area economy grew 0.6% quarter on quarter in the July to September period, FXStreet reported on November 13, up from the 0.4% flash estimate, which was also the consensus forecast and the previous quarter's rate. A revision of two-tenths of a percentage point is large by the standards of the exercise, since flash estimates are normally close to the final figure and statisticians rarely surprise anyone.

The revision matters for two reasons. First, it improves the starting point for the fourth quarter, since growth that has already occurred cannot be undone by next month's gloom. Second, it makes the economy look less fragile at precisely the point when European policymakers and investors were wondering how exposed it was to the problems of other regions. An economy that grew faster than the first estimate suggested three weeks ago is, at least, a better candidate for good surprises.

I would add that revised data are not forecasts. They show that Europe was somewhat stronger in the summer than first reported. They say nothing certain about the winter.

## Where the optimism comes from, and where it might not

Surveys of expectations are sensitive to recent headlines, and the survey was taken before Friday's sell-off in the United States, where, Reuters reported, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% and the S&P 500 3.70% in a single session. The investors surveyed answered before a major US sell-off, and the next survey will answer after it.

That timing matters because Europe is not insulated from American financial conditions. Equity markets, credit spreads and the dollar link the two economies, and the Federal Reserve, which Reuters said is priced for a near-certain 25 basis point rate rise in December, is not setting policy for the euro area but is affecting it. A European expansion that depends on benign global financial conditions is exposed when those conditions change.

## The reading

There is a respectable case for cautious optimism. Third-quarter growth was stronger than first estimated, sentiment improved by more than expected, and the euro area's measure of expectations rose by eight points in a month. There is an equally respectable case for caution. Sentiment surveys are cheap and quick, they overreact to recent news, and their record in predicting turning points is mixed. A current-conditions reading of minus 33.2 does not describe a boom.

Governments tend to describe such a mix as "resilience." The word is useful because it avoids committing to a direction. What the data actually say is simpler: Europe grew faster than reported, its forecasters are more hopeful than they were, and nobody has yet told them what happened on Wall Street on Friday.

The coming weeks will test whether the improvement in mood survives contact with American market stress. If the next ZEW reading holds, the optimism has some substance. If it collapses, we will have learned that European sentiment was, for a few weeks, a view of the weather taken before the storm. Economists, who generate forecasts the way other industries generate paperwork, will in either case have a revised estimate to publish.
