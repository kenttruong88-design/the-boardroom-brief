# China's Factory Prices Rise 5.7% While Consumer Prices Crawl 0.7%

China's National Bureau of Statistics reported on Monday that producer prices rose 5.7% in October from a year earlier, up from 5.0% in September and the fastest pace in nearly four and a half years, according to Reuters as cited by Intelligence Report. Consumer prices, in the same release, rose 0.7%, slightly below the 0.8% economists had expected and down from 0.9% in September. The gap between the two numbers is roughly five percentage points, which is the sort of discrepancy that demands an explanation and, in this case, has several.

The producer figure beat a Reuters poll forecast of 5.3%. Month on month, producer prices rose 0.7%. Prices of materials bought by industrial producers rose 7.9% on the year, and fuel and power rose 18.6%, according to BigGo Finance's account of the bureau's breakdown. This is not a manufacturing sector discovering pricing power. It is one discovering an energy bill.

## Where the money went

The contributors to the producer index read like the contents of a geopolitical risk register. Coal mining and washing was up 36.6% on the year. Oil and gas extraction rose 35.0%. Petroleum and coal processing climbed 29.9%, and chemical raw materials 16.4%. BigGo calculated that six energy-linked industries accounted for about 0.61 percentage points of the monthly gain, more than 80% of it. The bureau's own statisticians pointed to imported inflation, seasonal stockpiling ahead of winter, "orderly capacity adjustment" in some industries and demand linked to artificial intelligence, in that order of enthusiasm. Finwire's reading was blunter: an energy shock working through the system rather than a surge in new demand.

On the consumer side, the picture is notably less combustible. Food prices fell 2.9% on the year, a decline 1.2 percentage points larger than in September, with fresh vegetables down 16.9% and pork down 8.6%. Core consumer prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 0.8% on the year, down from 1.0% in September, and were flat month on month. Dong Lijuan, a bureau statistician cited by Xinhua, attributed price movements to a mix of imported inflation and domestic demand.

The oddest detail is where consumer prices did rise. Gasoline was up 22.4% on the year, adding about 0.63 percentage points to the headline reading, according to BigGo. Data storage devices rose 45.4%, tablets 29.3%, computers 23.0% and mobile phones 16.7%, together adding about 0.26 points. The bureau cited a sharp drop in international gold prices and falling prices for cars and appliances as drags. China's shoppers, in other words, are being squeezed at the petrol pump and the electronics shop, and relieved at the greengrocer.

## Who pays for the gap

A five-point wedge between what factories pay and what shoppers pay has to land somewhere, and the usual place is corporate margins. Reuters noted that the yuan's rapid gains are squeezing exporters, and that AI-related Chinese stocks have fallen. Bureau spokesperson Wang Guanhua said global inflation pressure has risen sharply but that price gains in China "remain relatively mild", crediting the industrial system, supply chains and price-stabilising policies. Liu Fang, a researcher at the National Development and Reform Commission, pointed to profits at large industrial firms, which rose 15.5% in the first nine months, and said prices should keep a mild upward trend.

Not all analysts are persuaded that the gap will close from the bottom. Feng Lin of Golden Credit Rating expects November's consumer price rise to narrow to 0.6% on the year, with prices staying soft through the end of the year. Zhang Di of China Galaxy Securities said demand recovery needs more time. Wen Bin of China Minsheng Bank was more bullish on factory prices and warned that oil could rebound if diplomatic talks falter, which, in the current climate, is a polite way of saying that the producer price index is partly a bet on what happens in the Gulf.

For economists accustomed to treating Chinese deflation as a structural feature, the producer figure is awkward. For central bankers, it is a reminder that a price index tells you what is being charged, not who is being charged. Beijing now has two price indices pointing in different directions and one set of households that can tell which is which.

The statisticians describe the recovery as mild. They are right about the half that reaches the shop floor. The other half is paid for in the dark, between the dock and the till.
