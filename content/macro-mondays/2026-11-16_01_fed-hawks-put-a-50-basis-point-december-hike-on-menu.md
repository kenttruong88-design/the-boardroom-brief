# Fed Hawks Put a 50-Basis-Point December Hike on the Menu

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said on November 10 that a quarter-point rate increase is his base case for the December meeting, but that a half-point move should be considered if October core inflation runs around 0.3% or higher, according to reports of his speech to the Bank of England Watchers Conference. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said the same week that the Federal Open Market Committee needs to deliver at least two more quarter-point increases, and that it may be possible to move more rapidly this winter.

*Photo: Mark Stebnicki via Pexels*

The Fed raised rates by 25 basis points in September and held in October, Kitco reported. Since then, October's consumer price data has come in above economists' forecasts. The policy debate has accordingly shifted from whether to tighten further to how to do so without appearing to have been caught unawares.

## What Waller actually said

The most quotable line belongs to Waller. "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk," he said, according to Forbes. This is an unusual argument for a central bank to make about itself, since credibility is conventionally established by doing what one has said, not by escalating the size of what one does. That said, the governor's reasoning is not frivolous. He pointed to inflation well above the committee's 2% target, to energy costs working their way into core prices, to AI-related capital spending, and to surveys showing firms expect to keep raising prices.

Forbes reported that Waller was particularly concerned about the breadth of price increases in core goods and in market-based services excluding housing. This matters because economists generally treat broad price pressure as more persistent than narrow price pressure. One expensive commodity is a shock. Many expensive categories are a trend.

Logan's reasoning was simpler. Inflation is trending too high, she argued, while the labour market sits near maximum employment, and monetary policy is still accommodative enough to add to the pressure. The word "accommodative" is doing considerable work in a sentence about a central bank that has been raising rates.

## The market has already voted

Traders did not wait for the minutes. According to a closing-market recap published by Investrade on November 13, investors price roughly an 88% chance of a 25 basis point hike in December and about 12% for 50 basis points. The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.152%, up 29.4 basis points over five sessions, the largest weekly rise since April 2025. The two-year yield rose 35.2 basis points to 4.872%.

Investrade also named Boston Fed President Susan Collins among the officials describing further tightening as likely. Forbes separately identified Governor Michelle Bowman as favouring hikes, though perhaps less aggressively than Waller. The Fed has not so much divided into hawks and doves as into hawks and slightly calmer hawks.

## Three events to watch

The calendar is dense. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is scheduled to speak on Monday at 17:00 GMT, according to FXEmpire. October retail sales are due Tuesday, with forecasters expecting headline growth of 0.2%, down from 0.8% the previous month. The minutes of the October FOMC meeting are published Wednesday at 19:00 GMT. The committee's next decision is on December 8 and 9, per Forbes.

The minutes deserve attention for a particular reason. They describe a meeting at which the committee held rates steady, written before the inflation report that has since reordered everyone's views. They are, in effect, the historical record of a more relaxed era, which in this case ended last Tuesday.

## The credibility problem

There is a real argument inside the Fed's current rhetoric, and it is worth separating from the theatre. If inflation expectations are drifting upward, a cautious central bank risks needing to raise rates further and for longer later. A single large move can be cheaper than a series of small ones. Waller's case is not that 25 basis points is wrong. It is that 25 basis points might be insufficient, and that discovering this in February would be more expensive than acting in December.

The counterargument is also old. A committee that pre-announces a larger hike on the strength of one data point risks looking reactive, and the risk of overtightening into a slowing economy does not vanish because the previous month's data were warm. Retail sales on Tuesday will offer one clue. The minutes on Wednesday will offer another.

What no one on the committee seems willing to say is the plainest summary of the situation. A central bank that raises rates in September and holds in October is now explaining that a larger move in December may be needed to protect the credibility the pause may have dented. Credibility, like trust, arrives on foot and leaves by hiking.
