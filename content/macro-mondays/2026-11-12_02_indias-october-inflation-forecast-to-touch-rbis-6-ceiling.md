# India's October Inflation Forecast to Touch the RBI's 6% Ceiling

India's consumer price inflation for October, scheduled for release on November 12, is forecast to land at or above 6%, the upper limit of the Reserve Bank of India's tolerance band, which would make it the first breach in about two years. September's reading was 5.54%, up from 4.83% in August, with food inflation at 7.74%, according to the Telangana Today's report on the official data. Forecasts for October range from roughly 5.9% to 6.2%, per NewsBytes. ICRA, the rating agency, expects 6.1%, according to its chief economist Aditi Nayar.

The RBI operates under a flexible inflation targeting framework: a medium-term target of 4% with a tolerance band of 2% to 6%. The band is designed to give the central bank room for supply shocks without surrendering the target. What it does not clearly specify, as economists will cheerfully note, is what counts as a breach. A number of 6.0% is at the ceiling. A number of 6.1% is above it. A framework that distinguishes between these has a lot riding on the first decimal place.

## What is driving it

The story is food. Vegetables, pulses and cooking oils weigh heavily in India's consumer basket, far more heavily than in advanced economies, which is why the headline number tends to follow the market stall rather than the policy meeting. Food inflation at 7.74% in September was well above the headline, and forecasters point to food prices as the principal driver of the expected acceleration. India Ratings (Ind-Ra) said in mid-October that retail inflation could reach about 6% in October while wholesale inflation might exceed 12%, according to Business Standard.

The wholesale number matters because it is where cost pressure first appears. When wholesale prices rise much faster than retail prices, the gap is a bill to be paid by someone. Producers can absorb it, which squeezes margins, or pass it on, which lifts the retail figure. In India, a country of intensely price-sensitive consumers, retailers often wait before passing costs through, and then do so in a rush.

## The policy question

There is a procedural dimension. Under the framework, if inflation stays above the upper tolerance level for three consecutive quarters, the RBI is required to explain the failure to the government in writing and set out the remedial steps. One month at the ceiling does not trigger that clock, but it is the sort of month from which clocks are started.

Central banks dislike being told that a number is surprising. They prefer to have predicted it. The RBI will say, with reason, that food-price shocks are supply-driven and temporary, and that monetary policy cannot make it rain. The orthodox response is to "look through" them. The complication is that looking through works best when the shock is brief, and a ceiling touched in October invites the question of what November holds.

Some commentators already expect a rate increase from the RBI in December, per commentary on the forecasts. I would put it more carefully. A central bank that is mandated to hold inflation within a band and finds itself at the top edge of that band is not obliged to act. It is obliged to explain why it does not. That explanation has a cost in credibility if it has to be repeated at several meetings.

## The comparison nobody wants to draw

Inflation is also pressing on central banks elsewhere this autumn. The Bank of England raised its rate by 25 basis points to 4% on November 5, so the Indian reading arrives in a world where tightening is not an unusual position. India is hardly an outlier. It is merely the economy in which the outcome will be decided by the price of onions, among other things, rather than by the price of electricity or the strength of the labour market.

It is worth remembering what the band was for. India adopted flexible inflation targeting in 2016, and the band of 2% to 6% was a compromise between ambition and realism. It was not intended to be a place where the economy sits on the upper line.

The data will tell us on Thursday evening whether October touched the ceiling or went through it. If it is the former, expect a statement that inflation remains "within the tolerance band." If it is the latter, expect a statement that the breach is "transient" and "driven by food." In both cases the vegetable sellers will have been consulted last.
