# UK Economy Grows 0.7% in Third Quarter, Led by AI and Cloud

The UK economy grew by 0.7% in the third quarter of 2026, the Office for National Statistics said on Thursday, matching the consensus of economists polled by the Press Association and accelerating from 0.5% in the second quarter. For a government that describes the economy as resilient whenever asked, the data had the rare quality of agreeing with the adjective.

*Photo: Candid Flaneur via Pexels*

GDP was 1.9% higher than a year earlier, the ONS said. Real GDP per head rose 0.7% on the quarter and 1.7% on the year. In nominal terms, output grew 1.4% on the quarter and 4.3% on the year, implying a GDP deflator of 2.3% over twelve months. The first quarter of 2026 is shown at 0.6% in the bulletin's tables, so the economy has now strung together three consecutive quarters of expansion at or above half a percent, a run of consistency that Treasury press officers will, one assumes, mention at some length.

## A single engine, running hot

The composition matters more than the total. Services grew 0.9% on the quarter, according to the ONS, with business-facing services up 1.0% and consumer-facing services up 0.5%. Production rose just 0.1%. Construction fell 0.7%.

The standout was information and communication, which grew 2.1% in the quarter. The ONS describes it as the largest single driver of GDP growth across 2026 and says that many of the firms reporting the biggest increases in turnover are involved in activities related to artificial intelligence and cloud computing. In other words, a large share of the country's growth is a data centre and the people who bill for it.

Deutsche Bank's chief UK economist Sanjay Raja made a similar point before the release, telling the Press Association that business investment was being driven by data centres and IT, defence and aircraft. He added that consumers "have also been out in force, despite the energy shock", and that "we expect the UK economic growth story to have cooled a bit" looking ahead. Household consumption rose 0.6% in the quarter, the ONS data show, which suggests the shoppers did their part.

The gap between real and nominal growth also deserves a glance. Nominal GDP rose 4.3% on the year while real GDP rose 1.9%, and the difference is the 2.3% deflator, a reminder that roughly half of the money economy's expansion over the past twelve months was price rather than volume. Households feel the former first. The statisticians record the latter first. Both are correct, and neither is the same as being better off.

## What the expenditure side says

Gross fixed capital formation rose 1.7% in the quarter. Business investment rose 0.4% on the quarter and 3.0% on the year. Government consumption rose 0.6%. Exports of goods and services grew 1.6%, while imports grew 1.8%, a small drag from trade that the headline figure absorbed without comment.

The monthly path was less tidy than the quarter implies. GDP grew 0.2% in September, after showing no growth in August, revised down from 0.1% in the previous release, and an unrevised 0.4% in July, according to the ONS monthly estimate. A quarter built on a strong July, a stalled August and a respectable September is a quarter that rewards the people who read the tables rather than the press release.

## The caveats a CFO should want

Three points are worth carrying into any planning meeting. First, growth concentrated in one sector is exposed to that sector's capital cycle. Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey said this week that artificial intelligence is already starting to benefit the UK economy, according to Investment Week on November 10, which is encouraging for the country and awkward for anyone hoping to find a second engine. Second, the ONS will publish productivity flash estimates for the third quarter on Tuesday, November 17, which will show how much of this output came from efficiency and how much from simply working more. Third, the next quarterly GDP release is not due until December 22, so there is no new data to correct any impression formed this week.

None of this makes the number less welcome. A 0.7% quarter is a 0.7% quarter, and ministers will say so, repeatedly. The footnote here is that the engine of the recovery is a technology cycle, and technology cycles, like governments, tend to describe their own prospects with considerable optimism.

British growth, in short, has found a hobby. Whether it becomes a career is a question for the productivity figures on Tuesday.
