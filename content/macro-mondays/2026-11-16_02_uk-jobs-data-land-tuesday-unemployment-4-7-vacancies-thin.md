# UK Jobs Data Land Tuesday With Unemployment at 4.7% and Vacancies Thin

The Office for National Statistics publishes its next labour market bulletin on Tuesday, 17 November. The release it replaces, issued on 20 October, put the UK unemployment rate at 4.7% for June to August 2026, down 0.1 percentage points on both the year and the quarter, and showed vacancies falling to 691,000, according to the ONS. Neither number tells the whole story, and the ONS was careful to say so.

## A rate that fell for the wrong reasons, or at least not the obvious ones

The headline looks tidy. The employment rate for people aged 16 to 64 rose to 75.3%, up 0.2 percentage points on the year and the quarter, and economic inactivity fell to 20.9%, per the ONS. A falling unemployment rate alongside rising employment is, in a normal economy, good news.

The difficulty is the other half of the labour market, which is the demand for workers. Vacancies in July to September stood at 691,000, down 21,000, or 2.9%, from the previous quarter and down 37,000 on the year. The ONS said this was the largest quarterly fall since the opening months of 2026. It also noted that, outside the pandemic, vacancies were last this low in July to September 2014, when the figure was 686,000.

Put plainly, the unemployment rate is falling in an economy where firms are advertising fewer jobs than at any point in over a decade. The ONS offered a possible explanation: smaller firms may not be recruiting because of labour costs. Employers, in other words, have concluded that an additional worker is the single most expensive item on the menu.

## Payrolls are going nowhere

Data from HMRC's pay-as-you-earn records show the number of payrolled employees fell by 111,000, or 0.4%, between August 2025 and August 2026. The early estimate for September was 30.3 million, down 66,000 on the year and up 13,000 on the month. The ONS cautioned that these early estimates are provisional and are likely to be revised next month, a caveat that is useful to remember when anyone describes a one-month movement as a trend.

The ONS's own description of the picture is "broadly flat" in 2026. Anyone reading that phrase as an endorsement should note that flat is a direction chosen by economies unwilling to commit.

## Pay is cooling, but the public sector is not

Annual growth in regular pay, excluding bonuses, was 3.7% in June to August. Total pay growth, including bonuses, was also 3.7%, down from 3.9% in the previous three months. The ONS said total pay growth was at its lowest in nearly six years, having last been lower in August to October 2020.

The sector split is instructive. Public sector regular pay rose 6.3%, against 3.0% in the private sector. The ONS attributed the public sector figure to the timing of pay awards, so this is a calendar effect, not a sudden outbreak of generosity in Whitehall. In real terms, adjusted for CPIH inflation, regular pay grew 0.6% and total pay 0.7%. Using CPI, the figures were 0.8% and 0.9%.

For households, this means wages are still ahead of prices, narrowly. For the Bank of England, whose job is to worry about both, this is the unglamorous middle of the road: wage growth is moderating, which is helpful, but hiring is weak, which is not.

## What to look for on Tuesday

There are three things worth checking. The first is whether unemployment continues to fall as vacancies shrink, or whether the two finally converge. The second is whether total pay growth, already at its lowest in almost six years, continues downward. The third is how far the September payroll estimate is revised, since the ONS has already said it is likely to change.

None of this is a forecast. The ONS has not yet published the figures, and economists will be debating them within minutes of release.

The broader point is easier to state than to resolve. Britain's labour market is neither collapsing nor thriving. Employers are not firing in bulk, but they are not hiring with any conviction either. Workers, for their part, are mostly staying where they are. Government statistics describe this as stability. A more candid description might be a market in which everyone has agreed, by silent consensus, to wait for somebody else to move first.
