# US GDP Gets a Second Look Wednesday With 3.5% Expected

The Bureau of Economic Analysis will publish its second estimate of third-quarter gross domestic product on Wednesday, November 25, at 8:30am Eastern, and the first estimate is already on the record: real GDP grew at an annual rate of 3.4 percent between July and September, up from 2.2 percent in the second quarter. According to a consensus calendar published by HeyGoTrade and attributed to FXEmpire, economists expect the second estimate to read 3.5 percent. That is a forecast of a 0.1 point revision to a number that was itself a forecast, delivered by an agency that will revise it again.

## What the first estimate said

The BEA's advance estimate, released on October 29, attributed the third-quarter growth to inventory investment and equipment spending, notably computers, together with household spending on health-care services and prescription drugs. Exports fell, led by petroleum products, according to the release. Federal nondefense spending rose because sales from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve were smaller than in the second quarter, which is an unusual way for a government to add to growth: by selling less of something it holds.

The price data in the same release are the part of the quarter that deserves more attention than the headline. The PCE price index rose at a 2.1 percent annual rate in the third quarter, down from 5.0 percent in the second. Core PCE, which excludes food and energy, rose 2.5 percent, against 3.3 percent in the second quarter. Those are sharp declines in a series the Federal Reserve is meant to be controlling. Whether they describe a durable change or a handsome interlude is the question the central bank is paid to answer.

## The revision habit

Second estimates are not usually dramatic. Stocktitan's preview, published November 23, notes that the second estimate has moved quarterly growth by about 0.2 percentage point on average since 2021. A consensus forecast of 3.5 percent therefore sits comfortably inside the normal range of revision, and a print anywhere between 3.3 and 3.7 percent would be unremarkable.

The history is more instructive than the average. The BEA's third estimate for the second quarter put real GDP growth at 2.2 percent, according to the agency's own release. That was 0.7 point above the second estimate of 1.5 percent. A quarter that was initially described as sluggish thus became, after the fact, a respectable one. The advance figure for a quarter is what the markets and the press react to. The revised figure is what the historians use. The distance between them is the cost of asking an economy for an answer before it has finished the work.

For readers who prefer their uncertainty in a single sentence: the economy is a large machine whose output is measured by a smaller machine whose method is to ask everyone how it is going and then adjust as the answers arrive.

## The other numbers on Wednesday

The GDP release is not alone. HeyGoTrade's calendar lists the October personal consumption expenditures report for the same morning, with a consensus of 0.3 percent monthly growth in core PCE, the same as the previous month. The calendar also shows a consensus of 201,000 for weekly initial jobless claims. The durable goods report is also due, though I could not find a published consensus or prior figure for it and will not guess.

The PCE report matters more than the GDP revision for the Federal Reserve, which targets PCE inflation and not growth. A core reading of 0.3 percent in a month, repeated, is a 3.7 percent annual rate, which is not the profile of a central bank that is comfortable. The arithmetic is mine, not a forecast, and it is the sort of arithmetic that makes the word "steady" sound like a request.

The market backdrop adds its own commentary. On Tuesday the 10-year Treasury yield stayed near 4.9 percent, according to the pages I read. Krishna Guha of Evercore ISI said, as paraphrased by HNGN, that markets remain uncertain about the stance of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh. A central bank chair whose stance is a matter of conjecture on the eve of the Thanksgiving break has chosen an interesting week to be enigmatic.

## What to look for

Three things will tell you whether the quarter was as good as the first estimate said. The first is the headline: a reading near 3.5 percent confirms the story. The second is the composition. If growth rests on inventories and computer equipment, as the first estimate indicated, a revision to either would change the quality of the number more than its size. Inventories, by their nature, are growth that has not yet been sold.

The third is the price index. A third-quarter PCE price rise of 2.1 percent followed a second-quarter rise of 5.0 percent. If the revised figure comes in higher, the soft landing in prices will have a smaller runway than advertised. If it comes in lower, the case for patience gets a little easier to make, and the officials who prefer to say so will be glad of the company.

The release will be digested over a long holiday weekend, during which the revised number will be quoted at dinner tables by exactly no one. By Monday, it will be old news, and by the third estimate it will be old history. The only honest conclusion is the one statisticians reach in private: the second estimate is not the answer, only a better draft of the question.
