# Bank of Japan's Ueda Says Rate Rises Should Continue

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda told parliament on 18 November that, with financial conditions still accommodative, it would be appropriate to keep raising the policy rate. The rate stands at 1.25 percent after the central bank held it at its 30 October meeting. Ueda's comments, reported by the New Straits Times, suggest the BOJ regards its December and January meetings as live.

## Mission accomplished, mission continuing

The BOJ's inflation target is 2 percent. By the bank's own description, its fourteen-year effort to reach that target has been a success, and policy is now focused on preventing an overshoot. For readers who learned their central banking in the era of deflation, that sentence deserves a moment of silence. A bank that spent a decade and a half trying to get Japanese prices to rise is now explaining, with a straight face, why they should be allowed to rise only so far.

The logic is not exotic. A policy rate of 1.25 percent, with underlying inflation having reached 2 percent, means real rates are still negative. The BOJ's staff estimate of the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains, ranges from 1.1 percent to 2.5 percent. That is a wide range, and the policy rate sits near its bottom. Ueda's argument, in effect, is that the economy is being asked to run with the stimulus switch on after the problem the stimulus was meant to solve has been resolved.

The Bank raised rates in June and September this year. At its October meeting it paused. The pause was not unanimous.

## The dissenter's case

Board member Hajime Takata proposed raising the rate to 1.5 percent last month and was outvoted. In a speech reported by The Edge Malaysia on 16 November, he said the policy rate needs to be placed, at an early stage, in what he called a "good" position, and that the BOJ must shift its stance away from encouraging a rise in underlying inflation. Takata also said he expected AI-related investment to stay solid despite market turbulence in early November.

Takata's position matters because the market has been shaping its expectations around it. Investors expect the policy rate to reach 1.5 percent in December or January, according to The Edge. Daiwa Securities, in a view reported by investingLive in September, expected hikes in December this year and April 2027, with the rate peaking near 2 percent. Governor Ueda is scheduled to give a speech and hold a press briefing on 30 November, which is the closest thing the calendar offers to a rehearsal for the December meeting.

A central bank that is willing to signal two meetings ahead is a central bank that does not want to be surprised by its own markets. Japan's ten-year government bond yield was at its highest since 1996 when investingLive last checked in late September,.

## Bonds and the budget

Ueda was asked about emergency bond purchases, the tool the bank could use to cap yields if the bond market were to move sharply. He said such purchases would be decided case by case, and warned that in that event long-term interest rates could become even more volatile. That is the sort of answer a governor gives when he would rather the question had not been asked.

Ueda also addressed fiscal policy. The government plans to cut the consumption tax on food. Ueda said the cut would likely support growth, though how much would depend on whether companies pass the savings on to customers and on household sentiment. It is a measure that lowers prices on paper while the central bank is trying to keep the broader price level from overshooting. The two institutions are therefore pursuing complementary objectives with different tools, which is the best case, and in opposite directions, which is the other one.

## The read

There is a version of this story that is simply good news. Inflation reached target. Wages and prices have moved. The central bank is normalising a policy stance that nobody expected to be normalised in this decade. The only complication is that normalisation is a process with no obvious finishing line. The neutral-rate range of 1.1 percent to 2.5 percent says the destination could be anywhere between where Japan is now and double it.

Economists will note that central banks rarely find the neutral rate by announcing it. They find it by raising rates until something creaks. Governor Ueda has not said what he expects to creak. He has said that, for now, the accelerator should remain pressed, and that the bank will keep an eye on the speedometer. After fourteen years of pushing the car uphill, there is something touching about hearing the driver talk about the brakes.
