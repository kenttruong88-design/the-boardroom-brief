# US GDP Revised Up to 4.3% as Investment Surprises Everyone

The US Bureau of Economic Analysis said on Wednesday that real gross domestic product grew at an annualised rate of 4.3 percent in the third quarter, revised up 0.9 percentage points from the 3.4 percent advance estimate published on October 29. The second quarter had grown 2.2 percent. According to the United States Real Estate Investor, economists polled by Reuters had expected a revision to only 3.5 percent, which means the statisticians exceeded the consensus by 0.8 points. In macroeconomics this is known as a surprise. In government communications it is known as "revisions".

## What changed, according to the people who changed it

The BEA attributed the upgrade to "upward revisions to investment and consumer spending," as quoted by the United States Real Estate Investor. The details are worth reading slowly. Consumer spending grew 4.2 percent, up from 3.8 percent in the advance estimate, in figures attributed to Reuters. Business investment rose 16.1 percent, against 12.7 percent previously, with business equipment up 21.8 percent. Reuters noted this was the third straight quarter of double-digit business investment growth.

StockTitan reported that the investment upgrade was led by intellectual property products, notably software, based mainly on Bureau of Labor Statistics producer price data for system and application software publishing. That is a phrase one should read twice. A material share of a revision that moved headline growth by almost a full percentage point rests on a price index for software. This is not a criticism. It is simply a reminder that when a nation reports its economy to one decimal place, a great deal of the decimal is an estimate of what things cost.

Real final sales to private domestic purchasers, the measure economists prefer when they wish to look past inventories and trade, rose 5.8 percent, up from 4.9 percent, according to StockTitan. The United States Real Estate Investor quoted Reuters describing it as the fastest since the second quarter of 2021.

## The parts that did not get the memo

Housing remains the sector at the back of the class. Residential investment fell 3.4 percent in the quarter, after rising 2.8 percent in the second, according to the same report, which attributed the decline to weaker sales and construction activity. Trade was also a drag, subtracting 3.21 percentage points from growth as imports rose, per StockTitan. Inventories contributed 1.64 points and government 0.97.

The income side of the national accounts told a similar story, with a flourish. Real gross domestic income rose 4.4 percent, against 2.3 percent in the second quarter, and corporate profits from current production rose $206.0 billion at an annual rate, after a $384.0 billion rise in the previous quarter. StockTitan said profits were 21.3 percent above a year earlier, the fastest pace since late 2021. When output and income both come in strong and agree with each other, the statisticians are entitled to a small, private celebration.

## Why the central bank will not be celebrating

The Federal Reserve held its policy range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent on October 28 in a 10 to 2 vote, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack preferring a quarter-point rise, as StockTitan reported. The October statement said economic activity was expanding "at a strong pace." A 4.3 percent quarter is a rather emphatic confirmation.

Price measures in this release were unchanged from the advance estimate: the PCE price index rose at a 2.1 percent annual rate and core PCE at 2.5 percent, per both reports. Those are quarterly annualised figures and they are not the same thing as the year-on-year rates that dominate the Fed's discussions. The earlier September data, reported on October 29 by StockAnalysis, showed PCE inflation of 3.6 percent from a year earlier. A central bank deciding in December will be looking at the year-on-year picture, with a growth rate that provides no argument for patience.

The next FOMC decision is on December 9, and the third GDP estimate follows on December 23. Between those dates, the Fed will read an economy that has just been shown to be larger than it appeared. Ingrid Holt, who spent years watching institutions respond to inconvenient data, notes that this is rarely the moment they feel most reassured.

The closing observation is the old one. The revision did not change the economy of the third quarter. It changed what everyone is allowed to say about it. The economy was always 4.3 percent. We simply needed another month to find the last 0.9.
