# UK Inflation Hits 3.6% as Petrol Does the Heavy Lifting

UK consumer price inflation rose to 3.6 percent in the twelve months to October, from 3.5 percent in September, the Office for National Statistics said on 18 November. It was the fourth consecutive monthly rise, the highest reading since October 2025, and a long way from the 2.6 percent low recorded in June. The figure matched the consensus forecast, which is to say that the surprise, as one mortgage adviser put it, was that there was no surprise.

## A headline with one accomplice

The detail is more interesting than the total. CPIH, which includes housing costs, rose to 3.7 percent from 3.6 percent. Core CPI, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was 2.8 percent and little changed. Services inflation was 3.7 percent, also little changed. Food inflation, meanwhile, fell to 0.9 percent as supermarkets discounted, according to ITV News.

What moved the headline was transport. Motor fuel prices were 33.2 percent higher than a year earlier, and the ONS said fuel was the largest upward contribution to the change in the rate. Grant Fitzner, the ONS chief economist, described fuel as once again a major contributor. Restaurants and hotels were the largest offset. Diesel reached a record high in October, ITV reported.

An economist asked to describe a price-stability problem in which the broad basket is well behaved and one commodity is not would reach for the phrase "supply shock". A central bank asked the same question would reach for a rate rise. Both responses are available to Britain, and the Bank of England has chosen the second.

## The Bank's difficulty

Bank Rate is 4 percent following a hike earlier this month. According to MPA Magazine, financial markets, using LSEG pricing, put the probability of a further 25 basis point increase at the 17 December meeting at about 90 percent, which would take Bank Rate to 4.25 percent. ITV reports that markets expect Bank Rate to reach 4.75 percent by next June. The Bank itself expects inflation to peak at about 5 percent around the middle of next year, ITV and MPA reported, a forecast that sits uncomfortably beside a core rate of 2.8 percent.

The reason is energy. Ofgem's price cap rose 4 percent in October, lifting the typical annual household bill by about £60 to £1,723. A larger rise follows in January: ITV cites forecasts that household energy bills will increase by 26 percent. The Bank has pointed to cost pressures already in the pipeline, which is the language central banks use when the pipeline is the problem.

Raising interest rates does not make oil cheaper. It does, however, make it more expensive to borrow against the wages that are meant to absorb it. Real wage growth has nearly stalled and is likely to turn negative in the coming months, ITV reported. The Office for Budget Responsibility expects the spending power of the typical household to be flat through 2027 and 2028. The central bank is therefore tightening into a period in which households will already be doing the tightening themselves.

Mortgage holders have noticed. Two-year and five-year fixed mortgage rates stood at 5.93 percent and 5.95 percent respectively on 16 November, according to Moneyfacts. Propertymark chief executive Nathan Emerson said another increase in inflation would naturally cause concern for many consumers, which is a considered way to describe a mortgage quote.

## The Treasury's invoice

The fiscal picture, published by the ONS on 20 November, shows how inflation compounds. Public sector borrowing in October was £17.0 billion, £0.8 billion higher than a year earlier and £0.8 billion above the OBR's March forecast of £16.2 billion. Over the first seven months of the financial year, borrowing was £111.3 billion, £7.3 billion lower than a year ago but £3.3 billion above the OBR's forecast.

Debt interest in October was £11.2 billion, up £2.8 billion, or 33.4 percent, on a year earlier. Of that, £4.2 billion came from the uplift on index-linked gilts, whose payments rise with inflation. In other words, the government is a large holder of a liability that is indexed to the very statistic it would like to see fall. Public sector net debt reached £3,058.3 billion, or 95.8 percent of GDP, which is 1.9 percentage points higher than a year ago. The ONS noted that its comparison used the March forecast because October-forecast profiles were not available.

## The read

Britain's inflation story has a clear structure. Fuel and energy account for most of the upside. Food and the core measure are subdued. Wages are not keeping up, the Treasury's interest bill is indexed to prices, and the central bank is preparing a further increase in the cost of money. Each of those is defensible on its own terms. Together they resemble a household that responds to a leaking roof by raising the mortgage.

The next inflation release is due on 16 December, the day before the Bank decides. Officials will have one more number to consider and, if the past four months are a guide, an explanation ready for it.
