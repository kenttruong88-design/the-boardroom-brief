# Australia's New Housing Loans Fall 15.5% in a Quarter as Investors Flee

Australian lenders wrote 15.5 percent fewer new housing loans by value in the third quarter than in the second, according to State Street Global Advisors' weekly economic note for November 16, which draws on the latest national figures. The decline was concentrated among landlords. Investor lending fell 32.2 percent, while owner-occupier lending fell 5.3 percent. It is the sort of divergence in which the people with the most exposure to the housing market are the first to leave it.

## A quarter in which the investors left

A fall of nearly a third in investor lending is not a rounding error. Investors are the marginal buyers of the housing market, the people who borrow against one property to bid on another, and they are acutely sensitive to the cost of credit. Owner-occupiers, who need somewhere to live, are less so. The 5.3 percent decline in their lending is therefore the more worrying signal about affordability, and the 32.2 percent decline in investor lending is the more dramatic signal about appetite.

State Street's note, written by chief economist Simona Mocuta and colleagues, places the figure inside a broader softening. The NAB measure of business confidence stood at minus 15 in October. The Westpac-Melbourne Institute index of consumer sentiment fell 2.7 percent in November to 78.2, a reading that sits well below the 100 mark that separates optimists from pessimists. Businesses and households, in other words, are reaching the same conclusion by different routes.

The central bank has not yet finished. State Street said it retains a forecast of at least one further cash-rate increase in Australia. A tightening cycle that is still under way, applied to a housing market that is already contracting, is the macroeconomic equivalent of continuing to drive after the road has narrowed.

## Not only in Australia

Australia is not an outlier. State Street's note listed a series of data points from the same week that point in one direction. In the United States, existing home sales fell 5.5 percent in October to an annualised 3.75 million, the lowest since August 2010, and the inventory of homes for sale rose to 5.1 months' supply, the highest since November 2015. A market with sales at their lowest since 2010 and inventory at its highest since 2015 is a market with a price problem it has not yet admitted to.

The same note recorded how differently the cycle looks elsewhere. The United Kingdom's economy grew 0.7 percent in the third quarter in a preliminary estimate, with business investment up 0.4 percent. Bank of England Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli said a further rise in rates is likely "in the near term" but described that judgment as "conditional," and said uncertainty is "a reason to proceed gradually." State Street expects the Monetary Policy Committee to hold until a February increase. In Japan, goods producer prices rose 7.1 percent in October from a year earlier, and State Street's economists expected third-quarter GDP to shrink by about 0.1 percent when it is released this week.

The distinction between these economies is worth drawing carefully. Some are growing and tightening, some are shrinking and tightening, and some are being asked to tighten because inflation has become the one indicator that no government can argue with. Housing is the transmission mechanism that links all three, which is why a quarterly figure for Australian mortgage approvals is a more useful read on the global monetary cycle than it looks.

## What governments will say

The standard official response to a figure like this is that housing activity is "normalising." It is a fine word. It means that the thing that was happening has stopped, and that the people who made it happen have left. Governments prefer it to "contracting" because it implies that someone intended the outcome.

The more honest reading is that credit is working as intended. Central banks raise rates to reduce borrowing, and borrowing for investment property is where reduced borrowing shows up first. The question for the Reserve Bank of Australia is not whether the instrument is effective. It is whether it will be effective at a pace that the economy can absorb, and whether the figures due this week, including employment, which State Street expects to show a gain of about 2,000 jobs, confirm that the rest of the economy can absorb it. A gain of 2,000 jobs is not a strong labour market. It is, however, the sort of number that lets everyone say the labour market remains resilient.
