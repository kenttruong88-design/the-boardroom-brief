# ECB Accounts Show Officials Could Have Raised Rates in October, and Will in December

The European Central Bank's Governing Council was ready to raise interest rates at its October meeting, and chose not to. That is the plain reading of the account of the October 28 and 29 meeting, which the bank published on Thursday, November 26. According to Reuters, most policymakers who have spoken since have signalled a quarter-point increase on December 17, a move Reuters called "all but a done deal".

*Photo: Sascha Hormel via Pexels*

The accounts record that, at the October meeting, Philip Lane, the ECB's chief economist, proposed keeping the three key interest rates unchanged. They also record that members noted "they could have supported raising interest rates at the current meeting". Rates were not raised. The document adds that a hike then "would simply frontload the necessary monetary policy tightening", and that "inflation would be higher for longer than previously expected".

## The case for waiting, and its limits

There is, in fairness, a respectable argument for patience. Central banks are rightly cautious about moving on a single data point, and the Governing Council had already raised rates in June and again in September. A pause to observe the effect of two increases is the sort of discipline that textbooks recommend and committees admire.

The difficulty is that the data the Council was looking at did not suggest a pause. Euro-area inflation, as measured by the harmonised index, was 3.8% in September, up from 3.2% in August, according to the ECB's own document. Energy inflation was 18.9%, up from 14.3% a month earlier. Services inflation, the stickier and politically less convenient measure, stood at 3.2%, and core inflation at 2.5%.

Those are not the numbers of an economy in need of a breather. They are the numbers of an economy in which a headline rate has risen by 0.6 percentage points in a month, and in which the central bank's own minutes concede that inflation will stay higher for longer than it had expected. Holding rates under those conditions can be defended. Describing the decision as anything other than a choice to delay is harder.

## Frontloading the frontloading

The phrase in the accounts that deserves a frame is "frontload". A hike in October, the officials wrote, would have front-loaded tightening that is, in their own assessment, necessary. The necessary thing was then deferred to December. Economists will recognise the logic: the policy is appropriate, the timing is a matter of presentation, and the presentation is the Council's to choose.

The practical effect is a central bank that has told markets, through a series of speeches, what it will do on December 17, without having done it on October 29. Reuters reports that wage negotiations remain unfinished and that few officials have offered guidance beyond December. Market pricing, according to the ECB document, implies a deposit-rate peak around 3%. The destination is signposted. The route has been left to the traveller.

This is not an unusual condition for a central bank. Forward guidance exists precisely so that decisions can be anticipated. But it does invite a modest question about what the October meeting was for. If a majority could have supported a hike, and the majority is now supporting one, the intervening month appears to have served mainly to add an entry to the calendar.

## A change of cast

The institutional backdrop adds interest. Reuters reports that the ECB's president, Christine Lagarde, and its executive board member Isabel Schnabel are both due to leave in the coming months. Pablo Hernandez de Cos is the likely successor as president, taking over in early 2027. A central bank about to change its leadership has a particular incentive to leave its policy path unambiguous, and a particular reason not to leave its successors with a half-finished tightening cycle.

It also has a communication problem of a kind that governments recognise well: the need to describe a delay as strategy. The ECB's accounts do this with admirable economy. Nothing in them is false. The Council did not raise rates in October, and it did not claim that inflation was falling. It said, in effect, that it could have, and that it will.

## What to make of it

For businesses, the practical advice is unremarkable. Assume a 25 basis-point increase in December, treat the peak as a range rather than a number, and do not plan on the wage round arriving quietly. For households with variable-rate debt, the month of grace is a month of grace.

For those who watch central banks as an institution, the account offers a small lesson in how rates are decided. The decision is rarely made at the meeting. It is made in the weeks before, in speeches and in leaks, and then ratified, in the manner of a conclusion reached over lunch. The October account tells us what the Council thought. The December meeting will tell us whether it still does, and the market has already assumed the answer.
