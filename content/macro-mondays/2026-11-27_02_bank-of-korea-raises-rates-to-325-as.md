# Bank of Korea Raises Rates to 3.25% as Chip Boom Drives Growth Forecast to 3.9%

The Bank of Korea raised its benchmark repo rate by 25 basis points to 3.25% on Thursday, November 26, on a 5-2 vote of its Monetary Policy Board, according to a report of the decision. The explanation, in short, is that semiconductors are doing too well. It is the central bank's first increase since an October pause, following hikes in July and August.

The cumulative tightening this year is now 75 basis points. Twenty-one of 23 economists surveyed by Bloomberg had expected the move, so the surprise was confined to the two board members who voted against it.

## An economy with one large employee

The reason for the hike is the arithmetic of the chip cycle. The central bank estimates that Korean chipmakers' 2026 operating profit will be just under 700 trillion won, which the report puts at more than 20% of national income. Working-day-adjusted exports in October were up roughly 76% from a year earlier, and cumulative goods exports have passed $1 trillion.

When a single sector accounts for a fifth of national income, macroeconomic management becomes a form of sector management. Governor Shin Hyun Song said, "The semiconductor industry has expanded rapidly in the second half, and we expect that momentum to carry into next year". It is a sentence that works as a forecast and as a confession of dependence.

The bank has revised its growth forecasts upward by margins that would, in other circumstances, be described as heroic. It now expects 3.9% growth in 2026, up from 3.3% in its August projection, and 4.1% in 2027, up from 2.9%. Third-quarter GDP grew 1.0% on the quarter, against a consensus of 0.8%.

## The inflation puzzle

The interesting part is what the hike is not about. October consumer prices rose 2.8% from a year earlier, and core inflation was also 2.8%. These are above target, but they are not alarming, and they are not the figures that would usually drive a central bank to move three times in a year. Shin said inflation would not reach the 2% target until 2028.

A central bank that does not expect to meet its target for two years is, on the face of it, entitled to be patient. This one is not, which suggests that the real risk the board is watching is not the price of noodles. It is the price of everything else. A boom concentrated in one sector tends to spread through wages, property and credit before it shows up in the consumer price index, by which point a central bank is explaining rather than preventing.

The bank's problem is therefore the oldest in macroeconomics: how to tighten policy for an economy that is, by the numbers, extraordinarily healthy, on the grounds that its health is unbalanced. The standard remedy is to raise rates modestly and trust that demand outside the booming sector will cool first. That is a bet that the other 80% of the economy is more interest-rate sensitive than the part that is winning.

## What could go wrong

Bloomberg Economics, according to the report, expects the repo rate to reach 3.50% in February 2027, with the risk that the path changes if investment in artificial intelligence cools. That caveat is not minor. The profit estimates that justify the hike depend on global demand for chips built for AI data centres, and that demand depends on customers who are themselves spending ahead of revenue.

It is worth being exact about the dependency. The central bank is not tightening because it believes the boom will last. It is tightening because the boom is large enough to matter if it does, and costly to unwind if it does not. A policy rate set against a single export cycle is, in effect, a position on that cycle.

The prudent reading is that the Bank of Korea has chosen to lean against a boom it expects to continue, rather than wait to clean up after one it did not. That is textbook behaviour, and it is rare enough in practice to be worth noting. Most central banks, confronted with a cheerful quarter, discover reasons to wait.

The less prudent reading is that a country whose growth forecast has risen by more than a full percentage point in three months has just raised borrowing costs for everyone, on the strength of what its chipmakers say about next year. The governor, to his credit, said so himself. Few of his peers describe their policy dependencies so cleanly, and fewer still do it while the dependency is still paying.
