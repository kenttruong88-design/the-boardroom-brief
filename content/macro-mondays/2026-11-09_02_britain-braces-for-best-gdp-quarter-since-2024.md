# Britain Braces for Its Best GDP Quarter Since 2024, Forecasters Say

The UK's third-quarter GDP estimate, due on Thursday, is forecast to show growth of 0.7% on the previous quarter, which would be the strongest since the first quarter of 2024, according to Trading Economics. The second quarter grew 0.5%, per Yardeni Research. For an economy that has spent much of the past decade being described with adjectives such as "sluggish", a 0.7% reading is something of an event.

## The good number and the other number

The quarterly forecast is the encouraging one. The Trading Economics calendar also shows a consensus for annual third-quarter growth of 1.4%, down from 1.7%, and a forecast of flat monthly GDP for September. A strong quarter followed by a stalled final month is not a contradiction. It is the standard arithmetic of an economy that moved early and then stopped, and statisticians describe it in tones usually reserved for weather.

Strong quarterly growth with a flat final month implies the strength arrived early in the quarter, which is this column's inference rather than a forecast anyone has published. The practical reading is that Thursday's headline will probably be good, and that the detail underneath it will be subject to debate. That is the best outcome available to a Treasury that has to describe growth to an electorate that does not feel it. Finance ministries tend to describe a good quarter as the beginning of a trend and a bad one as noise. The same ministry will have both speeches drafted.

## Europe, in smaller type

On Friday the euro area's updated third-quarter GDP estimate follows the initial flash reading of 0.4% quarterly growth. Yardeni records annual growth of 1.3% for the bloc, with Spain growing 2.7% and France contracting 0.1%. That distribution is its own story. The euro area is, in aggregate, growing; the largest contributors to the aggregate are in no way growing at the same rate.

A growth figure arriving in the same week as a central bank's next inflation reading is the kind of coincidence that central bankers describe as a delicate balance. Households call it the weekly shop.

## Tokyo holds, for now

In Japan, the Bank of Japan held its policy rate at 1.25% in October, a 31-year high, after raising rates in September, according to Trading Economics. A summary of opinions from that meeting is due this week. A former BOJ chief economist expects a December hike and further lifts toward 2% next year, investingLive reported on Monday.

Rate-setters in Tokyo have a gift for communicating in subjunctive moods, and the summary of opinions is a document devoted to them. It is the one release this week in which the absence of a number is the point.

## The American backdrop

The foreign data arrives against a United States that is, on the available evidence, running warm. The Atlanta Fed's GDPNow model, cited by Trading Economics, puts fourth-quarter growth at 3.4%. Initial jobless claims were 189,000 last week, and Yardeni notes that the four-week average, 194,750, is the lowest since 1969. Those are the sorts of numbers that make a Fed hike in December look like maintenance rather than a decision, and they complicate the lives of every other central bank, whose currencies must now be held against a dollar with a persuasive economy behind it.

## A note from Beijing

One other data point deserves a paragraph. China's producer prices recorded their biggest rise since 2022, according to the investingLive summary on Monday, and Reuters reported that Beijing is suspending its 24% US tariff for two more months while keeping a 10% levy. Rising factory-gate prices in the world's largest manufacturer are the kind of number that reaches other countries' consumer price indices on a delay, and other countries' finance ministries are rarely thrilled to learn of it.

## What the week tests

The three economies in this week's calendar present three versions of the same dilemma. Britain is growing faster and may be about to slow. The euro area is growing slowly and is dealing with faster prices. Japan is raising rates from a floor that took decades to leave. In each, a government statistician will publish a figure, a minister will welcome it, and an economist will point out the number beneath it.

The reliable constant is the language. When growth is strong, it is described as "resilient". When it is weak, it is described as "challenging". When it is both at once, it is described as "mixed", which is a word that has never once appeared in a press release as an apology.
