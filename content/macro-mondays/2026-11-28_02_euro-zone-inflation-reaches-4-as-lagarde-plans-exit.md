# Euro Zone Inflation Reaches 4% as Lagarde Plans Exit

Inflation in the euro area reached 4% in October, up from 3.8% in September and a three-year high, according to Eurostat figures reported by Trading and Investment News on 18 November. The European Central Bank meets on 17 December, with its president, Christine Lagarde, due to leave office on 7 February 2027.

The timing has a certain administrative elegance. A central bank facing its highest inflation in three years has chosen this moment to inform the public that its leader is departing. Ms Lagarde said on 29 October, as quoted by Euronews, that she would leave "a sense of good work done" behind her. Economists will judge that sentiment on the evidence of the next two quarters.

## Where the 4% comes from

The breakdown makes plain that energy is the culprit. Energy prices were 21% higher than a year earlier in October, up from 19% in September. Services inflation rose by a tenth of a point to 3.3%. Fresh food accelerated to 4.6% from 4%, while non-energy industrial goods rose 1.2%, up from 1.1%. Inflation across the wider European Union was also 4%.

The spread between countries is wide. Romania recorded the highest rate at 7%, followed by Lithuania at 6.1% and Bulgaria at 5.9%. Sweden, at 0.6%, was lowest. A single monetary policy for the euro area covers countries whose inflation rates differ by a factor that would make most forecasters reach for a different word than "average".

Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, is a good deal more reassuring. Eurostat's flash estimate published on 4 November put it at 2.5%, unchanged and in line with forecasts, according to Reuters reporting cited by the same publication. The distance between 4% and 2.5% is the distance between an energy shock and a structural problem. It is the reason central bankers are not yet in open panic, and the reason they are not entirely relaxed either.

In a currency union, that arithmetic is rarely tidy: the average conceals everyone, and the committee must set one rate for a mean that no household actually experiences. Officials tend to describe this as a feature. Households tend to describe it as the price of groceries in their own country.

## A bank that has already moved

The ECB has not been idle. According to the same source, it raised its deposit rate on 11 June 2026 to 2.25% from 2.00%, and again on 10 September to 2.50%. At its 29 October meeting it held at 2.50%, with the main refinancing rate at 2.65%, and said it was not pre-committing to a particular rate path.

That phrase, familiar to anyone who has read central bank statements, is a promise not to make promises. It has the advantage of being almost always true.

Reuters, cited in the report, said a December move was likely, and that markets were pricing two to three further ECB hikes over the coming year, with expectations moving alongside oil prices. The ECB itself expects inflation to remain above 4% for much of the first half of 2027 before falling toward what it calls the "low twos".

The European Commission's autumn forecast, published on 17 November, was gentler on the full-year numbers, projecting euro area inflation averaging 3.2% in 2026 and 3.1% in 2027. An average of 3.1% is a comforting number. It is also a number that can contain a first half of 4% and a second half that is doing considerable work.

## The oil variable

Every central bank forecast of this kind rests on an assumption about energy, and energy has been behaving unpredictably. Oxford Economics noted in its US commentary that recent oil price declines support its view that headline inflation peaks in the fourth quarter. If the same logic holds in Europe, the 4% reading may prove to be near the top. If it does not, the ECB faces a harder choice: tightening into an economy that has little room for it.

The cost of that choice falls unevenly. Households in Romania and Lithuania are experiencing a very different inflation to those in Sweden, and the ECB's decision in December will affect all of them identically.

Ms Lagarde has, in other words, scheduled her departure for a point at which the major question is no longer whether the ECB can bring inflation down but how long it can afford to take. Her successor inherits a deposit rate of 2.5% or somewhat more, a 4% headline and a forecast that says patience. It is difficult to imagine a more representative handover.
