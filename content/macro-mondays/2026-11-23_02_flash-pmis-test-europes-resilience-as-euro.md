# Flash PMIs Test Europe's Resilience as Euro Area Inflation Sits at 4.0%

Business surveys for November are published on Monday, and economists expect them to show an economy that is slowing without yet breaking. According to a preview by the forex broker Orbex dated November 20, the consensus forecast for the euro-area composite PMI is 51.1, down from 51.4 in October, while France is expected to slip further into contraction at 46.0, from 46.6.

## What the forecasts say

A reading above 50 signals expansion and below 50 contraction, which makes the euro area's 51.1 a number that can be described as either growth or its absence, depending on which side of the lectern one is standing. Germany's manufacturing PMI is forecast at 53.8, against 54.2 in October, according to Orbex, so industry is still expanding, if slightly more slowly. France, by contrast, is where the picture is bleakest: a composite forecast of 46.0 would mark a continued and sizeable decline.

Britain is expected to show a similar softening. Orbex lists the UK services PMI forecast at 52.5, down from 52.8, and the manufacturing PMI at 52.5, also down from 52.8. In the United States, the S&P Global composite is forecast at 55.6, from 55.9, a figure that remains comfortably in expansion territory. A newswire headline on the October final reading records that it came in at 55.9 against a forecast of 57.6, and from 58.4 in September, so the American direction has been consistent, if not the level.

The pattern across all four economies is one of deceleration from a high base, which is the least dramatic form of bad news. It is also the form most likely to be described by officials as "resilience."

## Inflation is the other half

The trouble is that slower growth has not yet purchased lower prices. A weekly FX calendar published on Substack by FX Detective Forward on November 22 puts euro-area inflation at 4.0% for October, citing Eurostat's release on November 18. That is double the European Central Bank's 2% target. Orbex puts the policy implication bluntly, writing that most major central banks are "almost certain to raise rates in a couple of weeks."

That sentence is the broker's view, not an official statement, but it matches the market pricing elsewhere. The Federal Reserve meets on December 8 and 9, according to the FX Detective Forward calendar, and Kiplinger reports that the odds of a quarter-point hike are above 97%. The Bank of Japan meets on December 17 and 18. The point of the exercise is that growth is cooling in the places where prices are not.

The ECB will offer some evidence of its own thinking on Thursday at 13:30 CET, when it publishes the account of its October 28 and 29 meeting. Accounts are written in the careful language of committees, and economists read them with the attention that others reserve for the instructions on medicine bottles. The ECB's consumer expectations survey for October follows on Friday, and Vujčić of the ECB speaks in Vienna on Tuesday at noon. Japan's flash PMI arrives early on Tuesday, at 00:30 UTC, which will give Asia its own chance to confirm or contradict the European story.

## The currency question

Orbex argues that currency moves are shifting from the traditional contest between interest rates to a contest over growth, with higher US inflation weighing on the dollar if the hike odds for next year do not also rise. On the figures in the FX Detective Forward post, the euro was fixed by the ECB at 1.0990 against the dollar on Friday, from 1.1014 on Monday, while the dollar bought 158.64 yen at Friday's fixing, from 159.97 on Thursday.

None of that amounts to a dramatic currency story, which is probably the point. If the flash PMIs surprise on the downside in Europe while the US numbers hold, the dollar's advantage will survive another week. If they surprise upward in Europe, the ECB's job will look harder, since a firm economy with 4.0% inflation does not need to be encouraged.

The sensible expectation is therefore the dull one. Europe will report slow growth and high prices, the central banks will say they are watching both, and the account of the ECB's October meeting will explain that the committee considered all the relevant factors. In macroeconomics, the absence of surprise is not nothing; it is simply what a surprise looks like when it has been given enough time.
