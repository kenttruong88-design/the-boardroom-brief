# Japan's Core Inflation Hits 2.2%, Missing Forecasts as Subsidies Fade

Japan's core consumer price index, which excludes fresh food, rose 2.2% in October from a year earlier, up from 2.1% in September, according to figures released on Friday, November 20, and reported by Reuters. Economists had expected 2.3%. It is the second consecutive month above the Bank of Japan's 2% target, and it is a number that tells a tidier story than the data underneath it.

## The acceleration is a subsidy being switched off

The headline moved up for a reason that has little to do with households suddenly feeling richer. Energy prices rose 2.2% from a year earlier, against a fall of 0.6% in September, as the government scaled back its energy subsidies, per Reuters and ActionForex, which cites the Statistics Bureau. City gas rose 5.3%, electricity rose 1.6%, and gasoline fell 2.3%. ActionForex estimates that energy added about 0.2 percentage points to the core reading.

Strip the energy out, and the picture changes. The core-core measure, which excludes both fresh food and energy, slowed to 2.2% from 2.4%. Food excluding fresh items slowed to 2.2% from 2.9%. Goods prices rose 3.2%, up from 2.8%, but services, the component central bankers watch for evidence of wages feeding through to prices, rose only 1.4%, down from 1.5%.

In plain terms, the number that rose did so because the state removed a discount, and the number that tells you about durable inflation fell. A government that spends public money to hold down energy bills and then withdraws it is performing a small experiment in measuring its own influence. The result, as ever, is that prices were lower when someone was paying for them to be lower.

## A central bank with a calendar problem

None of this changes the Bank of Japan's plan. The policy rate stands at 1.25% after hikes in June and September, and the next meeting ends on December 18, where a move to 1.5% is widely expected. A majority of economists polled by Reuters expects a December hike and another by the end of March 2027.

The complication is that the economy is not cooperating. Reuters noted that third-quarter GDP unexpectedly contracted on weak consumption. A central bank tightening into a shrinking economy, on the strength of an inflation figure that is partly administrative, is not unprecedented, but it does require a certain composure.

Economists are looking past the October print. Takeshi Minami of Norinchukin Research Institute told Reuters he expects consumer inflation to accelerate sharply at the start of next year, and Reuters reported his view that inflation could pass 3% by March 2027. That is one economist's forecast, and this column treats it as such. Economy Minister Minoru Kiuchi, for his part, said wages and prices are rising stably in tandem, which is the kind of sentence a ministry produces when it would like the data to be a little more cooperative.

The Fed's own position is worth setting beside Tokyo's. Its policymakers held their target range at 3.75% to 4% at the October meeting, with two members dissenting in favor of a hike, according to the minutes published on November 18. A central bank that is already debating a larger move, set against one still raising rates from 1.25%, is a reminder that "tightening" means very different things in different capitals.

## Then there is Washington

Japan's decision does not happen in isolation. Yoshiki Shinke of Dai-ichi Life Research told Reuters that if the Federal Reserve raises rates in December, the Bank of Japan "will face a tough choice." The Fed is weighing exactly that, and a wider gap between American and Japanese rates tends to weaken the yen, which would feed back into import prices and, eventually, the very inflation the bank is trying to read.

So the sequence runs as follows. Subsidies end, headline inflation edges up, underlying inflation eases, the economy contracts, the Fed may tighten, and the yen may weaken. Each link is plausible, and each points in a different direction for a central bank that has to publish one number.

It would be unfair to say Tokyo has no good options. It has several, all of which require explaining to a public why a 2.2% number that missed forecasts is also a reason to raise rates. The government's skill at that kind of explanation will be tested before the year is out. The data, meanwhile, remain what they usually are: accurate, and open to more than one reading.
