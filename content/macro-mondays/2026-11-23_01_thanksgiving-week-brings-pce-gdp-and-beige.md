# Thanksgiving Week Brings PCE, GDP and Beige Book as Hike Looms

America's economic calendar is shortened this week by Thanksgiving, and the statisticians have responded by scheduling almost everything for Wednesday. According to Kiplinger's weekly calendar, updated on November 20, the Bureau of Economic Analysis will publish the October PCE price index, core PCE and the second estimate of third-quarter GDP at 8:30 a.m. Eastern on November 25, alongside durable goods and weekly jobless claims.

The GDP release is the quieter story. The second estimate of third-quarter growth revises the first using more complete source data, and revisions are usually modest. They are also the place where a rosy first reading goes to be quietly corrected, and the update typically carries a note that it reflects more complete information. The more interesting question is the composition of growth: if consumption is carrying the quarter while inflation accelerates, the real strength is smaller than the headline suggests, and the Fed will know it.

## The inflation number that matters

PCE is the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation, which is why a number published the day before a holiday will have an outsized claim on the attention of people who would prefer to be elsewhere. The September reading, per Kiplinger, showed headline PCE up 0.5% on the month and 3.6% from a year earlier, with core PCE up 0.3% on the month and 3.1% annually.

Forecasts for October are no cheerier. The Cleveland Fed's nowcast, as of November 19, pointed to headline PCE rising 0.3% on the month and 3.8% on the year, with core up 0.3% on the month and 3.2% on the year. If that is right, both measures will have accelerated on an annual basis, and the Fed will have received its latest piece of evidence that the problem it is trying to solve has not noticed the solution.

A nowcast is a model, not a release, and it deserves the caution that models deserve. But the direction matches other data. Kevin Nam of BCA Research was quoted by Kiplinger noting that the control group in October's retail sales, which feeds into the GDP calculation, rose 0.6% against a consensus forecast of 0.3%, after 0.1% in September. A consumer who is spending more than expected while prices rise faster than expected is not an obvious candidate for a rate cut.

## A decision already priced

The Fed's next meeting is on December 8 and 9, per the FX Detective Forward calendar. According to Kiplinger, CME FedWatch puts the odds of a quarter-point increase above 97%. That figure leaves little room for the economic data to surprise in the direction of mercy. What the data can do is shift the debate from whether the Fed raises rates in December to how many times it does so afterwards.

The week's other releases supply the supporting cast. The Conference Board's consumer confidence index for November is due Tuesday, together with the S&P Cotality Case-Shiller home price index for September. New home sales for October follow on Wednesday at 10 a.m., and the Fed's Beige Book, its anecdotal survey of regional business conditions, is published at 2 p.m. that day. John Williams of the New York Fed speaks at 4 p.m. on Monday, which gives the market a Fed voice before the data rather than after it.

The Beige Book deserves more attention than it normally receives. Statistics describe what happened in the past quarter, whereas the Beige Book collects what business contacts say is happening now, in the unpunctuated language of people who have to meet payroll. If the report mentions weakening demand alongside rising costs, that would be the stagflationary combination the Fed least wants to read about in December.

## The holiday as a policy variable

Markets are closed on Thursday and the trade balance and inventory data arrive on Friday, per Kiplinger, a day on which few people will be paying attention. The compressed week creates an odd incentive. Anyone reacting to Wednesday's data will do so with thin liquidity and a long weekend looming, which is not the setting in which markets are at their most reasoned.

Governments describe bad inflation news with considerable creativity, usually by emphasising that it is "broadly in line" with expectations that were revised upward last month. The October PCE release gives the Bureau of Economic Analysis a fresh chance to find the right adjective. The advice, for those with a table to attend, is to read the core figure first and the press release second.
