# UK Jobless Rate Falls to 4.6% as Vacancies Hit Post-2014 Low

The UK unemployment rate fell to 4.6% in the three months to September, the Office for National Statistics said on Tuesday, down 0.3 percentage points on both the quarter and the year. Economists had expected 4.7%, according to the ForexFundamentals calendar, which also recorded that the figure was the lowest of the last twelve releases. In the same publication, the ONS reported that vacancies had fallen to 690,000 in the three months to October, a level not seen outside the pandemic since mid-2014.

The headline numbers are, on their face, cheerful. The employment rate rose to 75.4%, up 0.4 percentage points on the year, the inactivity rate edged down to 20.8%, and the number of unemployed people stood at 1.684 million. A government wishing to describe these figures would reach for words such as "resilient". This column prefers to read the rest of the release.

## Fewer people looking, fewer jobs to find

Vacancies in August to October were down 22,000 on the previous three months and 37,000, or 5.0%, on the year. The vacancy rate was 2.1%, and there were 2.4 unemployed people for every vacancy, down from 2.5. Vacancies fell in 11 of 18 industry sectors, with the largest decline among businesses with one to nine employees, where they dropped by 22,000 to 73,000. The ONS noted that smaller firms cite labour costs as a reason.

Payrolls tell a similar story. The number of payrolled employees fell by 67,000, or 0.2%, in the year to September, and the ONS's provisional flash estimate for October put the total at 30.2 million, down 34,000 on September and 95,000 on the year. The claimant count, meanwhile, rose to 1.714 million in October, up on both the month and the year.

A jobless rate that falls while payrolls shrink and vacancies thin is not a contradiction in statistics. It is, rather, what happens when the denominator of the survey and the numerator of the payroll disagree about who exists. The ONS itself cautioned that the rotation of the Labour Force Survey sample partly explains the quarterly movements, and added that an operational error in May 2026 had "minimal impact" on the headline estimates. The second sentence is reassuring in inverse proportion to one's curiosity about the error.

## Pay, and the committee watching it

Regular pay growth slowed to 3.5% from 3.7%, and total pay growth to 3.6% from 3.8%. The ONS attributed the slowdown in regular pay to public sector wages: regular pay growth was 5.4% in the public sector and 3.1% in the private sector. In its own words, "Regular wage growth slowed, driven by a fall in public sector wage growth."

This matters because of what the Bank of England told the public on 5 November. The Monetary Policy Committee raised Bank Rate by a quarter point to 4%, by 8 votes to 1, with Catherine Mann preferring a half-point rise. The Bank's summary said that policy "is not on a pre-set path" and that the Committee would monitor "underlying pay, pricing behaviour, and slack". It had CPI inflation at 3.5% in September, with staff projecting around 5% by the middle of next year as energy costs feed through.

Of those three indicators, the jobs data speak directly to two. Pay is cooling, which is the good news, and it is cooling mostly in the public sector, which is the awkward news, because private-sector pay at 3.1% was never the problem. Slack is increasing in the sense that vacancies are falling, yet the unemployment rate is falling too, which is not what slack is supposed to do.

The minutes record how the committee itself reads the matter. Andrew Bailey wrote that "without news which softens the risks to inflationary pressures, I can see the argument for a further increase in rates." Huw Pill said that "of itself, a single 25-basis point hike is unlikely to be sufficient." Alan Taylor, meanwhile, said that "this is not the start of a hiking cycle." The committee thus holds the view that rates may rise further and that a cycle is not beginning, which is a coherent position only if one is a committee.

The ONS publishes October consumer price inflation on Wednesday and the next labour market release on 15 December. Britain's labour market is now tight enough to justify a rate rise and loose enough to make one unnecessary, depending on which table one opens first. The Monetary Policy Committee, which can open only one at a time, has kindly confirmed that it is not on a pre-set path.
