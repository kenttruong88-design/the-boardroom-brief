# UK Inflation Hits 3.6%, a Tenth Below the Bank's Forecast

UK consumer price inflation rose to 3.6% in October from 3.5% in September, the Office for National Statistics said on Wednesday, the highest rate in a year. The figure matched the median forecast in a Reuters poll but undershot the Bank of England's own projection of 3.7%, according to Reuters and RTE reporting of the release.

It is the fourth consecutive monthly rise since inflation reached a low of 2.6% in June. Core inflation, which strips out volatile items, held at 2.8%. Services inflation, the measure the Bank watches most anxiously, was unchanged at 3.7%, below the 3.8% economists had expected. The CPIH measure, which includes housing costs, rose to 3.7% from 3.6%.

## Fuel does the heavy lifting

The pressure came mainly from the forecourt. Motor fuel prices were 33.2% higher than a year earlier, compared with 29.8% in September, according to the reports. Petrol averaged 174.7 pence a litre, the highest since August 2022, and diesel reached a record 199.5 pence. Transport inflation rose to 7.5% from 6.6%.

Elsewhere, alcohol and tobacco inflation climbed to 4.5% from 2.5%, reflecting tobacco duty increases that arrived earlier than they did last year. Education costs rose 5.0%, up from 3.9%. Restaurants and hotels fell 0.4% on the month, which, in the circumstances, counts as relief.

## A forecast that is a warning

The Bank's own numbers explain why a slightly lower reading is not the same as good news. In its November Monetary Policy Report, published on November 5, the Bank projected CPI inflation averaging 4.9% in the second quarter of 2027. The Monetary Policy Committee had just voted 8-1 to raise Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4%, with one member preferring a rise of 50 basis points. The Bank said the degree of inflationary pressure in the pipeline was sufficient to warrant a rise.

The pipeline is mostly energy. The report put the Ofgem price cap at £1,723 for the fourth quarter of 2026 and expected it to reach about £2,150 in the first quarter of 2027, with direct energy effects adding about 1.4 percentage points to CPI. Wednesday's number is a snapshot of a process the Bank expects to continue for several more quarters.

There is some good news in the composition. The Bank's report put private-sector regular pay growth at 3% in August and said it should stay around that level through 2026. Wages are not, at present, chasing prices. That is a mixed blessing for households, who are not being compensated for the rise in the cost of living, but a comfort for a committee that fears a wage-price spiral.

## What the markets concluded

The reaction was muted. According to Reuters, the probability the market attached to a December rate rise fell to about 80% from 90%, though one outlet cited by Startup Fortune put the odds nearer 50%. Sterling dipped slightly and gilt futures rose. Markets still price three to four quarter-point increases by the end of 2027. The next MPC decision is due on December 17, with November's inflation figures published the day before.

David Rees of Schroders said that the slightly softer headline made a December hike less likely but did little to change the broader outlook. The remark is a fair summary of the entire release. The headline was a notch better than forecast. The trajectory is the same one it was last week.

The Chief Secretary to the Treasury, Emma Reynolds, pointed to the government's budget, saying it had started the long-term action needed to get energy costs down. The government's explanations for inflation tend to arrive in the future tense, while the petrol prices arrive in the present. At 199.5 pence a litre for diesel, the present has the stronger argument.

## The honest reading

A tenth of a percentage point below the central bank's forecast is a small mercy, and economists will argue about whether it is noise. What matters is the direction. Prices are rising faster than at any point this year, the Bank's own projection is that they will rise faster still, and policymakers are raising rates to meet it.

Ministers may prefer a more soothing adjective for the data. The more accurate one is "unfinished".
